Approaches to reproducing the stylized facts of equity markets can be divided
into bottom-up agent-based models, which derive return dynamics from
simulated interactions of heterogeneous agents
\cite{luxMarchesi1999, contBouchaud2000, farmerJoshi2002, lebaron2006,
arthur2021, farmer2025}, and top-down data-driven models, which fit
statistical structures directly to observed growth rate series.
Agent-based generators produce stylized facts as emergent properties
but are demanding to calibrate to specific assets, and computationally expensive to simulate at scale. 
On the other hand, top-down generators for asset prices go back at
least to Bachelier's diffusion model \cite{bachelier1900}; Osborne
\cite{osborne1959} and Samuelson \cite{samuelson1965} later
formalized geometric Brownian motion, which Black-Scholes-Merton
\cite{blackScholes1973} used as the price process for option
pricing. These diffusion baselines are tractable but fail on the
empirical regularities that drive risk and pricing. Mandelbrot
\cite{mandelbrot1963} showed that returns carry heavier tails
than the Gaussian admits; Fama \cite{famaEMH1970} reviewed the
evidence that raw returns have near-zero linear autocorrelation; and
Schwert \cite{schwert1989} documented persistence in monthly absolute
residuals, an early form of what Cont \cite{cont2001} later codified
as the volatility-clustering stylized fact. The autoregressive
conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) and generalized ARCH (GARCH)
models of Engle \cite{engle1982} and Bollerslev
\cite{bollerslev1986} reproduced the clustering but left marginals
light-tailed unless the noise term was itself heavy-tailed. They also offer no
mechanism for discrete regimes or sudden jumps. Two later
parametric families partially addressed these gaps.
Stochastic-volatility models \cite{steinStein1991, heston1993}
make volatility a latent process to capture clustering, but
estimating that process requires filtering or Markov chain Monte
Carlo (MCMC) methods \cite{kimShephardChib1998}.
Jump-diffusion models \cite{merton1976, kou2002} inject Poisson
events into the price path, producing heavy tails by construction
but offering no mechanism for the volatility persistence that
follows a jump.

Hidden Markov models address this gap by embedding regime and jump
behavior in a latent discrete chain. Hamilton \cite{hamilton1989}
introduced regime-switching to economics in a two-state form, and
later work applied it to volatility forecasting \cite{rossiGallo2006}
and equity trading \cite{nguyen2018}. Ryden et al.\
\cite{rydenTerasvirtaAsbrink1998} found that Gaussian-emission HMMs
matched heavy tails and the absence of return autocorrelation but
failed on volatility clustering; Bulla and Bulla \cite{bullaBulla2006}
recovered the clustering with hidden semi-Markov models (HSMMs)
that replaced the geometric dwell-time distribution of every state
with a parametric (negative-binomial) alternative. Both rely on
the Baum-Welch expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm
\cite{baumPetrieSoulesWeiss1970}, which is iterative and sensitive
to initialization. Deep generative alternatives learn the marginal
directly but routinely miss the volatility-clustering signature:
Takahashi et al.\ \cite{takahashiEtAl2019} found that standard
generative adversarial network (GAN) architectures
produced return sequences that visually resembled financial data yet
failed to reproduce the absolute-return autocorrelation function, and
Kwon and Lee \cite{kwonLee2024} confirmed the same on TimeGAN
\cite{yoonEtAl2019timegan}. Recent surveys
\cite{assefaEtAl2020, jordonEtAl2022, stengerEtAl2024} agree that
stylized-facts reproduction remains the primary quality criterion,
and a pretrained foundation-inference alternative for Markov jump
processes \cite{berghausEtAl2025} has not yet been evaluated on
financial data. The current study picks the latent-state route but follows a different design
principle than the HSMM. Rather than replace the dwell-time
distribution of every state, it leaves the empirically counted
transition matrix in place across the central states and adds an
event-driven override that, on a sparse subset of time slices,
forces the chain into the tail states for a Poisson-distributed
duration. The result is a sparse intervention: tail states receive a
Poisson-distributed dwell time, while the body of the chain keeps
the geometric (memoryless) dwell times of the empirical transition
matrix.

Multi-asset composition adds cross-asset dependence on top of a
univariate generator. Several families address this: mixture
hidden Markov models \cite{diasVermuntRamos2010} share latent
state across assets at high estimation cost in high dimensions;
copula approaches \cite{embrechtsMcNeilStraumann2002,
cherubiniLucianoVecchiato2004, aasCzadoFrigessiBakken2009}
separate marginals from cross-asset dependence at the cost of copula-family
selection and, in their standard static form, treat successive
time slices as independent. Alternatively, factor models reduce
the dependence of a firm's growth rate to one or more shared
factors \cite{rossAPT1976, famaFrench1993}. The single-index model (SIM) of Sharpe
\cite{sharpe1963} is the simplest factor model, decomposing each
asset's growth rate into a market-loaded term plus an idiosyncratic
residual. The single-index model's linear structure also makes it
computationally efficient and easy to interpret, and its
single-factor form pairs with the SPY-based generator developed in
Methods. 
Within SIM, the choice of residual specification trades off the
per-asset generator's distributional structure against the
composed variance. Methods describes a closed-form variance
correction that recovers both.

Validation of synthetic growth-rate paths combines two families of tests:
goodness-of-fit tests for distributional fidelity \cite{kolmogorov1933,
smirnov1948, andersonDarling1952} and autocorrelation diagnostics for
temporal structure \cite{cont2001}. Distributional and temporal tests are necessary but not
sufficient: a synthetic dataset can pass them yet still mislead
when fed into the task it was generated for. Booth and Fama \cite{boothFama1992} described one such failure: a
daily-rebalanced allocator on i.i.d.-residual paths extracts a
structural \emph{diversification return} that is largely absent in
real markets, where residual serial dependence at short horizons is
small but non-negligible \cite{cont2001}. We adopt the classical
distributional metrics together with a Value-at-Risk backtest
scored by Kupiec's unconditional-coverage test, and we address the
diversification-return artifact through a location-shift bias
correction described in Methods.
