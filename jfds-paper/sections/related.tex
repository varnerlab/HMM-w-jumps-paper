Synthesizing equity returns that match real markets has been an active
problem since the recognition that the Black-Scholes-Merton geometric
Brownian motion baseline \cite{blackScholes1973} fails on every
empirical regularity that matters for risk and pricing. Mandelbrot
\cite{mandelbrot1963} demonstrated that speculative price changes
exhibit heavy tails (\emph{leptokurtosis}) far beyond Gaussian
predictions; Fama \cite{famaEMH1970} reviewed evidence that raw
returns display negligible linear autocorrelation; Schwert
\cite{schwert1989} and others later documented persistent
\emph{volatility clustering}, in which the autocorrelation of
absolute returns decays slowly. Engle \cite{engle1982} and Bollerslev
\cite{bollerslev1986} introduced ARCH and GARCH conditional-variance
models that addressed volatility clustering but produced light-tailed
marginals unless paired with a fat-tailed innovation distribution, and
represented neither discrete regimes nor sudden jumps
\cite{dieboldInoue2001}. Stochastic-volatility extensions
\cite{steinStein1991, heston1993} treat volatility as a latent
continuous process at the cost of computationally intensive
estimation. Jump-diffusion models \cite{merton1976, kou2002} inject
Poisson-driven discrete events directly into the price process,
producing heavy tails by construction; they fit equity markets, where
macroeconomic announcements, earnings surprises, and geopolitical
events generate large discrete moves \cite{auYeungEtAl2020}, but in
standard form do not provide a mechanism for post-jump volatility
persistence.

Hidden Markov models offer a different solution by embedding regime
and jump dynamics in a latent discrete-state process.
Hamilton \cite{hamilton1989} introduced the regime-switching HMM to
economics in a two-state expansion-recession formulation, and
subsequent work applied the framework to volatility forecasting
\cite{rossiGallo2006} and equity trading
\cite{nguyen2018, kimNelson1999}. Ryd\'{e}n, Ter\"{a}svirta, and
\AA{}sbrink \cite{rydenTerasvirtaAsbrink1998} evaluated HMMs against
the stylized facts of daily returns and found that Gaussian-emission
HMMs with few states reproduced heavy tails and the absence of return
autocorrelation but failed to capture volatility clustering, with the
absolute-return autocorrelation decaying far too quickly relative to
empirical data. Bulla and Bulla \cite{bullaBulla2006} showed that
hidden semi-Markov models substantially improved
volatility-clustering reproduction by allowing realistic persistence
in high-volatility states. These approaches typically rely on the
Baum-Welch expectation-maximization algorithm
\cite{baumPetrieSoulesWeiss1970} for parameter estimation, which
requires iterative forward-backward passes and is sensitive to
initialization. The hybrid HMM of the present paper sidesteps both
limitations: it discretizes returns into quantile-defined regimes via
a Laplace cumulative distribution function, which enables direct
frequentist counting of transitions without EM, and augments regime
switching with a Poisson jump-duration mechanism that forces the chain
to dwell in high-volatility tail states for empirically realistic
durations.

Deep generative models have been explored as an alternative to
parametric and latent-state approaches. Generative adversarial networks
and their financial variants \cite{takahashiEtAl2019, yoonEtAl2019timegan,
kwonLee2024} can learn complex distributions directly from data, but
systematic evaluations have found that naive architectures fail to
reproduce volatility clustering even when they match the marginal
distribution \cite{takahashiEtAl2019}. Foundation inference models for
Markov jump processes \cite{berghausEtAl2025} offer a pretrained
alternative not yet evaluated on financial data. Surveys of synthetic
data quality more broadly \cite{assefaEtAl2020, jordonEtAl2022,
stengerEtAl2024} agree on the same concern: stylized-facts reproduction
is the primary quality criterion, and naive generators tend to fail on
at least one core stylized fact. The present paper follows the latent-state tradition
rather than the deep-generative one, trading architectural complexity
for a direct-counting fit augmented by a parameter-light jump override.

The single-index model (SIM) of Sharpe \cite{sharpe1963} is the
standard recipe for adding multi-asset factor structure to an
otherwise univariate generator: each asset's growth rate decomposes
into a market-loaded term plus an idiosyncratic residual, reducing the
parameter count relative to a full multivariate specification. The
construction generalizes to multi-factor variants
\cite{rossAPT1976, famaFrench1993} without changing its essential
form. Practitioners typically choose between two SIM-residual
specifications: the \emph{full-return} specification fits a per-asset
generator to the asset's full growth-rate history and plugs the draw
into the SIM as the residual, while the \emph{residual-fit}
specification fits the per-asset generator to the ordinary least
squares residual series and composes with no further correction. The
full-return path is operationally attractive because it lets a
per-asset generator calibrated for distributional interpretability,
cached for other purposes, or chosen to retain regime-switching or
jump structure enter the factor pipeline unchanged; however, it is
well known to inflate the marginal variance of the composed series
quadratically in the factor loading, while the residual-fit
specification gives up the per-asset generator's distributional
richness. Mixture hidden Markov models \cite{diasVermuntRamos2010}
address some of this by sharing latent state across assets, but joint
estimation in high dimensions is computationally demanding.
Copula-based dependence models \cite{embrechtsMcNeilStraumann2002,
cherubiniLucianoVecchiato2004, aasCzadoFrigessiBakken2009} separate
marginal distributions from the dependence structure but introduce
model-selection complexity in the copula family. The variance-corrected
SIM composition we develop below threads these constraints: the closed-form scalar correction lets a full-return
generator enter the factor pipeline while preserving the marginal
variance, and the auditable $R^2$-driven branch handles
index-tracking edge cases without external metadata.

Validation of synthetic financial data has historically relied on
goodness-of-fit tests for distributional fidelity
\cite{kolmogorov1933, smirnov1948, andersonDarling1952} and on
autocorrelation-based diagnostics for temporal structure
\cite{cont2001}. Recent work has emphasized downstream-task validation
as a complement: a synthetic dataset that passes distributional tests
can still fail when used for the task it was generated to support.
Booth and Fama \cite{boothFama1992} formalized one important
downstream concern: a daily-rebalanced allocator on iid-residual paths
extracts a structural \emph{diversification return} that is not
present under empirical positive residual autocorrelation
\cite{loMacKinlay1990}. We adopt both the classical distributional
metrics and a downstream Value-at-Risk backtest assessed by Kupiec
coverage, and we explicitly address the diversification-return
artifact via a documented bias correction.
