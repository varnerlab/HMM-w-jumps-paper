Generating synthetic financial time series that faithfully preserve
the statistical properties of real market data is a central challenge
in quantitative finance. Synthetic paths are often used for stress testing
risk models against plausible but unobserved market scenarios,
validating portfolio optimization algorithms, augmenting limited
training sets for machine-learning pipelines, and Monte Carlo
backtesting of allocators across many independent scenarios
\cite{assefaEtAl2020, jordonEtAl2022}. Empirical equity excess growth
rates exhibit three statistical regularities a faithful generative
framework must reproduce: a heavy-tailed (leptokurtic)
marginal distribution, negligible linear autocorrelation of raw
growth rates, and persistent volatility clustering, in which large changes in growth rates
are followed by large changes of either sign and small changes by
small changes \cite{mandelbrot1963, cont2001, stengerEtAl2024}. All
three are visible in SPY daily excess growth rates over $2014$--$2024$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}): the marginal density is
sharply peaked with heavy tails that a Gaussian fit cannot reproduce, the
quantile-quantile plot bends away from the Gaussian line at both tails, and the autocorrelation of absolute growth rates decays slowly
while the autocorrelation of raw growth rates sits near zero. These
regularities, called \emph{stylized facts}, are the benchmark for
synthetic financial data.

Existing generative approaches typically capture some of these regularities
but fail at others. For example, the generalized autoregressive
conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) family
\cite{engle1982, bollerslev1986} captures volatility clustering but
leaves light-tailed marginals unless fed a fat-tailed innovation. 
Stochastic-volatility and jump-diffusion
models \cite{heston1993, merton1976, kou2002} enrich tail behavior
but offer no mechanism for persistence after a jump. Deep generative
models \cite{takahashiEtAl2019, yoonEtAl2019timegan, kwonLee2024} can
learn complex marginals yet routinely miss the absolute-return
autocorrelation that defines volatility clustering. Hidden Markov
models (HMMs) \cite{rabinerJuang1986} offer a probabilistic
regime-switching structure, yet standard HMMs revert too quickly
to central regimes
after extreme events and fail to dwell in high-volatility tail states
long enough to reproduce the empirical clustering profile
\cite{rydenTerasvirtaAsbrink1998, bullaBulla2006}. 
Bridging the gap between the stylized facts requires a framework that preserves both distributional fidelity and
temporal structure while remaining computationally scalable
to large asset universes.

In this study, we developed a hybrid hidden Markov framework that
pairs a Laplace-quantile state partition with a Poisson
jump-duration mechanism to reproduce all three stylized facts. The Laplace partition
lets the transition matrix be estimated by direct frequentist
counting, bypassing the iterative Baum-Welch algorithm
\cite{baumPetrieSoulesWeiss1970, bilmes1998}, and easily scales to a $424$-asset pipeline. 
The Poisson override forces the chain to dwell in high-volatility tail states for
empirically realistic durations, closing the volatility-clustering
gap that standard HMMs leave open. Within each state we used a
Student-$t$ emission to accommodate the heavy tails that Normal
emissions miss. We validated the
framework on a $424$-ticker US equity and exchange-traded fund
(ETF) universe calibrated
against the $2014$--$2024$ SPY history, with $2025$ held out for
out-of-sample evaluation, scoring synthetic paths against seven
alternative generators (bootstrap, Gaussian, Laplace, GARCH(1,1), a
hidden semi-Markov model \cite{bullaBulla2006}, a hidden Markov model
without jumps, and a Gated Recurrent Unit \cite{choEtAl2014gru}
neural baseline) on Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS), Anderson-Darling
(AD), Wasserstein-$1$, and Hellinger pass rates for distributional
fidelity, together with the autocorrelation function of absolute
growth rates for volatility clustering. 

Extending the framework to
multi-asset workflows required addressing a known limitation of the
naive single-index composition: drawing the residual from a per-asset
generator inflates the composed marginal variance quadratically in
the factor loading. We closed this limitation with a closed-form
variance correction that restores each ticker's empirical variance
after factor composition, so the composed marginal matches its
calibration target rather than the inflated naive composition. 
Next, allocator backtests run on synthetic data face a separate
multi-asset artifact: drawing each asset's residual independently
of the others exaggerates the daily cross-sectional spread among
assets, and daily rebalancing converts that excess spread into
spurious portfolio gains on any synthetic series generated using
this approach. We removed the bias with a calibrated
location-shift on each ticker's drift. Finally, since tail-risk
applications such as stress testing and risk-model validation rely
on calibrated Value-at-Risk (VaR) forecasts, for each ticker we
asked the generator to forecast tomorrow's worst plausible loss,
ran the forecast over a real holdout window, and checked whether
the actual breach frequency matched what the model promised (a
one-day VaR backtest scored by Kupiec's unconditional-coverage
test).
