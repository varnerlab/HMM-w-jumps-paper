The empirical foundation of this study was a dataset of $424$ United
States-listed equities and exchange-traded funds spanning ten years
($2014$--$2024$). To validate the hybrid hidden Markov framework on a
single canonical asset, we focused our headline analysis on the SPDR
S\&P 500 ETF (SPY), reserving the $2,766$ observations from January 3,
$2014$ to December 31, $2024$ for in-sample fitting and the subsequent
$249$ trading days of $2025$ for out-of-sample testing. The data
consisted of daily open, high, low, and close prices and
volume-weighted average price values. We computed the excess
growth rate $G_{i,j}$ for ticker $i$ between time period $j-1
\rightarrow j$ from the equity price series $P_{i,j}$ as
\begin{equation}
    G_{i,j} \equiv \left( \frac{1}{\Delta t} \right) \cdot
    \ln\!\left( \frac{P_{i,j}}{P_{i,j-1}} \right) - r_f,
    \label{eq:growth_rate}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta t = 1/252$ (one trading day in years) and $r_f$ a constant
continuously compounded risk-free rate derived from STRIPS bond yields.
The excess growth rate has units of year$^{-1}$ and is time-additive
under continuous compounding.

\subsubsection{State space, Laplace partition, and emissions}
\label{sec:method-hmm-states}

We defined the hybrid hidden Markov model as the tuple
$\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathbf{T}, \mathbf{E}, \bar{\pi})$
\cite{rabinerJuang1986}, where the hidden state
$S_t \in \mathcal{S} = \{1,\dots,N\}$ represented market regimes, the
observation space $\mathcal{O} \subset \mathbb{R}$ consisted of
continuous excess growth rate values, $\mathbf{T}$ governed
regime-to-regime transitions, $\mathbf{E}$ specified the
state-conditional emission model, and $\bar{\pi}$ denoted the
stationary distribution. States were defined by quantile boundaries
$Q_k = F_L^{-1}(k/N;\, \mu_L, b_L)$ of a fitted Laplace distribution,
with $Q_0 = -\infty$ and $Q_N = +\infty$. The Laplace was chosen for
its sharper peak (better matching the concentration of small price
movements \cite{mandelbrot1963, cont2001, kotzKozubowskiPodgorski2001})
and its closed-form quantile function, which scaled to the $424$-ticker
pipeline without iterative optimization \cite{bilmes1998}. An
observation was assigned to state $k$ when $Q_{k-1} < G_t \le Q_k$. The
within-state emission followed a location-scale Student-$t$
distribution
\begin{equation}
    G_t \mid S_t = k \;\sim\; \mu_k + \sigma_k \cdot t_{\nu},
    \qquad \nu = 5,
    \label{eq:emission}
\end{equation}
where $\nu=5$ was selected from a sensitivity analysis over
$\nu \in \{3,4,5,6,7,8,10,15,30,\infty\}$ as the value that minimized
the kurtosis gap without degrading distributional fidelity
($\nu=\infty$ recovers the Normal baseline).

\subsubsection{Transition matrix and jump override}
\label{sec:method-hmm-jump}

We estimated the transition matrix $\mathbf{T}$ through direct
frequentist counting: the discrete state assignments allowed
straightforward counting of observed transitions between regimes,
making the approach conceptually simple and free of the initialization
sensitivity inherent to expectation-maximization
\cite{bilmes1998}. A consequence of direct counting was that empirical
transition matrices often had low probabilities for exiting central
states into tail states, which caused models to lose the effect of
volatility clustering too quickly \cite{bullaBulla2006}. We corrected
this by adding two jump parameters, $\epsilon$ representing the
probability of a jump event and $\lambda$ representing the mean
jump-event duration, along with a tail-state selection rule. Tail
states were defined as $\mathcal{S}_{-} = \{1, \dots, N_{\rm tail}\}$
and $\mathcal{S}_{+} = \{N - N_{\rm tail} + 1, \dots, N\}$, where
$N_{\rm tail}$ was user-configurable. When a jump was triggered, the
jump duration $K$ was sampled from a Poisson distribution
$K \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$, which is standard for modeling
independent events in fixed intervals \cite{glasserman2003}. During
these $K$ steps, the standard Markovian transitions were overridden to
target tail states, with a user-configurable bias $p_{\rm neg}$
(default $0.52$) that favored negative-tail states to reproduce
gain/loss asymmetry (Figure~\ref{fig:model_architecture}). Given the
state assignments and transition matrix, the remaining model
components were estimated directly from the data: the emission
parameters $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k$ were the sample mean and standard
deviation of observations assigned to state $k$, and the empirical
transition matrix exhibited a dominant near-diagonal band reflecting
short-range regime persistence (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-transitions}).
The stationary distribution $\bar{\pi}$ satisfied
$\bar{\pi} = \bar{\pi}\mathbf{T}$ and was estimated by matrix powering
($\mathbf{T}^{50}$) \cite{hamilton2018regime, grinsteadSnell1997}. An
initial state was drawn from $X_0 \sim \bar{\pi}$; at each subsequent
step the chain either transitioned according to the empirical row
$\mathbf{T}_{X_{n-1},\,\cdot}$ (probability $1-\epsilon$) or entered a
Poisson jump episode (probability $\epsilon$) that forced the state
into $\mathcal{S}_{-}$ or $\mathcal{S}_{+}$ for $K$ consecutive steps
before normal transitions resumed. In both cases an observation was
sampled from the emission distribution $O_n \sim \mathbf{E}_{X_n}$. The
full simulation pseudocode is given in Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-pseudo}.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter optimization}
\label{sec:method-hmm-tuning}

To identify the jump probability $\epsilon$ and mean jump duration
$\lambda$ \cite{merton1976, kou2002}, we implemented a multi-objective
grid search that minimized the discrepancy between historical and
simulated market signatures. The objective targeted two stylized facts
\cite{cont2001, rydenTerasvirtaAsbrink1998}: volatility clustering,
measured as the squared error between the observed and simulated
autocorrelation function (ACF) of absolute excess growth rates
\cite{schwert1989} up to a lag of $L=252$ trading days (approximately
one trading year), and leptokurtosis, captured by a penalty term on
the global kurtosis \cite{mandelbrot1963}:
\begin{equation}
    J(\epsilon, \lambda) =
    \sum_{\tau=1}^{L} \left( \mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau)
    - \overline{\mathrm{ACF}}_{\rm sim}(\tau) \right)^2
    + w_K \left( K_{\rm obs} - \overline{K}_{\rm sim} \right)^2,
    \label{eq:grid_search}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ and $K_{\rm obs}$ are the
empirical absolute-growth autocorrelation at lag $\tau$ and the global
kurtosis, respectively, and the simulated counterparts
$\overline{\mathrm{ACF}}_{\rm sim}(\tau)$ and $\overline{K}_{\rm sim}$
were averaged across $200$ independent synthetic paths of $2{,}766$
trading days per grid point \cite{glasserman2003}. We set the
kurtosis-penalty weight to $w_K = 0.20$ to balance tail behavior
against the temporal ACF term. The grid explored $\epsilon$ from
$10^{-4}$ to $2.5 \times 10^{-2}$ and $\lambda$ from $10$ to $160$. The
full pseudocode is given in Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-pseudo}.

\paragraph{From single-asset marginals to multi-asset composition.}
The hybrid hidden Markov framework above produces a synthetic excess
growth-rate path for one asset at a time, with a marginal that
reproduces the asset's heavy tails, regime persistence, and
volatility-clustering profile (validated empirically in
Section~\ref{sec:results}). Multi-asset workflows that consume these
paths (portfolio construction, factor risk attribution, multi-name
stress testing) need a second ingredient on top of the per-asset
marginals: a cross-sectional structure that ties the synthetic series
together so they co-move the way real markets do. The next subsection
develops a closed-form composition rule that takes the per-asset
hybrid HMM marginal as a black-box input and inserts it into a
single-index market factor in a way that preserves the marginal the
generator was built to deliver.
