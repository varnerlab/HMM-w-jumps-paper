Synthetic financial data often fail to match important features of
real returns. Researchers use synthetic paths to stress-test risk
models against unobserved market scenarios, validate portfolio
optimization algorithms, and evaluate allocation strategies across
many simulations~\cite{assefaEtAl2020, jordonEtAl2022}. We focused on
three common patterns in daily equity excess growth rates: a
heavy-tailed return distribution, little autocorrelation in raw
growth rates, and persistent volatility clustering, in which large
changes are followed by large changes and small changes by small
changes~\cite{mandelbrot1963, cont2001, stengerEtAl2024}. All three
patterns are visible in SPY daily excess growth rates from
$2014$--$2024$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}). Most observed
returns lie close to zero, but extreme returns occur much more often
than a Gaussian model predicts
(Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}a and
Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}b). Raw growth rates show little
correlation across days (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}c). In
contrast, absolute growth rates remain positively correlated over
longer periods (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}d), so large and
small changes cluster over time. These common patterns are called
\emph{stylized facts}. They show why matching only the return
distribution is not enough: raw returns should remain nearly
uncorrelated, while their sizes should remain correlated over time.

Different generators capture different parts of this behavior. The
generalized autoregressive
conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) family~\cite{engle1982,
bollerslev1986} captures volatility clustering but may need
heavy-tailed random shocks to match extreme returns. Stochastic-volatility and
jump-diffusion models~\cite{heston1993, merton1976, kou2002} produce
heavy tails, but jumps alone do not create persistent volatility.
Deep generative
models~\cite{takahashiEtAl2019, yoonEtAl2019timegan, kwonLee2024}
can learn flexible return distributions, but some miss the
correlation in absolute returns that marks volatility clustering.
Standard hidden Markov models (HMMs)~\cite{rabinerJuang1986} switch
among market regimes but return to central states too quickly to
reproduce long periods of large
returns~\cite{rydenTerasvirtaAsbrink1998, bullaBulla2006}. To address this
problem, we built a separate hidden Markov generator for each asset.
It divided returns into Laplace-quantile states, used Student-$t$
draws within each state, and estimated state transitions by direct
counting. Its jump-duration variant (HMM-WJ) added temporary runs in
the extreme-return states. This model defined the single-asset part of
the study.

Good single-asset paths are not enough for a multi-asset simulation.
The assets must also respond to shared market movements and retain
relationships that the market does not explain. Portfolio risk and
the results of rebalancing depend on how assets move
together~\cite{embrechtsMcNeilStraumann2002, boothFama1992}. Copulas and
multivariate hidden-state models can model these relationships
directly, but they require fitting the assets
together~\cite{diasVermuntRamos2010, aasCzadoFrigessiBakken2009}. Factor models
are easier to scale because they divide each asset's return into
common and asset-specific
parts~\cite{sharpe1963, rossAPT1976, famaFrench1993}. However, a factor
model needs a path for the asset-specific part, which is called the
residual. A generator fitted to an asset's observed returns instead
produces a full-return path. Using the full-return path as a residual
can shift the asset mean and add market variance a second time.
Residuals generated independently also miss shared sector and style
movements that the market does not explain. The challenge is to
combine the single-asset generators with a dependence model without
changing the behavior that either part is meant to capture.

In this study, we developed a variance correction for combining
generators fitted separately to each asset with a shared synthetic
market path through a single-index model. We subtracted the mean of
each asset-level path and rescaled the path before adding the market
component. This prevented the full-return draw from shifting the
fitted mean and prevented the market factor from adding variance a
second time. The method reused the asset-level generator without
fitting another model to each asset's regression residuals. We tested
the method on $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset universe, with
SPY as the common market path. To focus on how the paths were
combined, we set the jump probability to zero in this experiment.
With all $2014$--$2024$ parameters held fixed, the correction
increased the mean share of paths that passed the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov test of the return distribution from $83.5\%$ to
$87.0\%$ on $416$ complete asset histories from $2025$. It also
reduced the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance from
$1.29$ to $0.97$. The method did not capture dependence among
residuals, so independent residuals could overstate the return from
daily rebalancing. Together, these results show that a realistic
multi-asset simulation must do three things: reproduce each asset's
behavior, preserve its response to the market, and capture dependence
among residuals. Our method did the first two but not the third.
