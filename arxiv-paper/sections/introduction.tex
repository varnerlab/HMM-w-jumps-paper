Researchers and practitioners use synthetic equity paths to test risk
models, evaluate portfolio strategies, and examine market scenarios
beyond those observed in a historical sample~\cite{assefaEtAl2020,
jordonEtAl2022}. A multi-asset simulator must represent both the
behavior of each asset and the relationships among assets. Factor
models link many assets through a shared market path, a time series of
market returns~\cite{sharpe1963, rossAPT1976, famaFrench1993}. The
simplest of these, the single-index model, separates each asset's
return into an intercept, a market component scaled by the asset's
fitted loading, and a zero-mean asset-specific residual. A synthetic
asset path can then be composed by adding a simulated residual to the
intercept and market component. If single-asset generators have
already been fitted to each asset's full returns, their paths might
be reused as residuals. Each generator, however, already represents
its asset's total variance, including the part driven by the market.
Adding its paths to the market component therefore counts market
variance a second time. Subtracting each path's mean removes its mean
shift but leaves this excess variance. Fitting a second generator to
each asset's regression residuals avoids the problem but repeats the
fitting for every asset. Reusing the existing generators would avoid
that second fit, provided their paths could be transformed to supply
only the residual contribution. The question is whether such a
transformation can control the mean and variance of the composed paths
while retaining the generator's heavy tails and volatility clustering.

The transformation is useful only if each generator reproduces the
behavior of its own asset. We illustrate the features a generator must
capture with the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY,
which also supplied the market path in our multi-asset experiments.
Its $2014$--$2024$ returns show heavy tails and volatility clustering
(Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}). Most returns lie close to zero,
but extreme values occur much more often than a Gaussian model
predicts (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}a,b), a widely documented
feature of financial returns~\cite{mandelbrot1963, cont2001}. Raw
returns show little correlation across days, while absolute returns
remain positively correlated across many lags
(Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}c,d). Large and small absolute
returns therefore cluster in time, a property that synthetic
financial data often miss even when their return histogram appears
realistic~\cite{stengerEtAl2024}. To represent heavy tails and
volatility clustering, a hidden Markov model (HMM) draws returns from
state-dependent distributions
and uses transitions between states to represent temporal
behavior~\cite{rabinerJuang1986, rydenTerasvirtaAsbrink1998}. Each state
in the model used here has a heavy-tailed Student-$t$ return
distribution, and the next state depends only on the current state,
not on earlier returns. Fitted to SPY, the model leaves states with
the most negative or positive mean returns quickly, and the
autocorrelation of its absolute returns decays faster than in the
data. A duration
mechanism that occasionally keeps drawing from extreme-return states
for consecutive days can slow that decay~\cite{bullaBulla2006}.
Adding the market component, however, changes the distribution and
temporal behavior of the composed returns. An improvement in a
single-asset generator must therefore be checked again after
composition.

In this study, we developed a variance correction to reuse full-return
generators in a single-index model without counting market variance
twice. The correction centered and rescaled each simulated path to
remove the mean shift and account for the variance supplied by the
market component
(Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}). We applied it to heavy-tailed hidden
Markov generators for the $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset
universe, using $2014$--$2024$ for fitting and $2025$ as a holdout.
We compared the correction with naive composition, which centered the
paths without rescaling, with Gaussian residuals, and with generators
fitted directly to regression residuals. The correction improved
agreement with observed return distributions over naive composition
while retaining heavy tails in training (Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}).
With all fits held fixed, the correction also improved distributional
fit over naive composition and
brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to
one on the $416$ assets with complete holdout histories
(Table~\ref{tab:oos_composition}). The residual-fit hidden Markov
generator and the correction gave similar agreement with observed
return distributions in both periods. Without the duration mechanism, the correction left absolute-return
autocorrelation error essentially unchanged from naive composition (Table~\ref{tab:jump_ablation}).
Transferring SPY's duration settings to the composed paths improved
their autocorrelation agreement in training but worsened it in the
holdout (Table~\ref{tab:jump_ablation}).
The correction imposed a zero residual sum over each simulated path,
removing cumulative residual uncertainty at the path endpoint. Neither reuse nor residual
fitting modeled dependence among different assets' residuals, which
portfolio evaluation also requires~\cite{embrechtsMcNeilStraumann2002,
boucheyNemtchinovWong2015}. These results support reuse as a practical
option for daily-return comparisons when full-return generators
already exist.
