For each asset, the hidden Markov generator divided the range of growth rates, which we also call returns, into $N=100$ ordered intervals and treated the interval that contained a day's
return as that day's state (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}). We write
$t$ for the day index $j$ and omit the ticker index $i$ when
discussing one asset. At each simulated time step, the model
first selected a state and then drew a return from that state's
distribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:model_architecture}). We represented the
model as
$\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathbf{T}, \mathbf{E}, \bar{\pi})$
~\cite{rabinerJuang1986}.
The hidden state $S_t \in \mathcal{S} = \{1,\dots,N\}$ identifies the
interval on trading day $t$, and the observation space
$\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}$ contains the excess growth rates $G_t$
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:growth_rate}. The transition
matrix $\mathbf{T}$ sets the probabilities of moving between states,
$\mathbf{E}$ gives the return distribution within each state, and
$\bar{\pi}$ gives the initial state probabilities. We ordered the
states from the most negative to the most positive returns and placed their boundaries at the quantiles of a Laplace distribution,
fitted to the asset's in-sample returns, at the equally spaced
probability levels $k/N$. We used the Laplace
distribution because its sharp peak matches the concentration of
small price movements in Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}a
~\cite{kotzKozubowskiPodgorski2001, tothJones2019}. Its quantile
function also has a closed form, so the boundaries for all $424$
tickers required no iterative fitting~\cite{bilmes1998}. Specifically,
$Q_k = F_L^{-1}(k/N;\, \mu_L, b_L)$, where $F_L^{-1}$ is the Laplace
quantile function and $\mu_L$ and $b_L$ are the fitted location and
scale. We set $Q_0=-\infty$ and $Q_N=+\infty$ and assigned return
$G_t$ to state $k$ when $Q_{k-1}\le G_t<Q_k$. Because each training
return determines its state, the fitted state sequence is known; the
states are hidden only in simulated paths, where the model generates
the state sequence and reports only the returns.

The within-state emission followed a location-scale Student-$t$
distribution:
\begin{equation}
    G_t \mid S_t = k \;\sim\; \mu_k + \sigma_k \cdot t_{\nu},
    \qquad \nu = 5,
    \label{eq:emission}
\end{equation}
where $t_\nu$ denotes a standard Student-$t$ random variable with
$\nu$ degrees of freedom. For observations assigned to state $k$,
we used the sample mean for $\mu_k$ and the sample standard
deviation for $\sigma_k$, subject to two fallback rules. A state
with fewer than two observations used the mean and standard
deviation of that asset's full training series. A state with at
least two observations and sample standard deviation below $10^{-12}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$
retained its state mean but used the full-series standard deviation
as its scale. These exceptions kept emission parameters defined but could broaden
states containing repeated returns (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-fallback});
neither applied to SPY, whose states all held many observations. The same rules applied to the
generators that the multi-asset comparison fitted to regression
residuals. A standard $t_\nu$ has variance $\nu/(\nu-2)$, so
$\sigma_k$ is a scale parameter rather than the emission standard
deviation. At $\nu=5$, each state's emission standard deviation is
$\sigma_k\sqrt{5/3}$, larger than the sample standard deviation of
the returns assigned to that state. The emission is not truncated to
the state's interval, so a simulated return can fall outside it. We
selected $\nu = 5$ from a
sensitivity sweep and used $\nu = \infty$, which gives Normal
emissions, as a baseline; Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} gives the
explored range. For SPY, holding the rest of the model fixed, the
Student-$t$ emission raised simulated kurtosis toward the observed value and
raised the Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) and Anderson--Darling (AD) pass
rates relative to the Normal emission (Table~\ref{tab:emission_hsmm}).
A pass rate is the percentage of simulated paths whose two-sample
test against the observed returns gave $p>0.05$.

We estimated the transition matrix $\mathbf{T}$ by counting observed
transitions between consecutive states and dividing each row by its
total count. A state with no observed outgoing transition used a
uniform row, $\mathbf{T}_{kj}=1/N$ for every destination state $j$.
Because the training state sequence is known, these row frequencies
are the maximum-likelihood estimates, and counting avoided the
initialization sensitivity of iterative
expectation-maximization~\cite{bilmes1998}. We obtained the
initial-state distribution $\bar{\pi}$ by solving
$\bar{\pi}\mathbf{T}=\bar{\pi}$ with
$\sum_k\bar{\pi}_k=1$~\cite{hamilton2018regime, grinsteadSnell1997}.
Because $\mathbf{T}$ was estimated by counting, $\bar{\pi}$ equals
the vector of in-sample state frequencies up to end effects, so the
model's stationary growth-rate distribution is the
frequency-weighted mixture of the state emission distributions. The counted probability of remaining in the same state was low
(Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gen-nojump}), but volatility clustering
requires the chain to stay in the tail region, not in one state. Counting reproduces the observed
frequencies of consecutive state pairs. At longer lags, however, the
chain's absolute-return autocorrelation decays geometrically at a rate
set by the counted matrix, much faster than the slow decay in
Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}d. The counted chain therefore
forgets within a few steps whether it was in the tails and cannot
reproduce the observed volatility clustering~\cite{bullaBulla2006}. Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-generator} derives the fitted generator's
stationary law and autocorrelation structure and quantifies both this
rapid mixing and the autocorrelation added by the jump mechanism.

We added jump episodes to extend visits to extreme-return states and
call the resulting generator the hidden Markov model with jumps
(HMM-WJ).
When no jump episode was active, a jump was triggered with
probability $\epsilon$ at each step, and its duration was drawn as
$K\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the mean episode
length~\cite{glasserman2003}. A draw of $K=0$ produced no episode,
and the chain made an ordinary transition. For $K>0$, the episode
consisted of $K$ \emph{forced tail steps}, beginning with the step
that triggered it. At each forced step, the model replaced the
ordinary transition by first choosing one of two tail sets and then
drawing a state uniformly from that set. The set
$\mathcal{S}_{-}=\{1,\dots,N_{\rm tail}\}$ contained the most negative
states, and $\mathcal{S}_{+}=\{N-N_{\rm tail}+1,\dots,N\}$ contained the
most positive states. The model chose the negative set with
probability $p_{\rm neg}$ and the positive set otherwise. Because
$p_{\rm neg}$ is slightly above one half, negative extreme returns
occurred slightly more often than positive ones. The choice was
repeated at each forced step, so one episode can contain
returns of both signs. Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} lists $\epsilon$, $\lambda$,
$p_{\rm neg}$, and $N_{\rm tail}$; we tuned $\epsilon$ and $\lambda$
and fixed $p_{\rm neg}$ and $N_{\rm tail}$. Jump-diffusion models
use Poisson arrivals to add instantaneous price
jumps~\cite{merton1976, kou2002}. Our model instead uses the Poisson distribution for the episode
length, and an episode is a
sustained visit to the tail states rather than a single price move.
Setting $\epsilon=0$ removes the mechanism; we call this variant the
hidden Markov model with no jumps (HMM-NJ). Two closed-form approximations describe how much of a path, and how
many paths, the mechanism affects (Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-generator}). Forced steps occupy a long-run
fraction of approximately $\epsilon\lambda/(1+\epsilon\lambda)$ of all
steps. A path of $T$ steps contains at least one episode with
probability approximately $1 - e^{-\epsilon T}$, so $\epsilon$ sets
how many paths contain an episode, while $\lambda$ sets how long an
episode lasts. The jump-augmented process is Markov only in the pair
formed by the state and the number of remaining forced steps. The solved $\bar{\pi}$ is therefore the exact stationary distribution
of the ordinary chain but only an approximate stationary start for
the jump-augmented process. The full simulation
procedure appears in Algorithm~\ref{alg:hmmwj}. Each step followed
$\mathbf{T}$ or continued a jump episode and then drew a return from
the selected state's emission distribution.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Hidden Markov model with jumps (HMM-WJ) single-asset marginal generator.}
\label{alg:hmmwj}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \REQUIRE Fitted parameters
    $(\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{E}, \bar{\pi}, \epsilon, \lambda,
    p_{\rm neg}, N_{\rm tail})$; horizon $T$;
    tail sets $\mathcal{S}_{-} = \{1, \dots, N_{\rm tail}\}$ and
    $\mathcal{S}_{+} = \{N - N_{\rm tail} + 1, \dots, N\}$.
  \ENSURE State sequence $\{S_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ and growth-rate path
    $\{G_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$.
  \STATE Draw $S_1 \sim \bar{\pi}$;\quad
    draw $G_1 \sim \mu_{S_1} + \sigma_{S_1}\, t_{5}$;\quad
    $k \gets 0$
    \hfill \COMMENT{stationary start; $k$: remaining forced-step counter}
  \FOR{$t = 2, \dots, T$}
    \IF{$k > 0$}
      \STATE $\mathcal{J} \gets \mathcal{S}_{-}$ w.p.\ $p_{\rm neg}$, else $\mathcal{S}_{+}$;\quad
        $S_t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{J})$;\quad $k \gets k - 1$
        \hfill \COMMENT{forced tail step; sign drawn per step}
    \ELSIF{$\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1) > \epsilon$}
      \STATE $S_t \sim \mathbf{T}_{S_{t-1}, \cdot}$
        \hfill \COMMENT{standard Markov transition}
    \ELSE
      \STATE $K \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$
        \hfill \COMMENT{trigger jump; $K$ is the episode length}
      \IF{$K = 0$}
        \STATE $S_t \sim \mathbf{T}_{S_{t-1}, \cdot}$
          \hfill \COMMENT{$K = 0$: no forced steps, standard transition}
      \ELSE
        \STATE $\mathcal{J} \gets \mathcal{S}_{-}$ w.p.\ $p_{\rm neg}$, else $\mathcal{S}_{+}$;\quad
          $S_t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{J})$;\quad
          $k \gets \min(K - 1, T - t)$
          \hfill \COMMENT{first forced step; $K$ steps total, sign per step}
      \ENDIF
    \ENDIF
    \STATE Draw $G_t \sim \mu_{S_t} + \sigma_{S_t}\, t_{5}$
      \hfill \COMMENT{Eq.~\eqref{eq:emission}}
  \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We tuned the jump probability $\epsilon$ and mean duration $\lambda$
on the volume-weighted SPY training series to match its absolute-return autocorrelation and excess
kurtosis~\cite{mandelbrot1963, cont2001, rydenTerasvirtaAsbrink1998};
the multi-asset comparisons either disabled jumps or transferred the
SPY values to the closing-price generators
(Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}). A path with no episode follows HMM-NJ, so its autocorrelation and
kurtosis errors do not depend on $(\epsilon,\lambda)$. We scored only \emph{jump-active} paths, those containing at least one
episode~\cite{glasserman2003}, so the objective measures how well an
episode reproduces the observed statistics, not how often episodes
occur. At each point of a grid of
$(\epsilon,\lambda)$ values, we simulated $200$ independent paths of
$2{,}766$ trading days. We minimized an objective that averaged, over
the jump-active paths, the squared errors in the autocorrelation
function (ACF) of absolute returns through lag $L$ plus a weighted
squared error in excess kurtosis:
\begin{equation}
    J(\epsilon, \lambda) =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_J|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_J}
    \left[
    \sum_{\tau=1}^{L} \left( \mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau)
    - \mathrm{ACF}_{r}(\tau) \right)^2
    + w_\kappa \left( \kappa_{\rm obs} - \kappa_{r} \right)^2
    \right],
    \label{eq:grid_search}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}_J$ is the set of jump-active simulated paths,
$\mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ and $\kappa_{\rm obs}$ are the empirical
absolute-growth autocorrelation at lag $\tau$ and excess kurtosis,
$\mathrm{ACF}_{r}(\tau)$ and $\kappa_r$ are the corresponding values
for path $r$, and $w_\kappa$ sets the relative weight of the kurtosis
error. When $\epsilon$ is small, as at the selected value, most
jump-active paths contain a single episode, whose forced
steps make up a fraction of about $\lambda/T$ of the path. The episode
adds a slowly decaying component to the absolute-return
autocorrelation, and $\lambda$ sets both its height and the number of
lags over which it persists. The kurtosis penalty limits $\lambda$, because forced steps make tail
returns common within a path rather than rare; they raise the variance
proportionally more than the fourth moment and so lower the path
kurtosis. The parameter $\epsilon$ acts mainly through the share
of paths that contain an episode, which the restriction to jump-active paths removes. The objective
therefore constrains $\epsilon$ only weakly, and the selected
$\epsilon$ lay at the lower edge of its search range
(Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}; Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gen-tuning}).
The search ranges, $w_\kappa$, $L$, and the remaining hyperparameters
are reported in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}. We fixed the selected
hyperparameters before running the generator comparisons and the
holdout evaluation.

Beyond the Normal-emission variant, we compared HMM-WJ with two
benchmarks that model persistence differently. A hidden semi-Markov
model (HSMM) baseline adapted from Bulla and
Bulla~\cite{bullaBulla2006} lengthens state visits through explicit
dwell-time distributions instead of jump episodes
(Table~\ref{tab:emission_hsmm}; Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-emission-hsmm}). The Normal-emission comparison
isolated the effect of heavy-tailed emissions, and the HSMM
comparison contrasted explicit dwell times with the jump-duration
mechanism, although the HSMM also used $8$ states rather than $100$ and so
differed in more than its duration model. To compare the hidden Markov
generator with a neural autoregressive model, we
included a gated recurrent unit (GRU) benchmark
(Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). The network took a $50$-return input window and had two recurrent
layers of $64$ hidden units each. We standardized the training series with its mean and standard
deviation, and separate output heads predicted the mean and log
variance of the next standardized return. We fitted the network on training SPY only,
selected weights by training loss, and sampled recursively from
the predicted Gaussian distributions. Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gru} specifies the optimizer, stopping rule,
initialization, and recorded computational environment. We scored all single-asset generators,
including these benchmarks, by their KS and AD pass rates and by the mean absolute error between the observed and the
ensemble-average simulated absolute-return autocorrelation functions
(ACF-MAE), over lags $1$--$252$ in training and $1$--$248$ in the
$249$-day holdout (Eq.~\eqref{eq:acf-ensemble-error} in Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-validation}).
