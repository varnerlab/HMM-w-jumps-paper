Synthetic generators of equity returns should reproduce three
stylized facts: heavy tails, weak autocorrelation in raw returns, and
volatility clustering (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation}). We used
heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators with optional jump episodes to
represent these features, then examined their reuse in a multi-asset
model with a shared market path. We developed a correction that
centers and rescales each generator's draws to avoid counting market
variance twice, without refitting to regression residuals
(Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}; Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}). Training and
$2025$ holdout comparisons tested distributional fit and whether
single-asset improvements in volatility clustering survived
composition.

The correction's practical value was avoiding a separate residual
fit while improving variance and distributional agreement over naive
composition (Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation};
Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}). The corrected method passed $73.6\%$ of
full-length training KS comparisons and retained heavy tails. On the
$2025$ holdout, it raised the mean cross-ticker KS pass rate from
$83.5\%$ under centered naive composition to $87.0\%$ and brought
the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance to $0.97$
(Table~\ref{tab:oos_composition}). The improvement therefore
persisted beyond the fitting period. Direct residual fitting remained
competitive: JumpHMM-on-residuals had a slightly higher pass rate in
training and a slightly lower rate in the holdout. When all methods'
residual draws were centered over the same training horizon on the
common asset set, JumpHMM-on-residuals and block bootstrap both
outperformed the corrected method on distribution-test pass rates and
Wasserstein distance (Table~\ref{tab:centering_control}). Before centering was matched, the comparison with residual-fit
methods therefore partly reflected different constraints on simulated
residual means. For corrected composition, some assets had lower pass rates in the
holdout than against matched-length training blocks. Reuse thus offered a way to improve
on naive composition without refitting, but did not establish a
distributional advantage over fitting the residuals themselves.

The jump comparison showed that improving the single-asset generator
did not automatically improve the composed paths
(Tables~\ref{tab:model_comparison} and~\ref{tab:jump_ablation}). On
SPY, the generator with jumps reduced absolute-return autocorrelation
error relative to its no-jump counterpart while retaining high
distributional pass rates. Other generators achieved lower
autocorrelation error or higher pass rates, so temporal and marginal
fit still required separate assessment. With multi-asset composition,
market-only jumps improved training temporal fit with little change
in the KS pass rate, whereas adding asset-level episodes produced a
larger temporal improvement at a substantial marginal-fit cost. Both
settings increased autocorrelation error in $2025$, despite the mean
composed-to-generator variance ratio remaining near one
(Table~\ref{tab:jump_ablation}). Variance control therefore did not
ensure that the transferred temporal behavior matched the data. These
tests transferred one SPY calibration from volume-weighted average
prices to closing-price models. The asset episodes also came from
full-return models and were not identified separately as idiosyncratic
events. Separate calibrations for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM had strong
in-sample fit, but three assets did not establish a general calibration
rule (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-cross-asset}). Per-asset tuning or distinct shared
and asset-specific episode models could change the tradeoff, but
neither was tested in the composition comparison.

Preserving total variance did not preserve the complete return
distribution, and corrected fit varied with market exposure
(Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}; Table~\ref{tab:beta_bucket}). Corrected
KS pass rates fell from $87.6\%$ in the lowest $\beta$ quartile to
$65.9\%$ in the highest, consistent with a larger market contribution
diluting the asset generator's shape. The correction accounted for variance
supplied by the market, but combining two independent
return distributions still changed the shape of the composed returns.
Clipping, the reduction of the market loading to retain some residual
variance, was unnecessary at observed market variance but activated
for most assets when market volatility was doubled or tripled (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sim-derivations}). That stress transformation
preserved the market mean. Reducing the loading while retaining the fitted intercept changed
the asset mean by
$(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$, where $\overline{g}_m$
is the market mean. The clipped branch therefore targeted total variance while retaining
a minimum residual contribution, at the cost of changing the
calibrated loading and mean. The stress test did not establish realistic return distributions
under the scaled markets. Variance correction therefore required separate checks of
distributional shape and calibrated market exposure.

The correction supported daily-return comparisons, but broader
simulation uses require additional modeling and validation. The alternative
correction for market trackers targeted their calibrated $R^2$, the
fraction of return variance explained by the market. Only two
observed assets tested it; a Gaussian simulation checked its formula
without fitting full-return generators (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sim-derivations}). Allowing states whose training observations were identical to
generate nearly constant returns improved training fit (Table~\ref{tab:fallback_sensitivity}). For those states, the main comparisons retained emissions scaled
by the asset's full-series spread, and the alternative still needs holdout validation. Improved
marginal fit did not establish reliable tail coverage: the corrected
method and JumpHMM-on-residuals had pooled-threshold one-day left-tail
$99\%$ VaR exceedance rates of $1.12\%$ and $1.16\%$, against a
nominal $1\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:var}). More simulated paths improved
threshold precision without supplying more observed extreme events;
the $249$-day holdout was too short for a precise ranking and covered
only one shared market period. Serial dependence also limited the
interpretation of KS and AD pass rates, whose tests assume independent
observations. Variance accounting also assumed independent asset and market
draws; nonzero covariance would require an additional cross-term.
For portfolio applications,
a shared market path did not supply the sector and style dependence
omitted by the evaluated independent residual draws
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:hybrid}; Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-bias}).
Omitting positive residual covariance can overstate the
diversification return from periodic rebalancing~\cite{boothFama1992,
boucheyNemtchinovWong2015, markowitz1976longRun}, including for
residual-fit baselines that draw residuals independently. A
synchronized multivariate block bootstrap, residual factor model, or
copula could address that omission, but the resulting dependence
needs direct evaluation. Per-path centering imposed a separate
limitation: each residual sum was zero at the chosen horizon, leaving
no residual uncertainty in terminal cumulative log returns conditional on
the market path and effective loading (Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sim-derivations}). Portfolio and cumulative-risk
applications therefore require both a joint residual model and a
construction that retains cumulative residual variation.
