Equity-market generators address stylized facts through either a
bottom-up or top-down approach. Bottom-up agent-based models~\cite{luxMarchesi1999,
contBouchaud2000, farmerJoshi2002, lebaron2006, arthur2021,
farmer2025} derive return dynamics from simulated interactions among
heterogeneous agents. They are demanding to calibrate and expensive
to simulate at scale. Top-down models instead fit statistical
structures directly to observed growth-rate series.
Top-down generators trace to Bachelier's diffusion model, later
formalized as geometric Brownian motion and used by
Black-Scholes-Merton for option
pricing~\cite{bachelier1900, osborne1959, samuelson1965,
blackScholes1973}. These diffusion baselines are tractable but fail
on the empirical regularities that drive risk and pricing.
Mandelbrot~\cite{mandelbrot1963} showed that returns have heavier tails
than a Gaussian model allows. Fama~\cite{famaEMH1970} reviewed evidence
that raw returns have near-zero autocorrelation. Schwert~\cite{schwert1989}
documented persistence in monthly absolute residuals, a pattern later
described by Cont~\cite{cont2001} as volatility clustering. The autoregressive
conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) and generalized ARCH (GARCH)
models of Engle and Bollerslev~\cite{engle1982, bollerslev1986}
reproduced clustering but required a heavy-tailed noise term to
produce heavy-tailed marginals. Neither model includes discrete
regimes or sudden jumps. Stochastic-volatility
models~\cite{steinStein1991, heston1993} make volatility a latent
process to capture clustering, but estimating that process requires
filtering or Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
methods~\cite{kimShephardChib1998}; jump-diffusion
models~\cite{merton1976, kou2002} inject Poisson events into the
price path, producing heavy tails by construction, but the jump
component alone does not produce persistent volatility.

Capturing discrete regimes and jumps requires a model of how market
states change over time. Hidden Markov models embed this behavior in a
latent discrete chain. This approach follows Hamilton's~\cite{hamilton1989}
two-state regime-switching model, later applied to volatility
forecasting~\cite{rossiGallo2006} and equity
trading~\cite{nguyen2018}. Ryden et
al.~\cite{rydenTerasvirtaAsbrink1998} found that Gaussian-emission
HMMs matched heavy tails and the absence of return autocorrelation
but did not reproduce volatility clustering. Bulla and
Bulla~\cite{bullaBulla2006} modeled clustering with hidden
semi-Markov models (HSMMs) that replaced the geometric dwell-time
distribution of every state with a parametric (negative-binomial)
alternative. Likelihood-fitted HMM and HSMM formulations commonly use
the Baum--Welch expectation-maximization (EM)
algorithm~\cite{baumPetrieSoulesWeiss1970}, which is iterative and can
be sensitive to initialization. Deep generative alternatives learn the
marginal directly but can miss the
volatility-clustering signature: Takahashi et
al.~\cite{takahashiEtAl2019} found that generative adversarial
network (GAN) architectures failed to reproduce the absolute-return
autocorrelation function, and Kwon and Lee~\cite{kwonLee2024}
reported the same failure for TimeGAN~\cite{yoonEtAl2019timegan}. Recent
surveys~\cite{assefaEtAl2020, jordonEtAl2022, stengerEtAl2024} agree
that stylized-facts reproduction remains the primary quality
criterion. Our marginal component also used a latent-state chain, but
it differed from the HSMM. It kept the empirically counted transition
matrix for the central states and added an event-driven
override that forces the chain into the tail states for a
Poisson-distributed duration on a subset of time steps.

Building a multi-asset path adds a second task: modeling how assets
move together. Separate single-asset generators set each asset's
return distribution and behavior over time, but they do not define
joint market moves. Mixture hidden Markov
models~\cite{diasVermuntRamos2010} can share a hidden state across
assets, but they become costly to estimate as the number of assets
grows. Copula methods~\cite{embrechtsMcNeilStraumann2002,
cherubiniLucianoVecchiato2004, aasCzadoFrigessiBakken2009} separate
each asset's distribution from the relationships among assets. They
require a choice of copula family and, in their standard static form,
do not model dependence across successive days. Factor models offer a
simpler approach for large asset sets by linking returns to a small
number of shared factors~\cite{rossAPT1976, famaFrench1993}. The
single-index model (SIM) of Sharpe~\cite{sharpe1963} uses one market
factor and splits each asset's return into a market term and an
asset-specific residual. This structure can use one synthetic market
path for many assets, but it creates a mismatch when the existing
asset-level generator was fitted to full returns rather than
residuals. Using the full-return path as a residual adds its mean to
the fitted intercept and adds market variance to variance already in
the path. Fitting a new generator to every residual avoids this
mismatch but requires a second model for every asset. We instead
centered and rescaled each full-return path before adding the market
factor. This correction lets the fitted asset-level generator be
reused while keeping the market term within the asset's total variance
budget. It does not, however, model dependence among the remaining
residuals. This limit means that validation must cover more than the
return distribution. We used Kolmogorov--Smirnov and
Anderson--Darling tests to assess the
distribution~\cite{kolmogorov1933, smirnov1948, andersonDarling1952},
autocorrelation to assess behavior over time~\cite{cont2001}, and
Value-at-Risk coverage to assess tail risk~\cite{manganelliEngle2001}.
We also tested a daily-rebalanced allocation because a model can fit
each asset separately and still fail at the portfolio level. Booth
and Fama~\cite{boothFama1992} showed how rebalancing volatile assets
can create a \emph{diversification return}. In synthetic data,
independent residuals can exaggerate this effect because they remove
shared sector and style movements. A constant shift can adjust the
average return level, but it cannot restore the missing dependence.
The correction therefore solves the mean and variance problem created
when full-return generators are combined with a market factor; it is
not a complete model of how assets move together.
