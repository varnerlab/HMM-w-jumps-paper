\subsection{Single-asset generator validation}

The SPY returns had heavy tails and persistent absolute-return
autocorrelation in both the training and holdout windows, providing
two targets for generator validation
(Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical_motivation};
Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-generator}). Individual raw-return correlations
were small, although the Ljung--Box test detected joint serial
dependence. We compared HMM-WJ with seven alternatives on the training
window, using $1{,}000$ paths of $2{,}766$ trading days
(Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}; Fig.~\ref{fig:model_comparison}).
The alternatives were independent bootstrap, Gaussian, and Laplace
draws; HMM-NJ; GARCH(1,1) with Gaussian innovations; the GRU
benchmark~\cite{choEtAl2014gru}; and the HSMM baseline adapted from
Bulla and Bulla~\cite{bullaBulla2006}. Both HMM variants had KS pass
rates within about one percentage point of the fitted Laplace
baseline but came much closer to the observed excess kurtosis, which
Laplace underestimated at $3.0$ against $7.7$. Both also had lower
Wasserstein-1 distances than Laplace, whereas Laplace had the lowest
Hellinger distance among the fitted generators. Bootstrap resampling
passed every KS test while reusing the observed returns, and Gaussian
sampling passed neither distribution test. The GARCH model had the
lowest mean absolute error between the observed and ensemble-average
absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE) but rarely passed
the distribution tests. The GRU also had lower ACF-MAE than the
HMM variants but rarely passed the distribution tests. The HSMM
passed $82\%$ of KS tests, underestimated excess kurtosis by more than
one third, and had the same ACF-MAE as HMM-NJ despite its flexible
state durations. A controlled emission ablation confirmed that
Student-$t$ emissions brought HMM-WJ excess kurtosis closer to the
observed value than Normal emissions
(Table~\ref{tab:emission_hsmm}). The HMM variants therefore captured
heavy tails well, but no fitted generator led on every metric.

Jump episodes improved temporal fit at a small cost in marginal fit
in training (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison};
Fig.~\ref{fig:model_comparison}b). Under the counted transition
matrix, every state had an expected residence time of $1$--$2$ steps,
and absolute-return autocorrelation decayed to near zero by lag $5$
(Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gen-nojump}). Calibration selected mean episode
duration $\lambda=90$ and jump probability $\epsilon=10^{-4}$
(Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}). About $24\%$ of training-length HMM-WJ
paths contained an episode; paths without one were equivalent to
HMM-NJ (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gen-renewal}). Across all paths,
ACF-MAE fell from $0.059$ for HMM-NJ to $0.053$ for HMM-WJ, with
small reductions in KS and AD pass rates. The episode frequency and
jump-active autocorrelation agreed with the generator calculations
(Table~\ref{tab:supp-generator-quantities}; Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gen-tuning}). The calibration objective scored
only jump-active paths, so it weakly identified $\epsilon$ and
selected the smallest grid value; the roughly one-quarter episode
share followed from that value and the path length, rather than being
a calibration target. Likewise, $\lambda$ was a fitted duration,
not an estimate of observed high-volatility spell lengths. Supporting sweeps showed that this tradeoff depended on both the
jump-active share and the state count: increasing either improved
autocorrelation fit but reduced distribution-test pass rates (Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gen-tuning}; Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sensitivity}).
The primary choice $N=100$ balanced the training metrics, but the
state-count sweep used SPY alone and did not establish a suitable
resolution for other assets or shorter histories (Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sensitivity}).

The frozen HMM variants retained high distribution-test pass rates on
the $249$ held-out SPY returns from $2025$, although the short sample
limited discrimination among generators
(Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison};
Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gen-renewal}). We compared those returns
with $249$-day simulated paths. The KS pass rates were $96.6\%$ for
HMM-NJ and $95.4\%$ for HMM-WJ, but weaker generators also passed
more often than in the longer training comparison. Few paths
contained an episode at $\epsilon=10^{-4}$, so the holdout mainly
tested the no-jump dynamics shared by the two variants (Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-gen-renewal}). The fitted Laplace baseline
matched or exceeded both on KS and AD pass rates and had equal or
smaller Wasserstein-1 and Hellinger distances. Its excess kurtosis of
$2.8$, however, remained well below the observed $6.9$, whereas both
HMM variants reached $6.2$. Repeating HMM-WJ fitting and calibration
for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM gave training KS pass rates near $99\%$ for
all three, but holdout rates ranged from $74.0\%$ for JNJ to
$97.7\%$ for NVDA (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-cross-asset}). The single-asset evidence supported heavy-tail representation, but
did not establish uniform holdout fit across assets or metrics. The six-method composition comparison used
the no-jump variant to avoid per-ticker jump calibration, and a
separate multi-asset comparison tested the transfer of SPY's jump
settings.

\subsection{Variance-corrected multi-asset composition}

We tested whether hybrid composition preserved generator variance
while recovering the calibrated market loading across the $423$
non-market assets of the training universe
(Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}; Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}). The six-method
comparison included GARCH(1,1)-$t$ on the $393$ assets with eligible
fits. With observed training SPY as the market path, the index trackers QQQ
and SPYG used the $R^2$-preserving branch, and the other $421$ assets,
which we call non-tracker assets, used the variance-preserving branch
without clipping (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sim-derivations}). The two trackers targeted
the calibrated $R^2$ rather than the generator variance, so we marked
them separately (Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}). We paired each composed
path with its uncomposed generator draw to measure the ratio of
composed to generator variance directly. Across the non-tracker
assets, the median of the per-ticker median ratios was $1.3171$ for
naive composition and $1.0001$ for hybrid composition, and the hybrid
ticker medians ranged from $0.9933$ to $1.0072$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}b). Individual hybrid paths still varied
around the target, because the correction assumes zero covariance
between the scaled asset draw and the market path, whereas a finite
path has a small nonzero sample covariance. Across non-tracker hybrid
paths, the median absolute departure of the variance ratio from one
was $1.12\%$, and its $95$th percentile was $3.41\%$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}b). The correlation between generator
draws and the observed market path averaged near zero for every asset
(Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-cov}), which supported the zero-covariance
assumption on average but not on each path.

The methods differed more in distributional fit than in factor
recovery (Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}; Fig.~\ref{fig:tails}). All six
methods had median absolute $\beta$ errors between $0.017$ and
$0.022$. Naive and hybrid composition had a median absolute intercept
error of $0.002\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, an order of magnitude below the
other methods, because their centered residual paths reproduced the
observed training asset means exactly when combined with observed SPY
without clipping. Refitting OLS left an intercept error equal to the
loading error times the market mean. The small intercept errors thus
followed from centering, not from predicting asset means. Gaussian SIM had the
lowest median $R^2$ error but almost never passed the distribution
tests, and its median excess kurtosis was an order of magnitude below
the observed median of about $13$. Hybrid composition passed
$73.6\%$ of KS comparisons, against $7.1\%$ for naive composition,
and its advantage held across the calibrated $\beta$ range. Its own
pass rate nonetheless fell from $87.6\%$ in the lowest $\beta$
quartile to $65.9\%$ in the highest
(Fig.~\ref{fig:preservation}a; Table~\ref{tab:beta_bucket}). A larger market variance share may help explain this trend by
diluting the asset generator's shape (Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-residual-choice}). Hybrid composition
retained heavy tails, with median excess kurtosis of $7.2$ and a
median Hill estimate of $0.37$, reported as the reciprocal tail index
so that larger values indicate heavier tails (Fig.~\ref{fig:tails}).
The excess-kurtosis shortfall was also present under naive
composition, whose median was similar. Hybrid composition's median
$R^2$ error fell between those of the naive and Gaussian methods,
because its variance-preserving branch targeted generator variance
rather than observed $R^2$.

Direct residual fitting was competitive with reuse, and controlling
for residual centering favored the residual-fit HMM and block
bootstrap (Tables~\ref{tab:aggregate}
and~\ref{tab:centering_control}). Under each method's native mean
treatment, JumpHMM-on-residuals passed $75.8\%$ of KS comparisons,
slightly above hybrid composition; block bootstrap passed $73.3\%$
and GARCH(1,1)-$t$ passed $67.4\%$. The residual-fit HMM and block
bootstrap had slightly lower Wasserstein distances than hybrid
composition. The residual-fit HMM also closely matched hybrid composition
in excess kurtosis and Hill estimate, whereas block bootstrap and GARCH
produced lighter tails. Only naive and hybrid composition centered
their residual draws, so we repeated the comparison after centering
all six methods on the common $393$-asset set
(Table~\ref{tab:centering_control}). The hybrid KS pass rate stayed
at $74.4\%$ because its paths were already centered; the
JumpHMM-on-residuals rate rose from $75.8\%$ to $78.4\%$, and block
bootstrap rose from $73.5\%$ to $76.7\%$. Both residual methods
also had higher AD pass rates and lower Wasserstein distances than
hybrid composition after centering. The control brought every
method's median intercept error to $0.002\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ while
leaving variance, recovered loading, $R^2$, and excess kurtosis
unchanged. A separate training diagnostic found that emission fallback rules
also disadvantaged the full-return methods on $39$ assets but affected
none of the residual-fit models (Table~\ref{tab:fallback_sensitivity}; Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-fallback}). Using empirical scales in their
populated zero-return states implied an aggregate hybrid KS gain of
about one percentage point, less than the gap to JumpHMM-on-residuals.
The main comparisons retained the original rule because this
alternative had no holdout validation (Online
Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-fallback}). Together, these
checks supported variance correction over naive reuse without a
second fit, but did not establish an advantage over direct residual
fitting.

\subsection{Holdout performance and VaR coverage}

To test out-of-sample generalization without refitting or look-ahead
bias, we froze the fitted models and SIM parameters before evaluating
them against $416$ complete asset histories from $2025$
(Table~\ref{tab:oos_composition}). Hybrid composition reached an
$87.0\%$ mean cross-ticker KS pass rate, against $83.5\%$ for naive
composition. Its median ratio of synthetic to observed $2025$ variance
was $0.97$, whereas naive composition inflated that ratio to $1.29$.
The JumpHMM-on-residuals method reached an $86.2\%$ KS pass rate and a slightly lower median $W_1$ than hybrid composition. As in
training, these holdout comparisons used each method's native mean
treatment, and no common-centering control was run on the holdout. Higher holdout pass rates did not by themselves establish better
generation: all methods passed more often than in training, and the
gap between hybrid and naive composition narrowed (Tables~\ref{tab:aggregate}
and~\ref{tab:oos_composition}). For every method, pass rates against
matched-length $249$-day training blocks lay between the full-length
training and $2025$ rates, consistent with lower test power in shorter
samples contributing to the rise. Those blocks also used simulated
rather than observed SPY, so the comparison did not isolate sample
length or fully explain the $2025$ increase. Nor did improvement hold
for every asset: about three quarters of hybrid tickers passed more
often in $2025$ than against their matched training blocks and one
quarter passed less often, and every method retained a lower tail of
weakly fitted tickers (Fig.~\ref{fig:oos_composition}).

To test whether the holdout improvement over naive composition
extended to reliable tail thresholds, we estimated per-ticker one-day
left-tail Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds from $5{,}000$ separate
$249$-day paths and counted exceedances on the observed $2025$
histories (Table~\ref{tab:var}). The coverage level
$\alpha=0.95$ has a nominal exceedance rate of $1-\alpha=5\%$ and gave
each asset about $12$ expected exceedances. At that level, hybrid
composition had a mean
exceedance rate of $5.48\%$, closer to nominal than the $4.04\%$ of
naive composition. At $\alpha=0.99$, the hybrid rate of $1.12\%$ and
the naive rate of $0.88\%$ lay equally close to the $1\%$ nominal rate
on opposite sides. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method had rates of
$5.62\%$ and $1.16\%$, close to hybrid composition, and Gaussian SIM
had the largest $99\%$ rate at $1.72\%$. Doubling the threshold pool from
$2{,}500$ to $5{,}000$ paths changed every method's mean exceedance
rate by less than $0.006$ percentage points at either coverage level
(Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-var-ensemble}). The advantage of hybrid over
naive composition thus appeared at the $95\%$ level but not at the
$99\%$ level. Neither comparison established a ranking, because each
asset contributed few exceedances, only about $2$--$3$ expected at the
$99\%$ level, and all assets shared one market period, so their
exceedances were not independent. The hybrid mean exceedance rates were above nominal at both
coverage levels, and this descriptive check could not confirm
threshold calibration. The
cross-asset standard deviations of the exceedance rates described how
realized rates varied among assets; they did not test miscalibration
or validate coverage in other periods.

\subsection{Jump-enabled multi-asset composition}
\label{sec:results-jump-composition}

Transferring SPY's jump settings improved temporal fit in training
but worsened it in the $2025$ holdout
(Table~\ref{tab:jump_ablation}, where hybrid composition is labeled
Corrected). We compared jumps disabled, market-only jumps, and
market-plus-asset jumps under naive and hybrid composition, without
per-asset tuning. This experiment simulated the market in both
windows and used each market path's variance in the correction, so
its baseline differed from the six-method comparisons. Its ACF-MAE
was calculated for each path and then averaged, unlike the
single-asset error calculated from the ensemble-average
autocorrelation. Under hybrid composition, market-only jumps reduced
the training $25$-lag ACF-MAE by $5.3\%$; the $95\%$ Monte Carlo
interval excluded zero. The KS pass rate fell by $0.3$ percentage
points, with an interval that included zero
(Table~\ref{tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty}). Adding asset jumps
reduced ACF-MAE by $14.6\%$ relative to no jumps but lowered the KS
pass rate from $73.9\%$ to $61.7\%$, a much larger marginal-fit
cost than for SPY, whose settings were tuned. In the holdout,
market-only and market-plus-asset jumps instead raised the $25$-lag
ACF-MAE by $2.7\%$ and $6.9\%$. Both $95\%$ Monte Carlo
intervals excluded zero, and the $60$-lag errors also rose
(Tables~\ref{tab:jump_ablation}
and~\ref{tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty}). Holdout KS pass rates
changed little with market-only jumps; with both market and asset
jumps, they fell by about $1.5$ percentage points relative to no jumps. Across all settings
and both windows, the mean ratio of composed to generator variance
under hybrid composition remained within $0.05\%$ of one. This
aggregate included the trackers, whose branch targeted $R^2$ rather
than generator variance, and did not establish preservation for every
asset. Only $2.2\%$ of simulated one-year market paths contained an
episode (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-jump-composition}), limiting evidence
about market jumps; the single observed holdout year also could not
establish performance in other market periods.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}

We examined how the branch-selection threshold
$R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$ assigned assets to branches in the observed
universe and whether any value of the variance floor $f$ triggered
clipping (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sim-derivations}). The
$R^2$-preserving branch applied only to QQQ and SPYG, whose hybrid
compositions passed every KS test in training. The highest-$R^2$ asset
outside these trackers, the individual stock BLK, fell about $0.16$
below the $0.80$ threshold, so the assignment did not depend on a
narrow margin. Across the explored thresholds from $0.70$ to $0.90$ at
the default floor, only the QQQ assignment changed, moving to the
variance-preserving branch at $0.90$. At the default threshold, no
value of $f$ from $0.05$ to $0.30$ caused any asset to be clipped. The
empirical evaluation of the $R^2$-preserving branch was therefore
limited to two assets, and the observed market variance never
activated clipping.

A synthetic Gaussian check supplemented the two real assets using
the $R^2$-preserving branch: across the tested grid, median recovered
loadings and $R^2$ differed from their supplied targets by at most
$0.0011$ and $0.0017$, respectively (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sim-derivations}).
Each reference series combined the observed training SPY path with an
independent Gaussian residual whose variance gave the target $R^2$
under Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2-target}. The composer received the true
loading and $R^2$, a second independent Gaussian draw, and the target
residual variance, which made the residual scale in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2-rescale} exactly one. Every composed series passed
the KS comparison with its reference, but their shared SPY path made
the samples dependent, so the pass rates were descriptive rather than
calibrated tests. The grid checked implementation and finite-sample
accuracy under this restricted construction; it did not exercise
full-return HMM fitting, non-unit residual scaling, or simulated
market paths.

Increasing market volatility activated clipping for most assets,
although no path clipped under the observed market variance
(Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sim-derivations}). A larger market variance raises the market
loading ratio $\rho_i$, the market's share of the target variance;
on the variance-preserving branch, clipping begins when
$\rho_i>1-f$. To measure when clipping activates, we
multiplied deviations of the observed training SPY path from its mean
by $\gamma\in\{1,2,3\}$, which preserved the market mean and
multiplied its standard deviation by $\gamma$. We then recomposed all
$423$ assets with the scaled path's variance in the correction,
holding the fitted marginals, intercepts, and calibrated loadings
fixed (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-sim-derivations}). Clipping applied to no composed path
at $\gamma=1$ and to $76.4\%$ and $95.2\%$ of composed paths at
$\gamma=2$ and $3$. Because clipped paths retained their fitted
intercepts while the market mean stayed unchanged and nonzero, their
means shifted by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$ by
construction. The stress test therefore characterized when clipping
activated and how it moved asset means; it did not evaluate fit under
the scaled markets.
