Synthetic multi-asset equity data must reproduce each asset's return distribution and its relationship with the market. Reusing a generator fitted to full asset returns creates a problem: adding its draws to a market factor counts market variance twice. We derived a correction that centers and rescales each draw over a fixed horizon before adding the market factor, allowing reuse without fitting a second generator to regression residuals. We tested the correction on $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset United States equity and exchange-traded-fund universe, using hidden Markov generators with heavy-tailed emissions. The corrected paths retained heavy tails and recovered the calibrated market loadings with low error. On $416$ complete asset histories held out from $2025$, the correction improved the mean Kolmogorov--Smirnov pass rate over naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one. Its one-day left-tail $99%$ Value-at-Risk exceedance rate, using thresholds pooled across separate simulated paths, was $1.12%$, compared with $1.16%$ for the residual-fit hidden Markov comparator and a nominal rate of $1%$. We also tested a jump-duration mechanism that extended visits to extreme-return states and improved volatility clustering for the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY. The multi-asset correction remained effective with jumps enabled, but transferring the SPY jump settings improved temporal fit in training and worsened it in the $2025$ holdout. The method provides a way to reuse fitted asset generators for daily-fit comparisons. Its zero-sum residual constraint removes terminal residual uncertainty, and unmodeled dependence among asset-specific residuals can lead to overstated rebalancing returns. Code, cached inputs, result summaries, and instructions for fitting the per-asset models accompany the paper.

Researchers and practitioners use synthetic equity paths to test risk models, evaluate portfolio strategies, and examine market scenarios beyond those observed in a historical sample. A multi-asset simulator must represent both the behavior of each asset and the relationships among assets. Factor models link many assets through a shared market path, a time series of market returns. The simplest of these, the single-index model, separates each asset's return into an intercept, a market component scaled by the asset's fitted loading, and a zero-mean asset-specific residual. A synthetic asset path can then be composed by adding a simulated residual to the intercept and market component. If single-asset generators have already been fitted to each asset's full returns, their paths might be reused as residuals. Each generator, however, already represents its asset's total variance, including the part driven by the market. Adding its paths to the market component therefore counts market variance a second time. Subtracting each path's mean removes its mean shift but leaves this excess variance. Fitting a second generator to each asset's regression residuals avoids the problem but repeats the fitting for every asset. Reusing the existing generators would avoid that second fit, provided their paths could be transformed to supply only the residual contribution. The question is whether such a transformation can control the mean and variance of the composed paths while retaining the generator's heavy tails and volatility clustering.

The transformation is useful only if each generator reproduces the behavior of its own asset. We illustrate the features a generator must capture with the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY, which also supplied the market path in our multi-asset experiments. Its $2014$--$2024$ returns show heavy tails and volatility clustering (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]). Most returns lie close to zero, but extreme values occur much more often than a Gaussian model predicts (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]a,b), a widely documented feature of financial returns. Raw returns show little correlation across days, while absolute returns remain positively correlated across many lags (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]c,d). Large and small absolute returns therefore cluster in time, a property that synthetic financial data often miss even when their return histogram appears realistic.

A hidden Markov model (HMM) offers an interpretable way to represent both features: it draws returns from state-dependent distributions and uses transitions between states to represent temporal behavior. Each state in the model used here has a heavy-tailed Student-$t$ return distribution. The next state depends only on the current state, not on earlier returns. Fitted to SPY, it leaves states with the most negative or positive mean returns quickly, and the autocorrelation of its absolute returns decays faster than in the data. A duration mechanism that occasionally keeps drawing from extreme-return states for consecutive days can slow that decay. Adding the market component, however, changes the distribution and temporal behavior of the composed returns. An improvement in a single-asset generator must therefore be checked again after composition.

In this study, we developed a variance correction to reuse full-return generators in a single-index model without counting market variance twice. The correction centered and rescaled each simulated path to remove the mean shift and account for the variance supplied by the market component (Fig. [fig:preservation]). We applied it to heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators for the $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset universe, using $2014$--$2024$ for fitting and $2025$ as a holdout. We compared the correction with naive composition, which centered the paths without rescaling, with Gaussian residuals, and with generators fitted directly to regression residuals. The correction improved agreement with observed return distributions over naive composition while retaining heavy tails in training (Table [tab:aggregate]). With all fits held fixed, the correction also improved distributional fit over naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one on the $416$ assets with complete holdout histories (Table [tab:oos_composition]). The residual-fit hidden Markov generator and the correction gave similar agreement with observed return distributions in both periods. Without the duration mechanism, the correction left absolute-return autocorrelation error essentially unchanged from naive composition (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). Transferring SPY's duration settings to the composed paths improved their autocorrelation agreement in training but worsened it in the holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). The correction imposed a zero residual sum over each simulated path, removing cumulative residual uncertainty at the path endpoint. Neither reuse nor residual fitting modeled dependence among different assets' residuals, which portfolio evaluation also requires. These results support reuse as a practical option for daily-return comparisons when full-return generators already exist.

Single-asset return models treat changing volatility, market regimes, and extreme moves in different ways. Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) and generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models allow conditional variance to change over time. Even with Gaussian innovations, the changing variance can produce an unconditional return distribution with excess kurtosis. Heavy-tailed innovations can improve the tail fit when Gaussian GARCH understates the frequency of extreme returns. Stochastic-volatility models make volatility a latent process. Jump-diffusion models add discontinuous price moves, but a jump component alone does not explain persistent volatility. Markov-switching ARCH and GARCH models combine discrete regimes with changing conditional variance. Hidden-state models represent returns with a small number of market regimes. In a hidden Markov model (HMM), each latent state has a separate return distribution. Gaussian-emission HMMs can reproduce heavy tails and weak raw-return autocorrelation, but may still miss volatility clustering. Hidden semi-Markov models allow more flexible state durations and can improve volatility persistence. An HMM does not create a jump process by itself. Rare extreme moves must come from the state-dependent return distributions or from a transition rule designed for such events. Deep generative models learn return distributions and temporal features without specifying a small set of market regimes or a parametric return process. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are one class of deep generative model. Quant GANs have reproduced several financial time-series features, including volatility clustering and leverage effects. Sig-Wasserstein GANs use path signatures to learn features that depend on the order of observations. In contrast, evaluations of GAN paths, including paths from the time-series generative adversarial network (TimeGAN), have found too little autocorrelation in absolute returns. The conflicting results show that flexible architectures do not guarantee a match to financial stylized facts. Model design, training, and evaluation all matter.

Multi-asset generation also requires a model of cross-asset dependence. Multivariate GARCH models estimate time-varying covariance matrices. The dynamic conditional correlation model reduces the number of parameters by separating individual volatility models from correlation dynamics. Copulas separate each asset's return distribution from the dependence model. Dynamic copulas allow cross-asset dependence to change over time, while vine copulas provide flexible high-dimensional constructions. Factor models reduce dimension by linking each asset to a small number of shared factors. Shared-state HMMs and multivariate GANs can also generate joint market moves. A standard factor model can resample historical residuals or fit a separate residual generator for every asset. This paper addresses a narrower problem. We start with single-asset generators already fitted to full returns and ask whether they can be reused in a factor model. The relevant comparison is with methods fitted directly to regression residuals: those methods remove the market component before fitting, whereas reuse adjusts an existing full-return draw during composition. Neither approach specifies cross-asset residual dependence unless that dependence is modeled separately.

For each asset, the hidden Markov generator divided the range of growth rates, which we also call returns, into $N=100$ ordered intervals and treated the interval that contained a day's return as that day's state (Table [tab:hyperparams]). We write $t$ for the day index $j$ and omit the ticker index $i$ when discussing one asset. At each simulated time step, the model first selected a state and then drew a return from that state's distribution (Fig. [fig:model_architecture]). We represented the model as $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathbf{T}, \mathbf{E}, \bar{\pi})$ . The hidden state $S_t \in \mathcal{S} = \{1,\dots,N\}$ identifies the interval on trading day $t$, and the observation space $\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}$ contains the excess growth rates $G_t$ of Eq. Equation (eq:growth_rate). The transition matrix $\mathbf{T}$ sets the probabilities of moving between states, $\mathbf{E}$ gives the return distribution within each state, and $\bar{\pi}$ gives the initial state probabilities. We ordered the states from the most negative to the most positive returns and placed their boundaries at the quantiles of a Laplace distribution, fitted to the asset's in-sample returns, at the equally spaced probability levels $k/N$. We used the Laplace distribution because its sharp peak matches the concentration of small price movements in Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]a . Its quantile function also has a closed form, so the boundaries for all $424$ tickers required no iterative fitting. Specifically, $Q_k = F_L^{-1}(k/N;\, \mu_L, b_L)$, where $F_L^{-1}$ is the Laplace quantile function and $\mu_L$ and $b_L$ are the fitted location and scale. We set $Q_0=-\infty$ and $Q_N=+\infty$ and assigned return $G_t$ to state $k$ when $Q_{k-1}\le G_t<Q_k$. Because each training return determines its state, the fitted state sequence is known; the states are hidden only in simulated paths, where the model generates the state sequence and reports only the returns.

The within-state emission followed a location-scale Student-$t$ distribution: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} G_t \mid S_t = k \;\sim\; \mu_k + \sigma_k \cdot t_{\nu}, \qquad \nu = 5,  \end{equation} where $t_\nu$ denotes a standard Student-$t$ random variable with $\nu$ degrees of freedom. For observations assigned to state $k$, we used the sample mean for $\mu_k$ and the sample standard deviation for $\sigma_k$, subject to two fallback rules. A state with fewer than two observations used the mean and standard deviation of that asset's full training series. A state with at least two observations and sample standard deviation below $10^{-12}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ retained its state mean but used the full-series standard deviation as its scale. These exceptions kept emission parameters defined but could broaden states containing repeated returns (Table [tab:fallback_assets]); neither applied to SPY, whose states all held many observations. The same rules applied to the generators that the multi-asset comparison fitted to regression residuals. A standard $t_\nu$ has variance $\nu/(\nu-2)$, so $\sigma_k$ is a scale parameter rather than the emission standard deviation. At $\nu=5$, each state's emission standard deviation is $\sigma_k\sqrt{5/3}$, larger than the sample standard deviation of the returns assigned to that state. The emission is not truncated to the state's interval, so a simulated return can fall outside it. We selected $\nu = 5$ from a sensitivity sweep and used $\nu = \infty$, which gives Normal emissions, as a baseline; Table [tab:hyperparams] gives the explored range. For SPY, holding the rest of the model fixed, the Student-$t$ emission raised simulated kurtosis toward the observed value and raised the Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) and Anderson--Darling (AD) pass rates relative to the Normal emission (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]). A pass rate is the percentage of simulated paths whose two-sample test against the observed returns gave $p>0.05$.

We estimated the transition matrix $\mathbf{T}$ by counting observed transitions between consecutive states and dividing each row by its total count. A state with no observed outgoing transition used a uniform row, $\mathbf{T}_{kj}=1/N$ for every destination state $j$. Because the training state sequence is known, these row frequencies are the maximum-likelihood estimates, and counting avoided the initialization sensitivity of iterative expectation-maximization. We obtained the initial-state distribution $\bar{\pi}$ by solving $\bar{\pi}\mathbf{T}=\bar{\pi}$ with $\sum_k\bar{\pi}_k=1$. Because $\mathbf{T}$ was estimated by counting, $\bar{\pi}$ equals the vector of in-sample state frequencies up to end effects, so the model's stationary growth-rate distribution is the frequency-weighted mixture of the state emission distributions. The counted probability of remaining in the same state was low (Fig. [fig:model_internals] in Online Appendix [sec:supp-transitions]), but volatility clustering requires the chain to stay in the tail region, not in one state. Counting reproduces the observed frequencies of consecutive state pairs. At longer lags, however, the chain's absolute-return autocorrelation decays geometrically at a rate set by the counted matrix, much faster than the slow decay in Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]d. The counted chain therefore forgets within a few steps whether it was in the tails and cannot reproduce the observed volatility clustering. Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator] derives the fitted generator's stationary law and autocorrelation structure and quantifies both this rapid mixing and the autocorrelation added by the jump mechanism.

We added jump episodes to extend visits to extreme-return states and call the resulting generator the hidden Markov model with jumps (HMM-WJ). When no jump episode was active, a jump was triggered with probability $\epsilon$ at each step, and its duration was drawn as $K\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the mean episode length. A draw of $K=0$ produced no episode, and the chain made an ordinary transition. For $K>0$, the episode consisted of $K$ \emph{forced tail steps}, beginning with the step that triggered it. At each forced step, the model replaced the ordinary transition by first choosing one of two tail sets and then drawing a state uniformly from that set. The set $\mathcal{S}_{-}=\{1,\dots,N_{\rm tail}\}$ contained the most negative states, and $\mathcal{S}_{+}=\{N-N_{\rm tail}+1,\dots,N\}$ contained the most positive states. The model chose the negative set with probability $p_{\rm neg}$ and the positive set otherwise. Because $p_{\rm neg}$ is slightly above one half, negative extreme returns occurred slightly more often than positive ones. The choice was repeated at each forced step, so one episode can contain returns of both signs. Table [tab:hyperparams] lists $\epsilon$, $\lambda$, $p_{\rm neg}$, and $N_{\rm tail}$; we tuned $\epsilon$ and $\lambda$ and fixed $p_{\rm neg}$ and $N_{\rm tail}$. Jump-diffusion models use Poisson arrivals to add instantaneous price jumps. Our model instead uses the Poisson distribution for the episode length, and an episode is a sustained visit to the tail states rather than a single price move. Setting $\epsilon=0$ removes the mechanism; we call this variant the hidden Markov model with no jumps (HMM-NJ). Two closed-form approximations describe how much of a path, and how many paths, the mechanism affects (Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]). Forced steps occupy a long-run fraction of approximately $\epsilon\lambda/(1+\epsilon\lambda)$ of all steps. A path of $T$ steps contains at least one episode with probability approximately $1 - e^{-\epsilon T}$, so $\epsilon$ sets how many paths contain an episode, while $\lambda$ sets how long an episode lasts. The jump-augmented process is Markov only in the pair formed by the state and the number of remaining forced steps. The solved $\bar{\pi}$ is therefore the exact stationary distribution of the ordinary chain but only an approximate stationary start for the jump-augmented process. The full simulation procedure appears in Algorithm [alg:hmmwj]. Each step followed $\mathbf{T}$ or continued a jump episode and then drew a return from the selected state's emission distribution.

We tuned the jump probability $\epsilon$ and mean duration $\lambda$ on the volume-weighted SPY training series to match its absolute-return autocorrelation and excess kurtosis; the multi-asset comparisons either disabled jumps or transferred the SPY values to the closing-price generators (Table [tab:hyperparams]). A path with no episode follows HMM-NJ, so its autocorrelation and kurtosis errors do not depend on $(\epsilon,\lambda)$. We scored only \emph{jump-active} paths, those containing at least one episode, so the objective measures how well an episode reproduces the observed statistics, not how often episodes occur. At each point of a grid of $(\epsilon,\lambda)$ values, we simulated $200$ independent paths of $2,766$ trading days. We minimized an objective that averaged, over the jump-active paths, the squared errors in the autocorrelation function (ACF) of absolute returns through lag $L$ plus a weighted squared error in excess kurtosis: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} J(\epsilon, \lambda) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_J|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_J} \left[ \sum_{\tau=1}^{L} \left( \mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau) - \mathrm{ACF}_{r}(\tau) \right)^2 + w_\kappa \left( \kappa_{\rm obs} - \kappa_{r} \right)^2 \right],  \end{equation} where $\mathcal{R}_J$ is the set of jump-active simulated paths, $\mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ and $\kappa_{\rm obs}$ are the empirical absolute-growth autocorrelation at lag $\tau$ and excess kurtosis, $\mathrm{ACF}_{r}(\tau)$ and $\kappa_r$ are the corresponding values for path $r$, and $w_\kappa$ sets the relative weight of the kurtosis error. When $\epsilon$ is small, as at the selected value, most jump-active paths contain a single episode, whose forced steps make up a fraction of about $\lambda/T$ of the path. The episode adds a slowly decaying component to the absolute-return autocorrelation, and $\lambda$ sets both its height and the number of lags over which it persists. The kurtosis penalty limits $\lambda$, because forced steps make tail returns common within a path rather than rare; they raise the variance proportionally more than the fourth moment and so lower the path kurtosis. The parameter $\epsilon$ acts mainly through the share of paths that contain an episode, which the restriction to jump-active paths removes. The objective therefore constrains $\epsilon$ only weakly, and the selected $\epsilon$ lay at the lower edge of its search range (Table [tab:hyperparams]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]). The search ranges, $w_\kappa$, $L$, and the remaining hyperparameters are reported in Table [tab:hyperparams]. We fixed the selected hyperparameters before running the generator comparisons and the holdout evaluation.

Beyond the Normal-emission variant, we compared HMM-WJ with two benchmarks that model persistence differently. A hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla lengthens state visits through explicit dwell-time distributions instead of jump episodes (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-emission-hsmm]). The Normal-emission comparison isolated the effect of heavy-tailed emissions, and the HSMM comparison contrasted explicit dwell times with the jump-duration mechanism, although the HSMM also used $8$ states rather than $100$ and so differed in more than its duration model. To compare the hidden Markov generator with a neural autoregressive model, we included a gated recurrent unit (GRU) benchmark (Table [tab:model_comparison]). The network took a $50$-return input window and had two recurrent layers of $64$ hidden units each. We standardized the training series with its mean and standard deviation, and separate output heads predicted the mean and log variance of the next standardized return. We fitted the network on training SPY only, selected weights by training loss, and sampled recursively from the predicted Gaussian distributions. Online Appendix [sec:supp-gru] specifies the optimizer, stopping rule, initialization, and recorded computational environment. We scored all single-asset generators, including these benchmarks, by their KS and AD pass rates and by the mean absolute error between the observed and the ensemble-average simulated absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE), over lags $1$--$252$ in training and $1$--$248$ in the $249$-day holdout (Eq. Equation (eq:acf-ensemble-error) in Online Appendix [sec:supp-validation]).

The single-asset model generated each ticker separately and did not specify cross-asset dependence. For the multi-asset model, we added a shared market path as a common factor, using the single-index model (SIM) of Sharpe. We write $g_i(t)$ for the growth rate $G_{i,t}$ of asset $i$ on days $t=1,\dots,T$. The horizon $T$ is $2,766$ days in the training comparisons and $249$ days in the holdout. The same symbols denote observed paths when we estimate parameters and synthetic paths when we compose them: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} g_i(t) \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta_i\, g_m(t) \;+\; \eps_i(t), \qquad t = 1,\dots,T.  \end{equation} Here $\alpha_i$ is the intercept for asset $i$, $g_m$ is the growth-rate path of the market, SPY, $\beta_i$ is the asset's market loading, and $\eps_i$ is its asset-specific residual path. We use $\eps_i$ for the residual path and $\epsilon$ for the jump probability in the single-asset model. We estimated the intercept and market loading $(\alpha_i,\beta_i)$ from observed data by ordinary least squares (OLS). The choice of $\eps_i$ determined the composition method. A standard SIM draws independent Gaussian residuals with the variance estimated by OLS. These Gaussian residuals preserve the fitted market relationship but lack the heavy tails, and with jumps enabled the volatility clustering, that the single-asset generator produces. We called the Gaussian-residual method \emph{Gaussian SIM} and used it as one of the compared methods.

\paragraph{Naive full-path composition.}

Using a full synthetic growth-rate path $\tg_i$ drawn from the asset's own single-asset generator, the \emph{generator draw}, directly as the residual would give: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} g_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i\, g_m + \tg_i. \end{equation} The fitted intercept and market term already set the conditional mean of asset $i$, while the generator draw generally has $\E[\tg_i]\ne 0$. Equation Equation (eq:naive-composition) therefore has conditional mean $\alpha_i+\E[\tg_i]+\beta_i g_m$, not $\alpha_i+\beta_i g_m$. We therefore centered every generator draw before composition, subtracting its realized path mean: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \overline{\tg}_i \equiv \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tg_i(t), \qquad \tg_i^{\mathrm c}(t) \equiv \tg_i(t)-\overline{\tg}_i.  \end{equation} Therefore, $T^{-1}\sum_t\tg_i^{\mathrm c}(t)=0$ exactly. Subtracting the realized rather than the population mean makes the centering exact on every path. Subtracting a constant leaves both the sample variance and the sample covariance with $g_m$ unchanged. We used the centered construction $g_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i g_m+\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$, which corrects the mean but not the variance, as the \emph{naive} benchmark in all comparisons. The sample variance of each draw, $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2=\Var(\tg_i)=\Var(\tg_i^{\mathrm c})$, is the \emph{generator variance}; it is recomputed for every draw. It already represents the asset's total variance, so adding the market term adds market variance a second time. During composition, $g_m$ was the observed SPY path in the training comparison and a path simulated from SPY's closing-price generator in the holdout and jump comparisons, with one market path per replication shared by all assets, and in both cases it was independent of the asset draws. The covariance of a centered draw with the market path is zero in population and close to zero on a long realized path. We approximated the naive composed variance accordingly: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \Var(g_i) \;\approx\; \beta_i^2\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_{\gen,i}^2, \qquad \Cov(\tg_i^{\mathrm c},g_m)\approx 0,  \end{equation} where $\sigma_m^2=\Var(g_m)$ is the sample variance of the market path used in composition. The naive composed variance therefore exceeds the generator variance by $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2$, an excess that grows with the square of the market loading.

\paragraph{Variance correction.}

We set $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ as the target total variance for asset $i$. The market term contributed $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2$, so the residual term could contribute only the remaining variance. We defined $s_i$ as the nonnegative asset-specific residual scale, set $\eps_i=s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$, and required $\Var(\beta_i g_m+s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c})=\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$. Under the zero-covariance assumption, solving for $s_i$ gives the correction: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \boxed{\;s_i^2 \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}\;}  \end{equation} We call the market term's share of the target variance the \emph{market loading ratio}: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \rho_i \;\equiv\; \frac{\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2},  \end{equation} so that $s_i^2=1-\rho_i$ under the zero-covariance assumption. At $\rho_i=1$, the rule of Eq. Equation (eq:scale) leaves no asset-specific variance; for $\rho_i>1$, it has no real-valued solution.

With the observed variances, $\rho_i$ would equal the coefficient of determination of the regression, which is below one. Because $\rho_i$ instead uses the generator and market-path variances, it can reach or exceed one when the generator variance is low or the market path is more volatile than the observed SPY training series, particularly for assets with large $\beta_i$. We therefore required the asset-specific draw to contribute at least a fraction $f$ of the target variance, the \emph{floor}, and set $\rho_i^{\max}=1-f$. We applied the correction through an \emph{effective loading} $\beta_i^{\eff}$, the loading used in composition, which equals $\beta_i$ unless $\rho_i>\rho_i^{\max}$: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \beta_i^{\eff} \;=\; \begin{cases} \beta_i & \text{if } \rho_i \le \rho_i^{\max} \\[4pt] \sign(\beta_i)\,\sqrt{(1 - f)\,\dfrac{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}{\sigma_m^2}} & \text{if } \rho_i > \rho_i^{\max} \end{cases} \qquad s_i^2 \;=\; \begin{cases} 1 - \rho_i & \text{if } \rho_i \le \rho_i^{\max} \\[4pt] f & \text{if } \rho_i > \rho_i^{\max} \end{cases}  \end{equation} We call the second case of Eq. Equation (eq:clip) the \emph{clipped} case. In it, the correction reduced the magnitude of the loading but kept the sign of the calibrated $\beta_i$. We used $f=0.10$, so the asset-specific draw contributed at least $10%$ of the target variance for every asset under this rule (Table [tab:hyperparams]). We retained the fitted intercept $\alpha_i$ when clipping changed the loading. Because the scaled residual had zero sample mean, the composed mean was $\overline{g}_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i^{\eff}\overline{g}_m$, where $\overline{g}_m$ is the mean of the market path. Clipping therefore changed the mean by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$ relative to the calibrated linear mean on that same market path. The clipped case thus kept the intercept and the target variance and set the residual share to exactly $f$, but changed the loading and, unless $\overline{g}_m=0$, the mean. Online Appendix [sec:supp-residual-choice] examines the substitution of a full-return draw for the residual: it derives what the construction preserves exactly and how much of the generator's excess kurtosis survives composition as $\rho_i$ grows.

\paragraph{High-$R^2$ index trackers.}

The coefficient of determination $R^2_{i,\real}$, from the OLS regression of the observed asset on observed SPY, measures the fraction of the asset's variance explained by the market. The rule of Eq. Equation (eq:clip) targets total generator variance, not $R^2_{i,\real}$. Under the zero-covariance assumption, the composed $R^2$ under that rule equals $\rho_i$, capped at $1-f$ by the floor. It therefore cannot exceed $1-f$, and it matches $R^2_{i,\real}$ only when $\sigma_m^2/\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ equals the ratio of the observed SPY variance to the observed asset variance. Index-tracking funds, such as the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ and the S&P 500 growth fund SPYG, have $R^2$ against SPY close to or above the cap $1-f$, so that rule could not reliably reproduce their co-movement with the market. We therefore used two branches: the rule of Eq. Equation (eq:clip), the \emph{variance-preserving branch}, for most assets, and a separate \emph{$R^2$-preserving branch} when $R^2_{i,\real}$ was at least $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$. We set $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}=0.80$, which lies in a wide gap of the observed $R^2$ distribution between the index trackers and all other assets in our universe (Fig. [fig:r2_distribution]). This branch keeps $\beta_i^{\eff}=\beta_i$ and uses the same market-path variance $\sigma_m^2$ as the variance-preserving branch. We computed the residual variance required to recover $R^2_{i,\real}$ under the zero-covariance assumption: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \boxed{\;\sigma_{\eps,\mathrm{target}}^2 \;=\; \beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2 \cdot \frac{1 - R^2_{i,\real}}{R^2_{i,\real}}\;}  \end{equation} We rescaled the generator draw to the target residual variance: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \eps_i \;=\; \left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{\eps,\mathrm{target}}^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}}\,\right)\tg_i^{\mathrm c}.  \end{equation} This branch preserves $R^2_{i,\real}$ rather than the generator variance: the composed variance becomes $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2/R^2_{i,\real}$. The scale factor in Eq. Equation (eq:r2-rescale) exceeds one when the generator variance is below the target residual variance; the composed variance then exceeds the generator variance.

\paragraph{Complete composition rule.}

The complete rule has three outcomes, recorded by the branch flag $b_i$ in Algorithm [alg:hybrid]. Index trackers used the $R^2$-preserving branch; all other assets used the variance-preserving branch, with the calibrated loading or, in the clipped case, a reduced loading. Each composed path is then $g_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i^{\eff}g_m+\eps_i$. We call this complete rule the \emph{hybrid} composition, the label used in the tables and figures, and all results reported for the variance correction refer to it. Membership in the $R^2$-preserving branch depends only on $R^2_{i,\real}$ and is fixed for each asset. Everything else is computed per draw: because $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ is recomputed for each draw, so are $s_i$, $\rho_i$, and $\beta_i^{\eff}$, and one asset can be clipped in some draws and not in others. Because the draws are independent of the market path, OLS estimates from composed paths converge, as the horizon grows, to the intercept $\alpha_i$ and the effective loading $\beta_i^{\eff}$, which equals $\beta_i$ unless the draw is clipped. The variance target and the loading recovery are exact only when the sample covariance between the draw and the market path is zero; on a finite path, they deviate by that covariance term (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). Because centering and scaling fix the residual sum, every branch imposes a zero residual sum over the horizon $T$, which is stronger than a zero population mean. For a fixed market path and effective loading, the terminal cumulative log return therefore has no asset-specific residual uncertainty, although daily and intermediate returns still vary. In the clipped case, the effective loading depends on the variances of each draw, so it can vary between draws and produce terminal variation through the market term. Because centering and scaling use statistics of the entire draw, the construction also depends on the chosen horizon. We therefore evaluated it as a constrained finite-horizon construction for daily distributional fit and recovery of the market loading; it does not preserve cumulative residual uncertainty.

The construction links assets only through same-day market returns. A large market move can therefore affect several assets together, but the rule does not separately fit lead--lag effects or a common volatility factor in the residuals. Algorithm [alg:hybrid] also allows an optional rank reorder that adds residual dependence across assets; the six-method comparison did not use it. The reorder draws one joint vector per day from a copula fitted to the observed OLS residuals and permutes each asset's residual values over days so that their ranks over time match the ranks of that asset's copula coordinate. The reorder imposes cross-asset residual dependence and keeps each path's values and variance, but it replaces the generator's day-to-day ordering and with it any volatility clustering in the draw.

[DISPLAY] \begin{algorithm}[t] \caption{Hybrid single-index model (SIM) composition with variance correction.}  \small \begin{algorithmic}[1] \REQUIRE Nonconstant $g_m,\tg_i\in\R^T$, $i=1,\dots,N$; each $\tg_i$ is independent of $g_m$. \REQUIRE Parameters $(\alpha_i,\beta_i,R^2_{i,\real})$; floor $f\in(0,1)$; threshold $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}\in(0,1]$. \ENSURE Composed paths $g_i\in\R^T$ and branch flags $b_i$. \STATE $\sigma_m^2 \gets \Var(g_m)$. \FOR{each asset $i = 1,\dots,N$} \STATE $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2 \gets \Var(\tg_i)$; $\tg_i^{\mathrm c} \gets \tg_i-\overline{\tg}_i$. \IF{$R^2_{i,\real} \ge R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$} \STATE $v_i \gets \beta_i^2\sigma_m^2(1-R^2_{i,\real})/R^2_{i,\real}$. \STATE $s_i \gets \sqrt{v_i/\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}$; $\beta_i^{\eff} \gets \beta_i$; $b_i\gets\textsc{r2-preserve}$. \ELSE \STATE $\rho_i \gets \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 / \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ \IF{$\rho_i \le 1 - f$} \STATE $s_i \gets \sqrt{1-\rho_i}$; $\beta_i^{\eff}\gets\beta_i$; $b_i\gets\textsc{variance}$. \ELSE \STATE $s_i\gets\sqrt{f}$; $\beta_i^{\eff}\gets\sign(\beta_i) \sqrt{(1-f)\sigma_{\gen,i}^2/\sigma_m^2}$. \STATE $b_i\gets\textsc{clipped}$. \ENDIF \ENDIF \STATE $\eps_i \gets s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$. \ENDFOR \STATE \textit{Optional:} jointly rank-reorder $\{\eps_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$. \FOR{each asset $i = 1,\dots,N$} \STATE $g_i \gets \alpha_i + \beta_i^{\eff}\, g_m + \eps_i$ \hfill \COMMENT{Eq. Equation (eq:sim-composition)} \ENDFOR \end{algorithmic} \end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}

To test variance correction against alternative residual models, we compared six composition methods on $424$ United States equities and exchange-traded funds from Polygon.io (Table [tab:aggregate]). Each ticker had $2,767$ daily closing prices from January $2014$ through December $2024$, yielding $2,766$ growth rates; this complete-case selection conditioned the analysis on survival and data coverage. We fitted the same hidden Markov generator to each asset, including SPY (Table [tab:hyperparams]; $r_f=0$), and regressed the other $423$ assets on observed SPY to estimate $\alpha_i$, $\beta_i$, $R^2_{i,\real}$, and residual variance. All generators had jumps disabled to isolate composition from jump calibration. The naive and hybrid methods shared a centered full-return draw, with variance correction applied only by the hybrid method (Algorithm [alg:hybrid]). Four alternatives generated residuals from a Gaussian distribution, a residual-fit hidden Markov model (labeled JumpHMM-on-residuals), a stationary block bootstrap, or a GARCH(1,1)-$t$ model, and added them to $\alpha_i+\beta_i g_m$ without centering or rescaling. Comparator fitting and eligibility rules are given in Online Appendix [sec:supp-composition-evaluation]; $30$ non-stationary GARCH fits left $393$ eligible assets for that method. Using observed SPY as the common market path, we ran $100$ replications per asset with seed $1234$ and pooled training scores across tickers. We measured factor recovery by the absolute OLS errors $|\hat\alpha-\alpha_i|$, $|\hat\beta-\beta_i|$, and $|\hat R^2-R^2_{i,\real}|$, and naive and hybrid variance preservation by the composed-to-generator variance ratio. Distributional scores were KS and AD pass rates, Wasserstein-1 distance, excess kurtosis, and the Hill index of absolute returns (Online Appendix [sec:supp-validation]). Because only naive and hybrid forced the residual mean to zero, a paired centering control on the common $393$ assets separated this constraint from model fitting (Table [tab:centering_control]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-centering-control]).

To test performance without refitting, we froze all $2014$--$2024$ fits and evaluated $100$ simulated paths per asset against the $249$ growth rates observed in $2025$ (Table [tab:oos_composition]). Complete holdout histories were available for $416$ assets, including $386$ with accepted GARCH fits. Each replication used one simulated $249$-day no-jump SPY path shared across asset compositions. The correction used the observed training market variance in Eqs. Equation (eq:scale)--Equation (eq:r2-rescale), while each asset's generator variance remained draw-specific. Consequently, variation in simulated market variance could move composed variance away from the generator target even at zero sample cross-covariance. Observed $2025$ returns were used only for scoring. We repeated the KS, AD, Wasserstein-1, and kurtosis evaluations and measured variance for all six methods relative to observed $2025$ variance. Because goodness-of-fit tests have less power on short samples, we also compared the same simulated paths with randomly selected contiguous $249$-day blocks of observed training returns as a length-matched training reference. We averaged replications within ticker before summarizing across tickers, giving each asset equal weight.

To estimate one-day Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds without relying on a single short path, we pooled daily log returns, $\Delta t$ times the growth rates, from $5,000$ separately composed $249$-day paths per asset and method (Table [tab:var]). Each replication shared one simulated SPY path across assets and methods and followed the holdout centering and scaling conventions. We fixed the $95%$ and $99%$ coverage thresholds before scoring observed $2025$ returns; no holdout data entered fitting or threshold calibration. These unconditional thresholds describe a uniformly selected day within the $249$-day construction, retain its horizon dependence, and do not remove the zero-sum residual constraint of naive and hybrid paths. We reported mean exceedance rates and cross-ticker standard deviations descriptively, without treating dependent days or assets sharing a market as independent replications. Pool-size convergence and an independent simulation check are reported in Online Appendix [sec:supp-var-ensemble].

\paragraph{Jump-enabled multi-asset comparison.}

To test whether the temporal benefit of the single-asset jump mechanism survived composition, we compared naive and hybrid composition under three jump settings (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). Unlike the six-method comparison, which used observed SPY in training, this comparison simulated the SPY market path in both the $2,766$-day training window and the $249$-day holdout, so a jump setting could apply to the market generator. Jumps were disabled, enabled only in the SPY market generator, or enabled in both SPY and every asset generator. We froze the closing-price hidden Markov generators and SIM calibrations of the six-method comparison. Enabled models received the SPY settings of Table [tab:hyperparams], $(\epsilon,\lambda)=(10^{-4},90)$, $p_{\rm neg}=0.52$, and $N_{\rm tail}=5$, without per-asset tuning. These settings were tuned on volume-weighted SPY prices and applied here to generators fitted to closing prices. At these settings, most paths contain no episode, especially over the $249$-day holdout, so the jump effects are carried by a minority of paths. In both windows and for all three jump settings, the correction used each simulated market path's variance and each asset draw's variance. This differs from the six-method holdout, which fixed the market variance at its training value.

For each jump setting, naive and hybrid composition received the same asset and market draws, and one market path was shared across all assets in each replication. We generated $250$ paths for each of four seeds ($1234$, $2345$, $3456$, and $4567$), giving $1,000$ replications per asset, setting, and method. We used a path-level version of ACF-MAE, computed on each path and then averaged across paths (Eq. Equation (eq:acf-path-error) in Online Appendix [sec:supp-validation]); the single-asset ACF-MAE instead averages the path ACFs before measuring the error. We used lags $1$--$25$, the lag window of the tuning objective, with lags $1$--$60$ as a secondary check, and repeated the marginal and variance diagnostics. The temporal scores of the single-asset and jump comparisons therefore have different estimands and cannot be compared directly. We included all paths, not only jump-active ones, in the summaries; jump-active subsets only described the mechanism (Table [tab:jump_ablation_episodes]). For Monte Carlo uncertainty, we averaged each metric across tickers within each replication, which kept assets together under their shared market path. Jump settings were paired by replication, sharing the draws of any component whose jump setting did not change, and the approximate $95%$ confidence intervals were the mean of the $1,000$ replication-level differences plus or minus $1.96$ standard errors (Table [tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty]). These intervals describe simulation uncertainty conditional on the fitted models and observed histories, not uncertainty across market regimes.


\subsection{Single-asset generator validation}


We first characterized the SPY series and the HMM-WJ calibrated to its training window (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]; Table [tab:descriptive_stats]). The observed series had heavy tails and persistent absolute-return autocorrelation in both the training and holdout windows. Individual raw-return correlations were small, although the Ljung--Box test detected joint serial dependence (Table [tab:descriptive_stats]). Under the counted transition matrix, every state had an expected residence time of only $1$--$2$ steps, and the implied absolute-return autocorrelation decayed to near zero by lag $5$ (Fig. [fig:model_internals]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]). Jump episodes extended visits to the tail states and so provided a mechanism for more persistent volatility (Algorithm [alg:hmmwj]). Calibration against the $2014$--$2024$ history selected mean episode duration $\lambda=90$ and jump probability $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ (Eq. Equation (eq:grid_search); Table [tab:hyperparams]). Because $\epsilon$ mainly sets how many paths contain an episode and the objective scored only jump-active paths, the objective depended only weakly on $\epsilon$, and the search selected its smallest grid value (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]). The duration $\lambda$ was likewise a fitted parameter of the objective, not an estimate of how long observed high-volatility periods last.

We compared the calibrated HMM-WJ with seven alternative generators on the training window, using $1,000$ paths of $2,766$ trading days (Table [tab:model_comparison]; Fig. [fig:model_comparison]). Three alternatives drew independent returns: bootstrap resampling of the training returns with replacement, and Gaussian and Laplace distributions fitted to them. The other four were HMM-NJ, a GARCH(1,1) model with Gaussian innovations, the GRU benchmark, and the HSMM baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla. The HMM-NJ and HMM-WJ models had KS pass rates within about one percentage point of the fitted Laplace baseline but came much closer to the observed kurtosis, which Laplace underestimated at $3.0$ against $7.7$. Both HMM variants had lower Wasserstein-1 distances than Laplace, whereas Laplace had the lowest Hellinger distance among the fitted generators, so the ranking depended on the metric. Bootstrap resampling passed every KS test because it reused the observed returns, while Gaussian sampling passed neither distribution test. The GARCH(1,1) model had the lowest mean absolute error between the observed and ensemble-average absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE) but rarely passed the distribution tests, and the GRU showed the same tradeoff. The HSMM baseline passed only $82%$ of KS tests and underestimated kurtosis by more than one third, and its ACF-MAE equaled the HMM-NJ value despite its flexible state durations. A separate ablation within HMM-WJ isolated the emission distribution: Student-$t$ emissions produced kurtosis of $7.5$, close to the observed $7.7$, whereas Normal ($\nu=\infty$) emissions underestimated it by about $29%$ (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]). Both HMM variants also reproduced the observed skewness of $-0.75$ without a skewness parameter, because the state means and scales estimated from the asymmetric series carried its asymmetry into the mixture (Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]; Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).

Jump episodes traded a small loss of marginal fit for better temporal fit in training (Table [tab:model_comparison]; Fig. [fig:model_comparison]b). About $24%$ of the training-length HMM-WJ paths contained an episode, and paths without one were equivalent to HMM-NJ (Fig. [fig:statistical_validation]). Without episodes, the counted chain mixed so quickly that HMM-NJ had an ACF-MAE close to that of the three independent-draw generators. Across all HMM-WJ paths, ACF-MAE fell from $0.059$ for HMM-NJ to $0.053$, with a small reduction in KS and AD pass rates. The episode share, the fraction of forced steps, and the autocorrelation of jump-active paths agreed with the calculations in Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator] (Tables [tab:supp-generator-quantities] and [tab:supp-acf-prediction]). Because the objective scored only jump-active paths, the one-quarter share was not a calibration target; it followed from $\epsilon$ and the path length. To examine how that share traded marginal against temporal fit, we split $4,000$ simulated HMM-WJ paths into jump-active and no-jump groups and evaluated mixtures of the two, weighted by a jump-active fraction from zero to one (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]). Increasing that fraction lowered autocorrelation error but also lowered KS and AD pass rates, while the Hill upper-tail index remained stable. Kurtosis fell from above the observed value at a fraction of zero to below it at one. Forced steps drew repeatedly from the existing tail states without extending the extreme tail, so they raised the variance more than the fourth moment (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]). At the realized share of about one quarter, the mean path kurtosis of $7.5$ lay slightly below the observed $7.7$ (Table [tab:model_comparison]).

To test whether the HMM results depended on the state partition, we refitted both HMM variants for $N$ from $30$ to $200$ states (Table [tab:sensitivity_n]). Each refit kept five states in each tail set and re-ran the $(\epsilon,\lambda)$ search. The HMM-NJ pass rates changed by less than one percentage point. As $N$ increased, HMM-WJ autocorrelation error fell but so did its AD pass rate. The tail sets that receive forced steps then covered a narrower quantile band. Each forced step therefore drew a more extreme return, which lowered autocorrelation error, while each tail state's emission estimates rested on fewer observations, which lowered the AD pass rate (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sensitivity]). The primary analysis used $N=100$, which kept the HMM-WJ KS pass rate above $98%$ and its AD pass rate within a few percentage points of its best value while capturing part of the autocorrelation improvement. The sweep used the $2,766$-day SPY history alone. It therefore did not establish a state count for the other assets, which also used $N=100$, or for shorter histories, which would leave fewer observations per state.

To test whether the distributional fit held on data not used for fitting, we compared $249$-day paths from the frozen models with the $249$ held-out SPY returns from $2025$ (Table [tab:model_comparison]; Fig. [fig:statistical_validation]). On $249$ observations the tests had less power, so the pass rates of the weaker generators rose sharply and the differences among generators narrowed. The HMM-NJ and HMM-WJ models retained KS pass rates of $96.6%$ and $95.4%$, with kurtosis close to the observed value. At $\epsilon=10^{-4}$, few $249$-day paths contained an episode (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-renewal]), so this comparison mainly tested the no-jump dynamics that both variants share. The fitted Laplace baseline matched or exceeded both HMM variants on the KS and AD pass rates and had equal or smaller Wasserstein-1 and Hellinger distances. Its excess kurtosis of $2.8$, however, remained well below the observed $6.9$, whereas both HMM variants reached $6.2$ (Table [tab:model_comparison]). To check whether the single-asset fit carried over to other stocks, we repeated the fitting and calibration of HMM-WJ for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM on the same training and holdout windows (Table [tab:cross_asset]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-cross-asset]). Training KS pass rates were near $99%$ for all three, but holdout rates ranged from $74.0%$ for JNJ to $97.7%$ for NVDA. These findings supported the HMM variants as sources of heavy-tailed asset paths whose holdout fit varied by asset. On the SPY holdout, their advantage over the Laplace baseline lay in kurtosis rather than in pass rates or distances. The jump mechanism lowered autocorrelation error mainly in training and at a small cost in pass rates. The six-method composition comparison used the no-jump variant to keep per-ticker jump calibration out of the comparison, and a separate multi-asset comparison evaluated the transfer of jump settings.


\subsection{Variance-corrected multi-asset composition}


We compared six composition methods on the $423$ non-market assets of the training universe to test whether hybrid composition preserved generator variance while recovering the calibrated market loading (Table [tab:aggregate]; Fig. [fig:preservation]). The GARCH(1,1)-$t$ method covered the $393$ assets with eligible fits. With observed training SPY as the market path, the index trackers QQQ and SPYG used the $R^2$-preserving branch, and the other $421$ assets, which we call non-tracker assets, used the variance-preserving branch without clipping (Table [tab:branch]). The two trackers targeted the calibrated $R^2$ rather than the generator variance, so we marked them separately (Fig. [fig:preservation]). We paired each composed path with its uncomposed generator draw to measure the ratio of composed to generator variance directly. Across the non-tracker assets, the median of the per-ticker median ratios was $1.3171$ for naive composition and $1.0001$ for hybrid composition, and the hybrid ticker medians ranged from $0.9933$ to $1.0072$ (Fig. [fig:preservation]b). Individual hybrid paths still varied around the target, because the correction assumes zero covariance between the scaled asset draw and the market path, whereas a finite path has a small nonzero sample covariance. Across non-tracker hybrid paths, the median absolute departure of the variance ratio from one was $1.12%$, and its $95$th percentile was $3.41%$ (Fig. [fig:preservation]b). The correlation between generator draws and the observed market path averaged near zero for every asset (Fig. [fig:cov_diag]), which supported the zero-covariance assumption on average but not on each path.

The methods differed more in distributional fit than in factor recovery (Table [tab:aggregate]; Fig. [fig:tails]). All six methods had median absolute $\beta$ errors between $0.017$ and $0.022$. The naive and hybrid methods had a median absolute intercept error of $0.002\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, but this small error followed from centering, not from any ability of the generators to reproduce each asset's mean. The OLS fit to observed data passes through the sample means, so without clipping, the centered composed paths on the observed training SPY path reproduced each observed asset mean exactly. Refitting OLS to a composed path then gave an intercept error equal to the loading error times the market mean. The three residual-fit methods (JumpHMM-on-residuals, block bootstrap, and GARCH(1,1)-$t$) resampled or fitted the OLS residual series. These methods and Gaussian SIM did not center their draws, and their intercept errors were an order of magnitude larger. Gaussian SIM had the lowest median $R^2$ error but almost never passed the distribution tests, and its median excess kurtosis was an order of magnitude below the observed median of about $13$ across the $423$ assets. Hybrid composition passed $73.6%$ of KS comparisons, against $7.1%$ for naive composition, and its advantage held across the calibrated $\beta$ range. Its own pass rate nonetheless fell from $87.6%$ in the lowest $\beta$ quartile to $65.9%$ in the highest (Fig. [fig:preservation]a; Table [tab:beta_bucket]). It retained heavy tails, with median excess kurtosis of $7.2$ and a median Hill estimate of $0.37$, reported as the reciprocal tail index so that larger values indicate heavier tails (Fig. [fig:tails]). Its kurtosis was similar to that of naive composition, so the variance rescaling did not cause its shortfall from the observed median. Its median $R^2$ error fell between those of the naive and Gaussian methods, because the variance-preserving branch targets the generator variance rather than the observed $R^2$.

Fitting the OLS residuals directly needs no correction, because those residuals already exclude the market component, and two of the three residual-fit methods were competitive with hybrid composition (Tables [tab:aggregate] and [tab:centering_control]). The six-method comparison used each method's native mean treatment: naive and hybrid paths were centered to a zero residual sum, whereas the other four methods kept each draw's realized residual mean. Under native treatment, the residual-fit KS pass rates ranged from $67.4%$ for GARCH(1,1)-$t$ on its $393$ assets to $75.8%$ for JumpHMM-on-residuals. The hybrid rate lay about two percentage points below JumpHMM-on-residuals and slightly above block bootstrap (Table [tab:aggregate]). The JumpHMM-on-residuals and block-bootstrap methods had slightly lower Wasserstein distances than hybrid composition, and JumpHMM-on-residuals matched the hybrid kurtosis and Hill estimate almost exactly. The block-bootstrap and GARCH residual fits produced lighter tails than hybrid composition and JumpHMM-on-residuals, with lower kurtosis and Hill estimates. Subtracting each residual mean over the full training horizon from all six methods, on the common $393$-asset set, changed the KS ranking (Table [tab:centering_control]). On that set, the hybrid KS pass rate was $74.4%$ with or without centering. The JumpHMM-on-residuals rate, $75.8%$ on this set as on the full set, rose to $78.4%$, and the block-bootstrap rate rose from $73.5%$ to $76.7%$. Those two methods also had higher AD pass rates and lower Wasserstein distances than hybrid composition after centering, while Gaussian SIM and GARCH remained below it on both pass rates. Centering brought every method's median intercept error to $0.002\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ at the reported precision and left each path's variance, recovered loading, $R^2$, and excess kurtosis unchanged to numerical tolerance. The pass-rate gains therefore came from removing each draw's realized residual mean. Hybrid composition kept its advantage over naive composition under this control because both were already centered. The correction therefore improved on naive composition without a second generator fit, but hybrid composition trailed JumpHMM-on-residuals under both mean treatments and also trailed block bootstrap after centering.

The emission fallback rules were triggered only in full-return generators, so they could have handicapped naive and hybrid composition relative to the residual-fit methods (Tables [tab:fallback_assets] and [tab:fallback_sensitivity]). Each of $39$ non-market models had one state filled by days with unchanged closing prices, whose zero growth rates gave the state a sample spread below the fallback threshold. The fallback replaced that spread with the asset's full-series scale. The same $39$ models held $11$ further fallback states that were empty or held one observation, and these kept the fallback throughout. The SPY model and every JumpHMM-on-residuals fit needed no fallback, and the block-bootstrap and GARCH methods have no emission states. To measure the effect, we gave each zero-return state its own near-zero empirical spread, which stopped that state from emitting widely spread draws around zero. We then replayed the same $100$ training paths per affected asset with all other model components and random draws unchanged. The change raised hybrid KS pass rates from $68.6%$ to $78.9%$ and AD pass rates from $56.2%$ to $70.2%$ on those assets (Table [tab:fallback_sensitivity]). The fallback rule therefore lowered the training fit of the full-return methods on these assets without affecting the residual-fit comparators. Spread over all $423$ assets, the change would have raised the aggregate hybrid KS pass rate by about one percentage point, less than its gap to JumpHMM-on-residuals. The main comparisons retain the original rule, because the alternative was a post hoc training diagnostic that was not evaluated on held-out histories. Both the main comparison and this check used training histories only; the holdout evaluation tested whether the advantage of hybrid over naive composition persisted beyond the fitting period.


\subsection{Holdout performance and VaR coverage}


To test out-of-sample generalization without refitting or look-ahead bias, we froze the fitted models and SIM parameters before evaluating them against $416$ complete asset histories from $2025$ (Table [tab:oos_composition]). Hybrid composition reached an $87.0%$ mean cross-ticker KS pass rate, against $83.5%$ for naive composition. Its median ratio of synthetic to observed $2025$ variance was $0.97$, whereas naive composition inflated that ratio to $1.29$. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method reached an $86.2%$ KS pass rate and a slightly lower median $W_1$ than hybrid composition. As in training, these holdout comparisons used each method's native mean treatment, and no common-centering control was run on the holdout. Every method passed more often in $2025$ than in the full-length training comparison, and the gap between hybrid and naive composition shrank (Tables [tab:aggregate] and [tab:oos_composition]). For every method, the pass rate against matched-length $249$-day training blocks lay between its full-length training rate and its $2025$ rate. The shorter sample, which lowers test power, therefore accounted for much of the shrinking gap and part of the rise, although the matched blocks were also scored with simulated rather than observed SPY. The rest of the rise in $2025$ pass rates remained unexplained. The average pass rates also concealed substantial differences among tickers (Fig. [fig:oos_composition]). Most ticker-level rates were high, but every method had a lower tail of weakly fitted tickers. For hybrid composition, about three quarters of tickers passed more often in $2025$ than against their matched-length training blocks, and about one quarter passed less often (Fig. [fig:oos_composition]b). The rise from training blocks to $2025$ therefore did not hold for every asset.

To test whether the holdout improvement over naive composition extended to reliable tail thresholds, we estimated per-ticker one-day left-tail Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds from $5,000$ separate $249$-day paths and counted exceedances on the observed $2025$ histories (Table [tab:var]). Doubling the threshold pool from $2,500$ to $5,000$ paths changed every method's mean exceedance rate by less than $0.006$ percentage points at either coverage level (Table [tab:var_convergence]). The coverage level $\alpha=0.95$ has a nominal exceedance rate of $1-\alpha=5%$ and gave each asset about $12$ expected exceedances. At that level, hybrid composition had a mean exceedance rate of $5.48%$, closer to nominal than the $4.04%$ of naive composition. At $\alpha=0.99$, the hybrid rate of $1.12%$ and the naive rate of $0.88%$ lay equally close to the $1%$ nominal rate on opposite sides. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method had rates of $5.62%$ and $1.16%$, close to hybrid composition, and Gaussian SIM had the largest $99%$ rate at $1.72%$. The advantage of hybrid over naive composition thus appeared at the $95%$ level but not at the $99%$ level. Neither comparison established a ranking, because each asset contributed few exceedances, only about $2$--$3$ expected at the $99%$ level, and all assets shared one market period, so their exceedances were not independent. The hybrid mean rates exceeded nominal at both coverage levels, so its thresholds were slightly too permissive on average, and the descriptive check could not confirm their calibration. The cross-asset standard deviations of the exceedance rates described how realized rates varied among assets; they did not test miscalibration or validate coverage in other periods.


\subsection{Jump-enabled multi-asset composition}




To test whether jump episodes improved the temporal fit of the composed paths, we compared jumps disabled, market-only jumps, and market-plus-asset jumps under naive and hybrid composition (Table [tab:jump_ablation], where hybrid composition is labeled Corrected). Enabled generators used the SPY jump settings without per-asset tuning. This comparison simulated the market path in both windows and used each simulated path's variance in the correction, whereas the six-method comparisons used observed SPY in training and the training market variance in the holdout. With these conventions and different seeds and replication counts, its no-jump pass rates differ by up to about one and a half percentage points from those of the six-method comparisons. Its temporal error is the path-averaged ACF-MAE, which differs from the ensemble-average error of the single-asset comparison, so the two are not directly comparable. With hybrid composition, market-only jumps reduced the training ACF-MAE over lags $1$--$25$ by $5.3%$, and the $95%$ Monte Carlo interval for that reduction excluded zero. The KS pass rate fell by only $0.3$ percentage points, and the interval for that change included zero (Table [tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty]). Enabling jumps in both the market and asset generators reduced the ACF-MAE by $14.6%$ from the no-jump setting but lowered the KS pass rate from $73.9%$ to $61.7%$. That cost was far larger than the one jumps imposed on SPY, whose settings were tuned. Across all settings and both windows, the mean ratio of composed to generator variance under hybrid composition differed from one by less than $0.05%$. The correction therefore held the variance target with jumps enabled; both jump settings lowered training autocorrelation error, but only adding jumps to the asset generators materially lowered the KS pass rate. The training-period temporal improvement did not carry into the $2025$ holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). With hybrid composition, market-only jumps raised the $25$-lag ACF-MAE by $2.7%$ and market-plus-asset jumps raised it by $6.9%$. The $95%$ Monte Carlo intervals excluded zero for both increases, and the $60$-lag errors also rose (Tables [tab:jump_ablation] and [tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty]). The holdout KS pass rate changed little under market-only jumps and fell by about $1.5$ percentage points under market-plus-asset jumps. Only $2.2%$ of the simulated one-year market paths contained an episode (Table [tab:jump_ablation_episodes]). The holdout therefore judged the transferred settings on few episode paths and a single year of observed data, and it could not establish their behavior in other market periods.


\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}


We examined how the branch-selection threshold $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$ assigned assets to branches in the observed universe and whether any value of the variance floor $f$ triggered clipping (Figs. [fig:r2_distribution] and [fig:branch_map]; Table [tab:branch]). The $R^2$-preserving branch applied only to QQQ and SPYG, whose hybrid compositions passed every KS test in training. The highest-$R^2$ asset outside these trackers, the individual stock BLK, fell about $0.16$ below the $0.80$ threshold, so the assignment did not depend on a narrow margin. Across the explored thresholds from $0.70$ to $0.90$ at the default floor, only the QQQ assignment changed, moving to the variance-preserving branch at $0.90$. At the default threshold, no value of $f$ from $0.05$ to $0.30$ caused any asset to be clipped. The empirical evaluation of the $R^2$-preserving branch was therefore limited to two assets, and the observed market variance never activated clipping.

Because only two real assets used the $R^2$-preserving branch, we also checked it on a synthetic grid with known loading and $R^2$ (Table [tab:tracker_grid]). The grid covered $(\beta_{\mathrm{true}},R^2_{\mathrm{true}})\in\{0.8,1.0,1.2\} \times\{0.80,0.85,0.90,0.95,0.99\}$, with $100$ replications per cell. In each replication, a synthetic tracker series equaled $\beta_{\mathrm{true}}$ times the observed training SPY path plus an independent Gaussian residual, with zero intercept and the residual variance that gives $R^2_{\mathrm{true}}$ under Eq. Equation (eq:r2-target). The composition algorithm received $\beta_{\mathrm{true}}$ and $R^2_{\mathrm{true}}$ as its calibrated inputs and a second, independent Gaussian draw in place of a generator draw. The composer's generator variance was fixed at the target residual variance rather than recomputed from the draw, so the residual scale in Eq. Equation (eq:r2-rescale) was exactly one. Median loading and $R^2$ estimates from the composed series were close to their targets. Every composed series also passed the KS comparison with its synthetic tracker. Both series contained the same SPY path, however, so the two samples were not independent and these pass rates were descriptive rather than calibrated tests. The grid therefore checked the implementation and finite-sample accuracy of the $R^2$-preserving formula under a restricted Gaussian construction; it did not exercise full-return HMM fitting, non-unit residual scaling, or simulated market paths.

The decline of hybrid fit with calibrated $\beta$ (Table [tab:beta_bucket]; Fig. [fig:preservation]a) follows the market loading ratio $\rho_i$, the market's share of the target variance, which a larger loading raises for an asset of given variance. The scaled draw is a full-return path that already carries the asset's whole shape, and composition adds an independent market term to it. As $\rho_i$ grows, the market's shape therefore makes up more of the blend, and the composed distribution departs further from the asset's observed one; Online Appendix [sec:supp-residual-choice] derives this dilution for kurtosis. A more volatile market raises $\rho_i$ in the same way and, once $\rho_i$ exceeds $1-f$, activates clipping, which the observed market variance never did. To measure when clipping activates, we multiplied deviations of the observed training SPY path from its mean by $\gamma\in\{1,2,3\}$, which preserved the market mean and multiplied its standard deviation by $\gamma$. We then recomposed all $423$ assets with the scaled path's variance in the correction, holding the fitted marginals, intercepts, and calibrated loadings fixed (Table [tab:stress]). Clipping applied to no composed path at $\gamma=1$ and to $76.4%$ and $95.2%$ of composed paths at $\gamma=2$ and $3$. Because clipped paths retained their fitted intercepts while the market mean stayed unchanged and nonzero, their means shifted by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$ by construction. The stress test therefore characterized when clipping activated and how it moved asset means; it did not evaluate fit under the scaled markets.

The variance correction allowed fitted full-return generators to be reused in a single-index model without adding market variance twice (Fig. [fig:preservation]; Table [tab:aggregate]). Its practical value was avoiding a separate generator fit for each asset's regression residuals while improving on naive composition. The corrected method passed $73.6%$ of the full-length training KS comparisons and retained heavy tails (Table [tab:aggregate]). On the $2025$ holdout, it raised the mean cross-ticker KS pass rate from $83.5%$ under centered naive composition to $87.0%$ and held the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance at $0.97$ (Table [tab:oos_composition]). JumpHMM-on-residuals had a slightly higher pass rate in training and a slightly lower rate in the holdout. The comparison supported reuse as a practical option when full-return generators are already available; fitting directly to residuals remained a competitive alternative. Under equivalent centering on the common training assets, JumpHMM-on-residuals and block bootstrap both outperformed hybrid on distribution-test pass rates and Wasserstein distance (Table [tab:centering_control]). The original comparison therefore partly reflected different mean constraints. Neither method was best for every asset, and the average improvement concealed a subset of corrected paths whose fit deteriorated relative to matched-length training blocks. The validation established closer marginal fit, but it did not establish reliable tail coverage (Table [tab:oos_composition]; Table [tab:var]). The corrected method and JumpHMM-on-residuals had pooled-threshold one-day left-tail $99%$ VaR exceedance rates of $1.12%$ and $1.16%$, respectively, against the nominal $1%$ rate (Table [tab:var]). The larger simulation ensemble reduced threshold-estimation error while retaining each $249$-day path's construction. The remaining differences were descriptive: the $249$-day holdout contained too few expected extreme events per asset for a precise ranking or a general calibration claim. Distributional pass rates also require care because the two-sample KS and AD tests assume independent observations, whereas several evaluated generators produced serial dependence. We therefore interpreted pass rates alongside distributional distances, tail summaries, and autocorrelation errors. Matched-length training blocks reduced the difference in test power between the training and holdout comparisons, but the observed $2025$ history still represented only one market period. The evidence supported improved variance and distributional fit across the evaluated universe, with substantial uncertainty about performance in other periods and for individual assets.

The jump comparison showed that improving the single-asset generator did not automatically improve the composed paths (Table [tab:model_comparison]; Table [tab:jump_ablation]). On SPY, HMM-WJ reduced absolute-return autocorrelation error relative to HMM-NJ while retaining high distributional pass rates. Other generators achieved lower autocorrelation error or higher pass rates, so the single-asset result supported a tradeoff between temporal and marginal fit (Table [tab:model_comparison]). With multi-asset composition, market-only jumps improved training temporal fit with little change in the KS pass rate, whereas adding asset-level episodes produced a larger temporal improvement at a substantial marginal-fit cost. Both settings increased autocorrelation error in $2025$, although the variance correction remained effective (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). These tests transferred one SPY calibration from volume-weighted average prices to closing-price models. Separate calibrations for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM had strong in-sample fit, but three assets did not establish a general calibration rule (Table [tab:cross_asset]). The asset episodes also came from full-return models and were not identified separately as idiosyncratic events. Per-asset tuning or distinct shared and asset-specific episode models could change the tradeoff, but neither was tested here.

The correction's effect on the return distribution depended on the asset's market exposure (Fig. [fig:preservation]; Table [tab:beta_bucket]). Corrected KS pass rates declined from $87.6%$ in the lowest $\beta$ quartile to $65.9%$ in the highest because the market path accounted for more of the composed variance (Table [tab:beta_bucket]). Preserving total variance therefore did not preserve the complete shape of the original return distribution. The loading adjustment was unnecessary at observed market variance but activated for most assets when market volatility was doubled or tripled (Table [tab:stress]). That stress transformation preserved the market mean. Clipping retained the fitted intercept while reducing the magnitude of the loading, so it changed the asset mean by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$. The clipped branch therefore traded retention of the calibrated loading and mean for the variance budget and residual floor. Only two tracker assets used the separate $R^2$-preserving branch (Table [tab:branch]). The Gaussian grid supplemented that limited empirical evidence with a branch check at unit residual scale (Table [tab:tracker_grid]); it did not establish performance of the full fitted pipeline across the grid. Its nominal KS pass rates also lacked calibration for the shared-market comparison. These properties depended on generating asset and market draws independently. If their covariance were nonzero, the variance calculation would need an additional cross-term. The correction thus addressed a specific composition problem, with explicit assumptions about the supplied paths and adjustments when the market term consumed too much of the target variance. Marginal-fit conclusions also depended on how populated constant states were represented (Table [tab:fallback_sensitivity]). The paired control improved training fit when those states retained their empirical spread. The main results retain the original global-scale fallback, and the alternative requires holdout evaluation before its performance can be generalized. Dependence among asset-specific residuals remained outside the composition rule (Algorithm [alg:hybrid]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-bias]). A shared market path linked same-day asset returns and could produce simultaneous large moves, but independent residual draws omitted the sector and style dependence present in real assets. Omitting positive residual covariance can overstate the diversification return from periodic rebalancing; Online Appendix [sec:supp-bias] derives the contribution for this construction. The limitation also applies to the residual-fit baselines when their residuals are generated independently. Shifting paths leaves cross-asset covariance unchanged, and rescaling independent residuals cannot create the missing dependence. A synchronized multivariate block bootstrap, residual factor model, or copula could address that omission, while separate univariate models would improve only within-asset behavior. The present evaluation measured per-asset fit and VaR coverage, so it did not validate portfolio-level risk or rebalancing performance. Those uses require a joint residual model and direct evaluation of the resulting dependence. There is also a separate temporal limitation: per-path centering forces each residual sum to zero at the chosen terminal horizon (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). Conditional on the market path and effective loading, terminal returns therefore contain no residual uncertainty. Cross-sectional rank reordering preserves that sum and cannot restore cumulative residual variation. The reported daily-fit and one-day tail diagnostics do not validate cumulative-return risk. Such applications require a construction that retains cumulative residual uncertainty as well as validation of cross-asset dependence.

We developed a variance-corrected single-index method that reuses full-return generators without counting market variance twice (Fig. [fig:preservation]; Table [tab:aggregate]). Centering and rescaling allowed the generated asset paths to be combined with a shared market path without fitting a second generator to each asset's regression residuals. Across $423$ non-market assets, the method recovered calibrated market loadings with low error and retained heavy-tailed return distributions. On $416$ complete asset histories from $2025$, it improved the mean KS pass rate over centered naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one (Table [tab:oos_composition]). Residual-fit methods remained competitive. With pooled thresholds, the corrected method and JumpHMM-on-residuals had $99%$ VaR exceedance rates of $1.12%$ and $1.16%$, respectively, compared with the nominal $1%$ rate (Table [tab:var]). The short holdout did not establish calibration or a precise tail-performance ranking. The results supported reuse of existing generators as a practical alternative to fitting separate residual models, with validation still needed for each intended application. The jump experiments established that the correction also worked when the generators included extended episodes in extreme-return states (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). The SPY jump settings improved multi-asset temporal fit in training but worsened it in the $2025$ holdout, showing that the single-asset benefit did not transfer automatically. Correcting variance also left dependence among asset-specific residuals unresolved. Independent residual draws omit relationships that matter for portfolio risk and can overstate the return from rebalancing when positive residual covariance is missing. The composition rule therefore provides one component of a multi-asset simulator: it controls how generator variance is combined with the market factor, while portfolio-level applications require a joint residual model and validation of both cross-asset dependence and temporal behavior. Per-path centering also removes terminal residual uncertainty over the chosen horizon. Clipping retains the fitted intercept but changes the composed mean when the market path has a nonzero mean. The present evidence therefore supports the constrained construction for daily-fit comparisons, while cumulative-risk applications require a method that retains cumulative residual variation.

\section{Empirical cross-term diagnostic}




The variance correction of Eq. Equation (eq:scale) assumes $\Cov(\tg_i^{\mathrm c},g_m)\approx0$, allowing the variance derivation to omit the cross-term. Each generator draw is simulated independently of the market path, so this covariance is zero in population, but on a finite path its sample value is not. We measured the size of the sample cross-term for three residual sources: the full-return JumpHMM generator used by the hybrid and naive composition methods, the residual-fit JumpHMM generator used by the JumpHMM-on-residuals method, and the Gaussian residual draw of the Gaussian SIM. For each of the $423$ non-market tickers, we sampled $R = 100$ training-length growth-rate paths $\tg_i^{(r)}$ from each source and computed the sample correlation of each centered draw with the observed training growth-rate path $g_m$ of SPY, averaged over the $R$ draws: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \mathrm{cov}^{\mathrm{norm}}_i = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \frac{\Cov((\tg_i^{(r)})^{\mathrm c},\, g_m)}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^{(r)}\, \sigma_m}, \end{equation} where $\sigma_{\gen,i}^{(r)}$ is the sample standard deviation of draw $r$. Centering does not change covariance, so these values equal those obtained from the raw draws. We plotted the per-ticker values as histograms (Fig. [fig:cov_diag]). The universe means were $1.53\times10^{-4}$, $6.58\times10^{-5}$, and $2.26\times10^{-4}$ for the full-return, residual-fit, and Gaussian sources, respectively, and the per-ticker values of all three ranged from $-0.0061$ to $0.0061$. For two independent series of $2,766$ observations, the sample correlation has standard deviation near $1/\sqrt{2,766} \approx 0.019$, the spread we observed across single draws, so an average over $100$ draws has standard deviation near $0.002$. The per-ticker values are therefore of the size expected from sampling noise, and the omitted cross-term is negligible on average. On a single path, it is not zero, which is why the composed variance matches its target only approximately.

\section{Rebalancing under independent residuals}




To determine what a rebalanced portfolio of composed paths (Eq. Equation (eq:sim-composition)) inherits from the independent-residual construction, this appendix derives the portfolio's diversification return. On the variance-preserving branch, the variance correction in Eq. Equation (eq:scale) sets each composed variance to the generator variance. When the generator variance matches the empirical variance, the per-asset variance terms of the diversification return derived below are matched as well. What the construction does not match is the cross-asset residual covariance, and the derivation isolates where that omission enters.

The composition is built to reproduce per-asset marginals and single-factor cross-sectional dependence. Each asset's residual path, whether a rescaled full-return draw or a draw from a residual model, keeps its own temporal structure, but the residual paths are generated independently across assets at each date, so the construction omits cross-asset residual covariance. Consider a daily-rebalanced portfolio with constant target weights $w_i \ge 0$, $\sum_i w_i = 1$, reset to those weights at the start of each trading day. Throughout this derivation $g_i(t)$ is the annualized log-growth rate of Eq. Equation (eq:growth_rate), so the per-period log return on asset $i$ is $g_i(t)\,\Delta t$ with $\Delta t = 1/252$ yr. The wealth process is: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} W_t \;=\; W_{t-1} \sum_{i} w_i \exp\!\big(g_i(t)\,\Delta t\big),  \end{equation} so the per-step log return on the portfolio is $\log(W_t/W_{t-1}) = \log\!\sum_i w_i \exp(g_i(t)\,\Delta t)$. We suppress the time argument, writing $g_i$ for $g_i(t)$. A second-order Taylor expansion of the exponential about zero is $\exp(g_i\,\Delta t) = 1 + g_i\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}(g_i\,\Delta t)^2 + O((g\,\Delta t)^3)$. Substituting it into each summand of $\sum_i w_i \exp(g_i\,\Delta t)$ gives: [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \sum_{i} w_i \exp\!\big(g_i\,\Delta t\big) &= \sum_{i} w_i \!\left[\,1 + g_i\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}(g_i\,\Delta t)^2 + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big)\right] && \text{(Taylor)} \notag \\ &= \sum_{i} w_i + \Delta t \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big) && \text{(distribute)} \notag \\ &= 1 + \Delta t \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big). && \text{(}\textstyle\sum_i w_i = 1\text{)}  \end{align} A second-order Taylor expansion of $\log(1+x)$ about $x=0$ gives $\log(1+x) = x - \tfrac{1}{2}x^2 + O(x^3)$. Identifying $1 + x$ with the right-hand side of Eq. Equation (eq:exp-expand), the part beyond one is $x :=\Delta t \sum_i w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \sum_i w_i\, g_i^2$, and squaring keeps only the leading $(\Delta t)^2$ term: $x^2 = (\Delta t)^2 (\sum_i w_i\, g_i)^2 + O((\Delta t)^3)$ because the cross term has an extra $\Delta t$ factor. Substitution gives: [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \log\!\frac{W_t}{W_{t-1}} &= \log\!\Big(1 + x + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big)\Big) \notag \\ &= x - \tfrac{1}{2}\, x^2 + O\!\big(x^3\big) \notag \\ &= \Delta t \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 \notag \\ &\quad - \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \!\Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i\Big)^{\!2} + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big) \notag \\ &= \Delta t \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \!\left[\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 \right. \notag \\ &\hspace{8em}\left.{}- \Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i\Big)^{\!2}\right] + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big).  \end{align} Dividing through by $\Delta t$ extracts the portfolio's annualized one-step log-growth rate $g_p(t) := \Delta t^{-1}\log(W_t/W_{t-1})$, the portfolio counterpart of the per-asset $g_i(t)$ (still suppressing the time argument): [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} g_p \;=\; \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t \!\left(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 \;-\; \Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i\Big)^{\!2}\right) \;+\; O\!\big((\Delta t)^2\big).  \end{equation}

To obtain expected annualized portfolio growth, we restored the time argument and took unconditional expectations of Eq. Equation (eq:gp-onestep) under stationarity. Define the mean and variance of each asset's annualized growth rate: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \mu_i \;:=\; \E\!\big(g_i(t)\big), \qquad \sigma_i^2 \;:=\; \Var\!\big(g_i(t)\big), \end{equation} the covariance of the annualized rates $\Sigma_{ij} := \Cov\!\big(g_i(t), g_j(t)\big)$, and the portfolio variance $\sigma_p^2 := \Var\!\big(\textstyle\sum_i w_i g_i(t)\big) = w^\top \Sigma w$. The $O(\Delta t)$ correction in Eq. Equation (eq:gp-onestep) contains two squared quantities, $\sum_i w_i\, g_i(t)^2$ and $(\sum_i w_i\, g_i(t))^2$. Both expand by the second-moment identity, which holds for any random variable $X$ with finite second moment: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \E\!\big(X^2\big) \;=\; \E\!\big(X\big)^2 \;+\; \Var(X).  \end{equation} Applying this identity to $X = g_i(t)$ and summing against $w_i$ gives: [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \E\!\Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i(t)^2\Big) &\;=\; \sum_{i} w_i\, \E\!\big(g_i(t)^2\big) && \text{(linearity)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{i} w_i\!\left[\,\mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2\,\right] && \text{(by Eq. Equation (eq:second-moment))} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i^2 \;+\; \sum_{i} w_i\, \sigma_i^2.  \end{align} Applying the identity to $X = \sum_i w_i g_i(t)$, whose mean is $\sum_i w_i\mu_i$ and whose variance is $\sigma_p^2$, gives: [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \E\!\Big(\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i g_i(t)\Big)^{\!2}\Big) &\;=\; \E\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i g_i(t)\Big)^{\!2} \;+\; \Var\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i g_i(t)\Big) && \text{(by Eq. Equation (eq:second-moment))} \notag \\ &\;=\; \Big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i\Big)^{\!2} \;+\; \sigma_p^2.  \end{align} We substituted Eqs. Equation (eq:exp-gi2)--Equation (eq:exp-sum2) into the expectation of Eq. Equation (eq:gp-onestep) and grouped the variance and mean contributions as follows: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \E(g_p) \;={}& \sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t\!\left(\sum_{i} w_i\, \sigma_i^2 - \sigma_p^2\right)}_{\displaystyle D} \\ &+ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t\!\left[\sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i^2 - \Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i\Big)^{\!2}\right] + O\!\big((\Delta t)^2\big), \end{aligned}  \end{equation} the Booth--Fama decomposition, to second order, of the growth of a daily-rebalanced portfolio in annualized growth-rate units . The middle term, $D$, is the diversification return: $\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t$ times the difference between the weighted-average per-asset variance $\sum_i w_i \sigma_i^2$ and the portfolio variance $\sigma_p^2$. The moments $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i^2$ of the annualized rates have units $\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ and $\mathrm{yr}^{-2}$ respectively; the products $\Delta t\,\sigma_i^2$ and $\Delta t\,\sigma_p^2$ in Eq. Equation (eq:booth-fama) are the annualized variances ($\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$). The third term in Eq. Equation (eq:booth-fama) is the cross-sectional dispersion of the per-asset means, of order $\Delta t\,\mu^2$, while $D$ is of order $\Delta t\,\sigma^2$. For SPY, $\mu^2$ lies more than two orders of magnitude below $\sigma^2$ (Table [tab:descriptive_stats]), and we drop the third term, treating the same ordering as typical of daily equity growth rates. The realized annualized compound growth rate is $\bar g_p = (T\,\Delta t)^{-1}\log(W_T/W_0) = T^{-1}\sum_{t} g_p(t)$. If the composed growth rates form a stationary ergodic process, $\bar g_p$ converges to $\E(g_p)$ by the ergodic theorem. The long-run limit is then fixed by the unconditional joint distribution of $(g_i(t))_i$ at a single date and does not depend on the serial structure of the residual draw. This limit argument treats the residuals as a stationary process; the per-path centering and scaling of the composition act on a finite horizon and are outside it.

To show that $D$ is non-negative, we rewrote the bracket in $D$ as a sum over asset pairs. Expanding the portfolio variance, using $1 - w_i = \sum_{j \ne i} w_j$ for weights summing to one, and collecting each pair once gives: [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \sum_i w_i \sigma_i^2 - \sigma_p^2 &\;=\; \sum_i w_i \sigma_i^2 - \sum_i w_i^2 \sigma_i^2 - 2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \Sigma_{ij} && \text{(expand $\sigma_p^2$)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_i w_i (1 - w_i)\, \sigma_i^2 - 2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \Sigma_{ij} && \text{(collect)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_i \sum_{j \ne i} w_i w_j\, \sigma_i^2 - 2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \Sigma_{ij} && \text{($1 - w_i = \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} w_j$)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \left(\sigma_i^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 2\Sigma_{ij}\right) && \text{(pair terms)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \,\Var(g_i - g_j), && \text{(variance of a difference)}  \end{align} so that: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} D \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \,\Var(g_i - g_j) \;\ge\; 0.  \end{equation} The diversification return is therefore $\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t$ times a sum of pairwise difference variances with pair weights $w_i w_j$. It is zero only when every pair of assets with positive weight comoves perfectly ($\Var(g_i - g_j) = 0$) or all weight is on one asset. The rebalancing rule itself produces this effect: weights drift between rebalances, the rebalancer sells whatever has outperformed (its weight rose above target) and buys whatever has underperformed (its weight fell below target), and the trade systematically converts period-by-period cross-sectional dispersion into compounded growth. Markowitz  described this as the variance bonus of the long-run mean-variance allocation; Booth and Fama  called it the diversification return.

To separate market and residual contributions to the diversification return, we substituted the single-index decomposition $g_i(t) = \alpha_i + \beta_i\, g_m(t) + \eps_i(t)$ into Eq. Equation (eq:dr-pairwise), with market variance $\sigma_m^2 := \Var(g_m(t))$, per-asset residual variance $\sigma_{\eps,i}^2 := \Var(\eps_i(t))$, and $\Cov(g_m, \eps_i) = 0$ (checked empirically in Online Appendix [sec:supp-cov]). The pairwise difference variance becomes: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \Var(g_i - g_j) \;=\; (\beta_i - \beta_j)^2 \sigma_m^2 \;+\; \Var(\eps_i - \eps_j). \end{equation} Because $g_m$ and the residuals are uncorrelated, this relation splits the diversification return into market-beta-dispersion and idiosyncratic components: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} D \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t\,\sigma_m^2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j (\beta_i - \beta_j)^2}_{D_{\mathrm{mkt}}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \,\Var(\eps_i - \eps_j)}_{D_\eps},  \end{equation} where the market-beta-dispersion component $D_\mathrm{mkt}$ contains the weighted cross-sectional variance of $\beta$. Applying the pair identity of Eq. Equation (eq:pair-identity) with $\beta_i$ in place of $g_i$ gives: [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j (\beta_i - \beta_j)^2 &\;=\; \sum_i w_i (1 - w_i)\, \beta_i^2 - 2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \beta_i \beta_j && \text{(pair identity)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_i w_i \beta_i^2 - \Big(\sum_i w_i^2 \beta_i^2 + 2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \beta_i \beta_j\Big) && \text{(regroup)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_i w_i \beta_i^2 - \Big(\sum_i w_i \beta_i\Big)^{\!2}, && \text{(square of a sum)} \end{align} so $D_\mathrm{mkt} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t\,\sigma_m^2 [\sum_i w_i \beta_i^2 - (\sum_i w_i \beta_i)^2]$, the weighted variance of $\beta$ scaled by the annualized market variance $\Delta t\,\sigma_m^2$. Across the $423$ non-market tickers, the calibrated $\beta$ distribution spans $[0.03, 1.79]$ (Fig. [fig:branch_map]). A weighted variance is at most one quarter of the squared range, so $D_{\mathrm{mkt}} \le \tfrac{1}{8}\,\Delta t\,\sigma_m^2\,(1.79 - 0.03)^2$. The SPY closing-price series used for the multi-asset universe has annualized variance $\Delta t\,\sigma_m^2 = 0.030 \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ (Fig. [fig:preservation]), at which $D_{\mathrm{mkt}}$ is at most $0.012 \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, or $1.2$ percentage points per year. For any long-only weights on this universe, the market component therefore contributes little, and a larger diversification return must come from the idiosyncratic component $D_\eps$. In the synthetic construction, residual paths are generated independently across assets, so $\Var(\eps_i - \eps_j) = \sigma_{\eps,i}^2 + \sigma_{\eps,j}^2$ and, by the same pair identity, $D_\eps = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t \sum_i w_i (1 - w_i)\, \sigma_{\eps,i}^2$, a weighted sum of the annualized idiosyncratic variances $\Delta t\,\sigma_{\eps,i}^2$.

Because the second-order value of $D$ depends only on same-date unconditional moments, serial dependence in the residual draw does not change it. Equation Equation (eq:scale) chooses $s_i$ so that each composed variance $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2+\sigma_{\eps,i}^2$ equals the generator variance $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$, and any residual generator with the same same-date variances gives the same $D$. As an illustration, consider a stationary first-order autoregressive [AR(1)] residual $\eps_i(t) = \phi\, \eps_i(t-1) + \eta_i(t)$ with independent and identically distributed innovations $\eta_i$ whose variance is chosen so that $\Var(\eps_i) = \sigma_{\eta,i}^2 / (1 - \phi^2) = \sigma_{\eps,i}^2$. The parameter $\phi$ leaves the variance at a single date unchanged, so it does not change $\E(g_p)$ in Eqs. Equation (eq:booth-fama) and Equation (eq:dr-sim). It does change sampling noise: for an uncentered stationary AR(1) residual with variance $\sigma_\eps^2$, the variance of the sample mean is approximately $T^{-1}\sigma_\eps^2(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)$. Serial dependence therefore changes the per-path noise around the stationary mean, not the mean itself, and is not a lever on the diversification return at the level of the second-order formula.

The difference between synthetic and real residuals that does change $D$ is contemporaneous. The derivation assumes residuals are uncorrelated across assets at each date, as they are in the independent per-asset generator. Real residuals share sector and style factors, which reduce $\Var(\eps_i-\eps_j)$ below the independent-generator value $\sigma_{\eps,i}^2+\sigma_{\eps,j}^2$. Matching each marginal variance does not restore this covariance, so the synthetic $D_\eps$ exceeds its real-market counterpart when the omitted covariance is positive. We did not measure the size of this excess, which would require a joint-residual experiment with the marginals and the allocation rule held fixed. A comparison with realized portfolio returns would also contain a second effect: realized diversification returns on real equity portfolios fall short of the second-order formula at all rebalancing horizons. A measured excess of synthetic over realized diversification return would therefore bound the part caused by independent residuals from above rather than measure it.


\section{Validation metric definitions}




To distinguish marginal agreement from temporal agreement, we used separate distributional, tail, and autocorrelation summaries (Tables [tab:model_comparison] and [tab:jump_ablation]). This section defines those summaries; the factor-recovery errors and variance ratios of the composition scorecard are defined in the main text with that experiment. For one observed series $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ and $R$ synthetic paths $y^{(r)}$ of the same length, each distributional comparison used one complete synthetic path and the fixed observed history. The two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) statistic is the largest absolute difference between their empirical cumulative distribution functions, $D_{\mathrm{KS}}=\sup_z|F_x(z)-F_{y^{(r)}}(z)|$ . The Anderson--Darling (AD) comparison gives greater weight to differences near the tails . We used the approximate two-sample KS test and the modified, tie-aware two-sample AD calculation in \texttt{HypothesisTests.jl}, with asymptotic $p$-values. A path passed when $p>0.05$, and the pass percentage was $100$ times the fraction of passing paths. These percentages are not calibrated significance levels, for three reasons: returns are serially dependent, whereas the tests assume independent observations; every path is compared with the same observed series, so the comparisons are not independent of one another; and in sample, the generator was fitted to that series. A high pass rate therefore describes agreement with the supplied history and is not evidence that the model and the data share a distribution.

To quantify marginal discrepancies without a pass threshold, we computed Wasserstein-1 and histogram Hellinger scores for each path (Table [tab:model_comparison]). Let $x_{(j)}$ and $y^{(r)}_{(j)}$ denote the $j$th order statistics in increasing order. Because each synthetic path has the same length as the observed series, the Wasserstein-1 distance is: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} W_1(x,y^{(r)})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} |x_{(j)}-y^{(r)}_{(j)}|.  \end{equation} It has the growth-rate units $\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$. For each Hellinger comparison, we formed $50$ equally spaced bins between the minimum and maximum of the pooled pair of samples. Let $p_b$ and $q_b$ be the observed and simulated bin counts divided by the sample length $n$. The histogram score is: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} H(x,y^{(r)})=\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{50} (\sqrt{p_b}-\sqrt{q_b})^2\right]^{1/2}.  \end{equation} Every bin, including the last, contained its lower edge and excluded its upper edge, so observations equal to the pooled maximum fell outside all bins and that sample's bin weights sum to slightly less than one; we state this convention because the reported scores were computed with it. The score is a histogram approximation to the Hellinger distance between the two densities. Its bin edges depend on each pair, and its bins hold fewer observations in shorter samples, so it is best compared across generators within one window; holdout scores on $249$ observations run larger than training scores partly for that reason. Smaller Wasserstein and Hellinger scores indicate closer marginal agreement.

To describe tail shape, we computed excess kurtosis for each synthetic path and compared its mean over paths with the observed value (Table [tab:model_comparison]); the composition tables report medians instead. With $m_p(x)=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)^p$, the estimator was $\kappa(x)=m_4(x)/m_2(x)^2-3$, without a finite-sample bias correction. The composition scorecard also used the Hill index of absolute returns (Table [tab:aggregate]). Let $X_{(1)}\ge\cdots\ge X_{(n)}$ be decreasingly ordered absolute returns and $k=\max\{2,\lfloor0.05n\rfloor\}$. The implemented upper-tail estimator is: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \hat\xi=\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \log\!\left(\frac{X_{(j)}}{X_{(k)}}\right).  \end{equation} The estimate $\hat\xi$ is the reciprocal of the conventional tail index, so larger values indicate a heavier absolute-return tail; the threshold $X_{(k)}$ must be positive. These shape summaries do not measure how often returns exceed a Value-at-Risk threshold.

To assess whether the simulated distributions bracketed the observed one, we measured agreement at $99$ quantile levels (Table [tab:model_comparison]). For $u_j=j/100$, $j=1,\ldots,99$, let $Q_x(u_j)$ be the observed sample quantile and $Q_r(u_j)$ the corresponding quantile of path $r$. Let $L_j$ and $U_j$ be the $5$th and $95$th percentiles of $\{Q_r(u_j):r=1,\ldots,R\}$. All quantiles used linear interpolation between order statistics, the common software default (type 7). The reported quantile-envelope coverage is: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} C=\frac{100}{99}\sum_{j=1}^{99} \mathbf{1}\{L_j\le Q_x(u_j)\le U_j\}.  \end{equation} Larger values mean that more observed quantiles fall inside the pointwise simulation envelopes. This statistic assesses marginal quantile agreement; it does not estimate daily Value-at-Risk exceedance coverage or simultaneous confidence coverage.

To quantify persistence in return magnitude, we used the sample autocorrelation function (ACF) of absolute returns. Each series was centered by its own full-series absolute-return mean, $\bar a_x=n^{-1}\sum_t|x_t|$. At lag $\ell$, the calculation was: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} a_\ell(x)= \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n-\ell}(|x_t|-\bar a_x)(|x_{t+\ell}|-\bar a_x)} {\sum_{t=1}^{n}(|x_t|-\bar a_x)^2}.  \end{equation} The denominator used all $n$ observations, with no lag-dependent rescaling. Both error scores below start at lag one. The single-asset comparison measured the error of the ensemble-average ACF (Table [tab:model_comparison]). For a lag limit $L$, that mean absolute error is: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} E^{\rm ensemble}_L=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \left|a_\ell(x)-\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}a_\ell(y^{(r)})\right|.  \end{equation} We used $R=1,000$ and $L=\min(252,n-1)$: lags $1$--$252$ for the $2,766$-observation training series and $1$--$248$ for the $249$-observation holdout. This evaluation lag limit is separate from the $25$-lag window of the tuning objective in Eq. Equation (eq:grid_search). The state-resolution and emission comparisons (Tables [tab:sensitivity_n] and [tab:emission_hsmm]) used the same ensemble convention.

The multi-asset jump comparison instead measured error within each path before averaging (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). For one asset, its mean path error is: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} E^{\rm path}_L=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R} \left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} |a_\ell(x)-a_\ell(y^{(r)})|\right].  \end{equation} We used $L=25$ and $60$ in both windows and averaged these scores over assets. The fallback sensitivity check also reported this path score with $L=25$ (Table [tab:fallback_sensitivity]). At a common lag limit and on the same paths, the triangle inequality gives $E^{\rm ensemble}_L\le E^{\rm path}_L$: averaging the curves can cancel errors of opposite sign. The ensemble score asks whether the model's average autocorrelation curve matches the observed curve; the path score asks how far a single path's curve typically falls from it, sampling noise included. Their magnitudes cannot be compared directly across the single-asset and composition experiments, which also differ in lag limits, histories, and assets.

To describe simulation uncertainty in the single-asset comparison, we used a standard-error method suited to each statistic (Table [tab:model_comparison]). For a pass fraction $\hat p$, the reported binomial standard error was $100\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/R}$ percentage points. This estimate is zero when every path passes or fails; it does not establish that the underlying pass probability is exactly zero or one. For kurtosis, Wasserstein distance, and Hellinger score, the standard error was the sample standard deviation of the $R$ path scores divided by $\sqrt R$. For ensemble ACF error and quantile-envelope coverage, we used $500$ bootstrap resamples of the $R$ path indices, each of size $R$ with replacement. Each resample retained entire paths and recomputed the statistic across all lags or quantile levels. The standard error was the sample standard deviation of those $500$ recomputed values, without a further division by $\sqrt{500}$. All these errors condition on one observed history and a fixed fitted model; they omit uncertainty in fitting, model selection, and market regimes. The multi-asset jump contrasts used the replication as the unit instead: within each of the $1,000$ replications we averaged a metric across assets, differenced two jump settings, and took the standard deviation of the $1,000$ differences divided by $\sqrt{1,000}$ as the standard error, so that the dependence created by the shared market path stays inside each unit (Table [tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-jump-composition]).
