                                                Abstract
            Synthetic multi-asset equity data must reproduce each asset’s return distribution
            and its relationship with the market. Reusing a generator fitted to full asset returns
            creates a problem: adding its draws to a market factor counts market variance twice.
            We derived a correction that centers and rescales each draw over a fixed horizon
            before adding the market factor, allowing reuse without fitting a second generator
            to regression residuals. We tested the correction on 423 non-market assets in a
            424-asset United States equity and exchange-traded-fund universe, using hidden
            Markov generators with heavy-tailed emissions. The corrected paths retained
            heavy tails and recovered the calibrated market loadings with low error. On 416
            complete asset histories held out from 2025, the correction improved the mean
            Kolmogorov–Smirnov pass rate over naive composition and brought the median
            ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one. Its one-day left-tail 99% Value-
            at-Risk exceedance rate, using thresholds pooled across separate simulated paths,
            was 1.12%, compared with 1.16% for the residual-fit hidden Markov comparator
            and a nominal rate of 1%. We also tested a jump-duration mechanism that extended
            visits to extreme-return states and improved volatility clustering for the broad-
            market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY. The multi-asset correction remained
            effective with jumps enabled, but transferring the SPY jump settings improved
            temporal fit in training and worsened it in the 2025 holdout. The method provides
            a way to reuse fitted asset generators for daily-fit comparisons. Its zero-sum resid-
            ual constraint removes terminal residual uncertainty, and unmodeled dependence
            among asset-specific residuals can lead to overstated rebalancing returns. Code,
            cached inputs, result summaries, and instructions for fitting the per-asset models
            accompany the paper.


Keywords: hidden Markov model; jump-duration mechanism; single-index model; synthetic financial

1   Introduction
Researchers and practitioners use synthetic equity paths to test risk models, evaluate portfolio
strategies, and examine market scenarios beyond those observed in a historical sample [1, 2]. A multi-
asset simulator must represent both the behavior of each asset and the relationships among assets.
Factor models link many assets through a shared market path, a time series of market returns [3–5].
The simplest of these, the single-index model, separates each asset’s return into an intercept, a
market component scaled by the asset’s fitted loading, and a zero-mean asset-specific residual. A
synthetic asset path can then be composed by adding a simulated residual to the intercept and market
component. If single-asset generators have already been fitted to each asset’s full returns, their paths
might be reused as residuals. Each generator, however, already represents its asset’s total variance,
including the part driven by the market. Adding its paths to the market component therefore counts
market variance a second time. Subtracting each path’s mean removes its mean shift but leaves this
excess variance. Fitting a second generator to each asset’s regression residuals avoids the problem
but repeats the fitting for every asset. Reusing the existing generators would avoid that second fit,
provided their paths could be transformed to supply only the residual contribution. The question
is whether such a transformation can control the mean and variance of the composed paths while
retaining the generator’s heavy tails and volatility clustering.
The transformation is useful only if each generator reproduces the behavior of its own asset. We
illustrate the features a generator must capture with the broad-market exchange-traded fund with
ticker SPY, which also supplied the market path in our multi-asset experiments. Its 2014–2024 returns
show heavy tails and volatility clustering (Fig. 1). Most returns lie close to zero, but extreme values
occur much more often than a Gaussian model predicts (Fig. 1a,b), a widely documented feature of
financial returns [6, 7]. Raw returns show little correlation across days, while absolute returns remain
positively correlated across many lags (Fig. 1c,d). Large and small absolute returns therefore cluster
in time, a property that synthetic financial data often miss even when their return histogram appears
realistic [8].
A hidden Markov model (HMM) offers an interpretable way to represent both features: it draws
returns from state-dependent distributions and uses transitions between states to represent temporal
behavior [9, 10]. Each state in the model used here has a heavy-tailed Student-t return distribution.
The next state depends only on the current state, not on earlier returns. Fitted to SPY, it leaves
states with the most negative or positive mean returns quickly, and the autocorrelation of its absolute
returns decays faster than in the data. A duration mechanism that occasionally keeps drawing from
extreme-return states for consecutive days can slow that decay [11]. Adding the market component,
however, changes the distribution and temporal behavior of the composed returns. An improvement
in a single-asset generator must therefore be checked again after composition.
In this study, we developed a variance correction to reuse full-return generators in a single-index
model without counting market variance twice. The correction centered and rescaled each simulated
path to remove the mean shift and account for the variance supplied by the market component (Fig. 4).
We applied it to heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators for the 423 non-market assets in a 424-asset
universe, using 2014–2024 for fitting and 2025 as a holdout. We compared the correction with
naive composition, which centered the paths without rescaling, with Gaussian residuals, and with
generators fitted directly to regression residuals. The correction improved agreement with observed
return distributions over naive composition while retaining heavy tails in training (Table 3). With
all fits held fixed, the correction also improved distributional fit over naive composition and brought
the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one on the 416 assets with complete
holdout histories (Table 4). The residual-fit hidden Markov generator and the correction gave similar
agreement with observed return distributions in both periods. Without the duration mechanism, the
correction left absolute-return autocorrelation error essentially unchanged from naive composition
(Table 6). Transferring SPY’s duration settings to the composed paths improved their autocorrelation
agreement in training but worsened it in the holdout (Table 6). The correction imposed a zero residual
sum over each simulated path, removing cumulative residual uncertainty at the path endpoint. Neither
reuse nor residual fitting modeled dependence among different assets’ residuals, which portfolio
evaluation also requires [12, 13]. These results support reuse as a practical option for daily-return
comparisons when full-return generators already exist.

2   Related Work
Single-asset return models treat changing volatility, market regimes, and extreme moves in different
ways. Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) and generalized autoregressive condi-
tional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models allow conditional variance to change over time [14, 15].
Even with Gaussian innovations, the changing variance can produce an unconditional return distribu-
tion with excess kurtosis. Heavy-tailed innovations can improve the tail fit when Gaussian GARCH
understates the frequency of extreme returns. Stochastic-volatility models make volatility a latent
process [16–18]. Jump-diffusion models add discontinuous price moves [19, 20], but a jump com-
ponent alone does not explain persistent volatility. Markov-switching ARCH and GARCH models
combine discrete regimes with changing conditional variance [21, 22]. Hidden-state models represent
returns with a small number of market regimes. In a hidden Markov model (HMM), each latent state
has a separate return distribution. Gaussian-emission HMMs can reproduce heavy tails and weak
raw-return autocorrelation, but may still miss volatility clustering [23, 10]. Hidden semi-Markov
models allow more flexible state durations and can improve volatility persistence [11]. An HMM
does not create a jump process by itself. Rare extreme moves must come from the state-dependent
return distributions or from a transition rule designed for such events. Deep generative models learn
return distributions and temporal features without specifying a small set of market regimes or a
parametric return process. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are one class of deep generative
model. Quant GANs have reproduced several financial time-series features, including volatility
clustering and leverage effects [24]. Sig-Wasserstein GANs use path signatures to learn features that
depend on the order of observations [25]. In contrast, evaluations of GAN paths, including paths
from the time-series generative adversarial network (TimeGAN), have found too little autocorrelation
in absolute returns [26–28]. The conflicting results show that flexible architectures do not guarantee
a match to financial stylized facts. Model design, training, and evaluation all matter [1, 8].
Multi-asset generation also requires a model of cross-asset dependence. Multivariate GARCH models
estimate time-varying covariance matrices. The dynamic conditional correlation model reduces the
number of parameters by separating individual volatility models from correlation dynamics [29, 30].
Copulas separate each asset’s return distribution from the dependence model. Dynamic copulas allow
cross-asset dependence to change over time, while vine copulas provide flexible high-dimensional
constructions [31, 32]. Factor models reduce dimension by linking each asset to a small number of
shared factors [3–5]. Shared-state HMMs and multivariate GANs can also generate joint market
moves [33, 34]. A standard factor model can resample historical residuals or fit a separate residual
generator for every asset. This paper addresses a narrower problem. We start with single-asset
generators already fitted to full returns and ask whether they can be reused in a factor model. The
relevant comparison is with methods fitted directly to regression residuals: those methods remove
the market component before fitting, whereas reuse adjusts an existing full-return draw during
composition. Neither approach specifies cross-asset residual dependence unless that dependence is
modeled separately.

3   Methods
We fitted a single-asset generator for each asset and combined the generated paths with a variance-
corrected single-index model. The training data contained 424 United States-listed equities and
exchange-traded funds observed from 2014 through 2024, and data from 2025 served as a holdout.
The single-asset analysis focused on the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY. It used
2,767 daily volume-weighted average prices for fitting, which yielded 2,766 growth rates, and the
next 250 prices from 2025, which yielded 249 holdout growth rates. The multi-asset analysis used
daily closing prices for all assets, with SPY as the market, so SPY had two fitted generators: one on
volume-weighted prices for the single-asset analysis and one on closing prices for the market path.
Of the other 423 assets in the training universe, 416 had complete 2025 histories and were included
in the multi-asset holdout evaluation. We defined the excess growth rate Gi,j for ticker i between
trading days j − 1 and j from prices Pi,j−1 and Pi,j .

3.1   Per-asset hidden Markov marginal generator

For each asset, the hidden Markov generator divided the range of growth rates, which we also call
returns, into N = 100 ordered intervals and treated the interval that contained a day’s return as that
day’s state (Table 1). We write t for the day index j and omit the ticker index i when discussing one
asset. At each simulated time step, the model first selected a state and then drew a return from that
state’s distribution (Fig. 2). We represented the model as M = (S, O, T, E, π̄) [9]. The hidden
state St ∈ S = {1, . . . , N } identifies the interval on trading day t, and the observation space O ⊂ R
contains the excess growth rates Gt of Eq. (1). The transition matrix T sets the probabilities of
moving between states, E gives the return distribution within each state, and π̄ gives the initial state
probabilities. We ordered the states from the most negative to the most positive returns and placed
their boundaries at the quantiles of a Laplace distribution, fitted to the asset’s in-sample returns, at
the equally spaced probability levels k/N . We used the Laplace distribution because its sharp peak
matches the concentration of small price movements in Fig. 1a [35, 36]. Its quantile function also
has a closed form, so the boundaries for all 424 tickers required no iterative fitting [37]. Specifically,
Qk = FL−1 (k/N ; µL , bL ), where FL−1 is the Laplace quantile function and µL and bL are the fitted
location and scale. We set Q0 = −∞ and QN = +∞ and assigned return Gt to state k when
Qk−1 ≤ Gt < Qk . Because each training return determines its state, the fitted state sequence is
known; the states are hidden only in simulated paths, where the model generates the state sequence
and reports only the returns.
The within-state emission followed a location-scale Student-t distribution:
                              Gt | St = k ∼ µk + σk · tν ,          ν = 5,                            (2)
where tν denotes a standard Student-t random variable with ν degrees of freedom. For observations
assigned to state k, we used the sample mean for µk and the sample standard deviation for σk , subject
to two fallback rules. A state with fewer than two observations used the mean and standard deviation
of that asset’s full training series. A state with at least two observations and sample standard deviation
below 10−12 yr−1 retained its state mean but used the full-series standard deviation as its scale. These
exceptions kept emission parameters defined but could broaden states containing repeated returns
(Table S17); neither applied to SPY, whose states all held many observations. The same rules applied
to the generators that the multi-asset comparison fitted to regression residuals. A standard tν has
variance ν/(ν − 2), so σk is a scale parameterprather than the emission standard deviation. At ν = 5,
each state’s emission standard deviation is σk 5/3, larger than the sample standard deviation of the
returns assigned to that state. The emission is not truncated to the state’s interval, so a simulated
return can fall outside it. We selected ν = 5 from a sensitivity sweep and used ν = ∞, which gives
Normal emissions, as a baseline; Table 1 gives the explored range. For SPY, holding the rest of
the model fixed, the Student-t emission raised simulated kurtosis toward the observed value and
raised the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) and Anderson–Darling (AD) pass rates relative to the Normal
emission (Table S4). A pass rate is the percentage of simulated paths whose two-sample test against
the observed returns gave p > 0.05.

Tkj = 1/N for every destination state j. Because the training state sequence is known, these row
frequencies are the maximum-likelihood estimates, and counting avoided the initialization sensitivity

We added jump episodes to extend visits to extreme-return states and call the resulting generator
the hidden Markov model with jumps (HMM-WJ). When no jump episode was active, a jump was
triggered with probability ϵ at each step, and its duration was drawn as K ∼ Poisson(λ), where λ
is the mean episode length [40]. A draw of K = 0 produced no episode, and the chain made an
ordinary transition. For K > 0, the episode consisted of K forced tail steps, beginning with the step
that triggered it. At each forced step, the model replaced the ordinary transition by first choosing
one of two tail sets and then drawing a state uniformly from that set. The set S− = {1, . . . , Ntail }
contained the most negative states, and S+ = {N − Ntail + 1, . . . , N } contained the most positive
states. The model chose the negative set with probability pneg and the positive set otherwise. Because
pneg is slightly above one half, negative extreme returns occurred slightly more often than positive
ones. The choice was repeated at each forced step, so one episode can contain returns of both signs.
Table 1 lists ϵ, λ, pneg , and Ntail ; we tuned ϵ and λ and fixed pneg and Ntail . Jump-diffusion models
use Poisson arrivals to add instantaneous price jumps [19, 20]. Our model instead uses the Poisson
distribution for the episode length, and an episode is a sustained visit to the tail states rather than a
single price move. Setting ϵ = 0 removes the mechanism; we call this variant the hidden Markov
model with no jumps (HMM-NJ). Two closed-form approximations describe how much of a path,
and how many paths, the mechanism affects (Online Appendix S4). Forced steps occupy a long-run
fraction of approximately ϵλ/(1 + ϵλ) of all steps. A path of T steps contains at least one episode
with probability approximately 1 − e−ϵT , so ϵ sets how many paths contain an episode, while λ
sets how long an episode lasts. The jump-augmented process is Markov only in the pair formed by
the state and the number of remaining forced steps. The solved π̄ is therefore the exact stationary
distribution of the ordinary chain but only an approximate stationary start for the jump-augmented
process. The full simulation procedure appears in Algorithm 1. Each step followed T or continued a
jump episode and then drew a return from the selected state’s emission distribution.
We tuned the jump probability ϵ and mean duration λ on the volume-weighted SPY training series to
match its absolute-return autocorrelation and excess kurtosis [6, 7, 10]; the multi-asset comparisons
either disabled jumps or transferred the SPY values to the closing-price generators (Table 1). A
path with no episode follows HMM-NJ, so its autocorrelation and kurtosis errors do not depend
on (ϵ, λ). We scored only jump-active paths, those containing at least one episode [40], so the
objective measures how well an episode reproduces the observed statistics, not how often episodes
occur. At each point of a grid of (ϵ, λ) values, we simulated 200 independent paths of 2,766 trading
days. We minimized an objective that averaged, over the jump-active paths, the squared errors in the
autocorrelation function (ACF) of absolute returns through lag L plus a weighted squared error in
excess kurtosis:

3.2   Variance-corrected multi-asset composition

The single-asset model generated each ticker separately and did not specify cross-asset dependence.
For the multi-asset model, we added a shared market path as a common factor, using the single-index
model (SIM) of Sharpe [3]. We write gi (t) for the growth rate Gi,t of asset i on days t = 1, . . . , T .
The horizon T is 2,766 days in the training comparisons and 249 days in the holdout. The same
symbols denote observed paths when we estimate parameters and synthetic paths when we compose
them:
                       gi (t) = αi + βi gm (t) + εi (t),          t = 1, . . . , T.                   (4)
Here αi is the intercept for asset i, gm is the growth-rate path of the market, SPY, βi is the asset’s
market loading, and εi is its asset-specific residual path. We use εi for the residual path and ϵ for the
jump probability in the single-asset model. We estimated the intercept and market loading (αi , βi )
from observed data by ordinary least squares (OLS). The choice of εi determined the composition
method. A standard SIM draws independent Gaussian residuals with the variance estimated by
OLS. These Gaussian residuals preserve the fitted market relationship but lack the heavy tails, and
with jumps enabled the volatility clustering, that the single-asset generator produces. We called the
Gaussian-residual method Gaussian SIM and used it as one of the compared methods.

the construction also depends on the chosen horizon. We therefore evaluated it as a constrained
finite-horizon construction for daily distributional fit and recovery of the market loading; it does not

Evaluation protocol. To test variance correction against alternative residual models, we compared
six composition methods using a dataset of 424 United States equities and exchange-traded funds
from Polygon.io (Table 3). Each ticker had 2,767 daily closing prices from January 2014 through
December 2024, yielding 2,766 growth rates; this complete-case selection conditioned the analysis on
survival and data coverage. We fitted the same hidden Markov generator to each asset, including SPY
                                                                                             2
(Table 1; rf = 0), and regressed the other 423 assets on observed SPY to estimate αi , βi , Ri,real , and
residual variance. All generators had jumps disabled to isolate composition from jump calibration.
The naive and hybrid methods shared a centered full-return draw, with variance correction applied
only by the hybrid method (Algorithm 2). Four alternatives generated residuals from a Gaussian
distribution, a residual-fit hidden Markov model (labeled JumpHMM-on-residuals), a stationary block
bootstrap, or a GARCH(1,1)-t model, and added them to αi + βi gm without centering or rescaling.
Comparator fitting and eligibility rules are given in Online Appendix S3.1; 30 non-stationary GARCH
fits left 393 eligible assets for that method. Using observed SPY as the common market path, we ran
100 replications per asset with seed 1234 and pooled training scores across tickers. We measured
factor recovery by the absolute OLS errors |α̂ − αi |, |β̂ − βi |, and |R̂2 − Ri,real
                                                                                 2
                                                                                      |, and naive and
hybrid variance preservation by the composed-to-generator variance ratio. Distributional scores were
KS and AD pass rates, Wasserstein-1 distance, excess kurtosis, and the Hill index of absolute returns
(Online Appendix S3). Because only naive and hybrid forced the residual mean to zero, a paired
centering control on the common 393 assets separated this constraint from model fitting (Table S14;
Online Appendix S11).
To test performance without refitting, we froze all 2014–2024 fits and evaluated 100 simulated
paths per asset against the 249 growth rates observed in 2025 (Table 4). Complete holdout histories
were available for 416 assets, including 386 with accepted GARCH fits. Each replication used one
simulated 249-day no-jump SPY path shared across asset compositions. The correction used the
observed training market variance in Eqs. (8)–(12), while each asset’s generator variance remained
draw-specific. Consequently, variation in simulated market variance could move composed variance
away from the generator target even at zero sample cross-covariance. Observed 2025 returns were
used only for scoring. We repeated the KS, AD, Wasserstein-1, and kurtosis evaluations and measured
variance for all six methods relative to observed 2025 variance. Because goodness-of-fit tests have
less power on short samples, we also compared the same simulated paths with randomly selected



                                                          10
contiguous 249-day blocks of observed training returns as a length-matched training reference. We
averaged replications within ticker before summarizing across tickers, giving each asset equal weight.
To estimate one-day Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds without relying on a single short path, we pooled
daily log returns, ∆t times the growth rates, from 5,000 separately composed 249-day paths per
asset and method (Table 5). Each replication shared one simulated SPY path across assets and
methods and followed the holdout centering and scaling conventions. We fixed the 95% and 99%
coverage thresholds before scoring observed 2025 returns; no holdout data entered fitting or threshold
calibration. These unconditional thresholds describe a uniformly selected day within the 249-day
construction, retain its horizon dependence, and do not remove the zero-sum residual constraint of
naive and hybrid paths. We reported mean exceedance rates and cross-ticker standard deviations
descriptively, without treating dependent days or assets sharing a market as independent replications.
Pool-size convergence and an independent simulation check are reported in Online Appendix S12.

Jump-enabled multi-asset comparison. To test whether the temporal benefit of the single-asset
jump mechanism survived composition, we compared naive and hybrid composition under three
jump settings (Table 6). Unlike the six-method comparison, which used observed SPY in training,
this comparison simulated the SPY market path in both the 2,766-day training window and the
249-day holdout, so a jump setting could apply to the market generator. Jumps were disabled, enabled
only in the SPY market generator, or enabled in both SPY and every asset generator. We froze the
closing-price hidden Markov generators and SIM calibrations of the six-method comparison. Enabled
models received the SPY settings of Table 1, (ϵ, λ) = (10−4 , 90), pneg = 0.52, and Ntail = 5,
without per-asset tuning. These settings were tuned on volume-weighted SPY prices and applied here
to generators fitted to closing prices. At these settings, most paths contain no episode, especially over
the 249-day holdout, so the jump effects are carried by a minority of paths. In both windows and
for all three jump settings, the correction used each simulated market path’s variance and each asset
draw’s variance. This differs from the six-method holdout, which fixed the market variance at its
training value.
For each jump setting, naive and hybrid composition received the same asset and market draws,
and one market path was shared across all assets in each replication. We generated 250 paths for
each of four seeds (1234, 2345, 3456, and 4567), giving 1,000 replications per asset, setting, and
method. We used a path-level version of ACF-MAE, computed on each path and then averaged
across paths (Eq. (S22) in Online Appendix S3); the single-asset ACF-MAE instead averages the path
ACFs before measuring the error. We used lags 1–25, the lag window of the tuning objective, with
lags 1–60 as a secondary check, and repeated the marginal and variance diagnostics. The temporal
scores of the single-asset and jump comparisons therefore have different estimands and cannot be
compared directly. We included all paths, not only jump-active ones, in the summaries; jump-active
subsets only described the mechanism (Table S13). For Monte Carlo uncertainty, we averaged each
metric across tickers within each replication, which kept assets together under their shared market
path. Jump settings were paired by replication, sharing the draws of any component whose jump
setting did not change, and the approximate 95% confidence intervals were the mean of the 1,000
replication-level differences plus or minus 1.96 standard errors (Table S12). These intervals describe
simulation uncertainty conditional on the fitted models and observed histories, not uncertainty across
market regimes.

4     Results
4.1   Single-asset generator validation

We first characterized the SPY series and the HMM-WJ calibrated to its training window (Fig. 1;
Table S1). The observed series had heavy tails and persistent absolute-return autocorrelation in both
the training and holdout windows. Individual raw-return correlations were small, although the Ljung–
Box test detected joint serial dependence (Table S1). Under the counted transition matrix, every state
had an expected residence time of only 1–2 steps, and the implied absolute-return autocorrelation
decayed to near zero by lag 5 (Fig. S3; Online Appendix S4). Jump episodes extended visits to the
tail states and so provided a mechanism for more persistent volatility (Algorithm 1). Calibration
against the 2014–2024 history selected mean episode duration λ = 90 and jump probability ϵ = 10−4
(Eq. (3); Table 1). Because ϵ mainly sets how many paths contain an episode and the objective scored
only jump-active paths, the objective depended only weakly on ϵ, and the search selected its smallest


                                                   11
grid value (Online Appendix S4.6). The duration λ was likewise a fitted parameter of the objective,
not an estimate of how long observed high-volatility periods last.
We compared the calibrated HMM-WJ with seven alternative generators on the training window,
using 1,000 paths of 2,766 trading days (Table 2; Fig. 3). Three alternatives drew independent returns:
bootstrap resampling of the training returns with replacement, and Gaussian and Laplace distributions
fitted to them. The other four were HMM-NJ, a GARCH(1,1) model with Gaussian innovations, the
GRU benchmark [41], and the HSMM baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla [11]. The HMM-NJ
and HMM-WJ models had KS pass rates within about one percentage point of the fitted Laplace
baseline but came much closer to the observed kurtosis, which Laplace underestimated at 3.0 against
7.7. Both HMM variants had lower Wasserstein-1 distances than Laplace, whereas Laplace had
the lowest Hellinger distance among the fitted generators, so the ranking depended on the metric.
Bootstrap resampling passed every KS test because it reused the observed returns, while Gaussian
sampling passed neither distribution test. The GARCH(1,1) model had the lowest mean absolute error
between the observed and ensemble-average absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE)
but rarely passed the distribution tests, and the GRU showed the same tradeoff. The HSMM baseline
passed only 82% of KS tests and underestimated kurtosis by more than one third, and its ACF-MAE
equaled the HMM-NJ value despite its flexible state durations. A separate ablation within HMM-WJ
isolated the emission distribution: Student-t emissions produced kurtosis of 7.5, close to the observed
7.7, whereas Normal (ν = ∞) emissions underestimated it by about 29% (Table S4). Both HMM
variants also reproduced the observed skewness of −0.75 without a skewness parameter, because the
state means and scales estimated from the asymmetric series carried its asymmetry into the mixture
(Online Appendix S4; Table S2).
Jump episodes traded a small loss of marginal fit for better temporal fit in training (Table 2; Fig. 3b).
About 24% of the training-length HMM-WJ paths contained an episode, and paths without one
were equivalent to HMM-NJ (Fig. S2). Without episodes, the counted chain mixed so quickly that
HMM-NJ had an ACF-MAE close to that of the three independent-draw generators. Across all
HMM-WJ paths, ACF-MAE fell from 0.059 for HMM-NJ to 0.053, with a small reduction in KS and
AD pass rates. The episode share, the fraction of forced steps, and the autocorrelation of jump-active
paths agreed with the calculations in Online Appendix S4 (Tables S2 and S3). Because the objective
scored only jump-active paths, the one-quarter share was not a calibration target; it followed from ϵ
and the path length. To examine how that share traded marginal against temporal fit, we split 4,000
simulated HMM-WJ paths into jump-active and no-jump groups and evaluated mixtures of the two,
weighted by a jump-active fraction from zero to one (Online Appendix S4.6). Increasing that fraction
lowered autocorrelation error but also lowered KS and AD pass rates, while the Hill upper-tail index
remained stable. Kurtosis fell from above the observed value at a fraction of zero to below it at one.
Forced steps drew repeatedly from the existing tail states without extending the extreme tail, so they
raised the variance more than the fourth moment (Online Appendix S4.6). At the realized share of
about one quarter, the mean path kurtosis of 7.5 lay slightly below the observed 7.7 (Table 2).
To test whether the HMM results depended on the state partition, we refitted both HMM variants
for N from 30 to 200 states (Table S5). Each refit kept five states in each tail set and re-ran the
(ϵ, λ) search. The HMM-NJ pass rates changed by less than one percentage point. As N increased,
HMM-WJ autocorrelation error fell but so did its AD pass rate. The tail sets that receive forced steps
then covered a narrower quantile band. Each forced step therefore drew a more extreme return, which
lowered autocorrelation error, while each tail state’s emission estimates rested on fewer observations,
which lowered the AD pass rate (Online Appendix S7). The primary analysis used N = 100, which
kept the HMM-WJ KS pass rate above 98% and its AD pass rate within a few percentage points of its
best value while capturing part of the autocorrelation improvement. The sweep used the 2,766-day
SPY history alone. It therefore did not establish a state count for the other assets, which also used
N = 100, or for shorter histories, which would leave fewer observations per state.
To test whether the distributional fit held on data not used for fitting, we compared 249-day paths
from the frozen models with the 249 held-out SPY returns from 2025 (Table 2; Fig. S2). On 249
observations the tests had less power, so the pass rates of the weaker generators rose sharply and
the differences among generators narrowed. The HMM-NJ and HMM-WJ models retained KS pass
rates of 96.6% and 95.4%, with kurtosis close to the observed value. At ϵ = 10−4 , few 249-day
paths contained an episode (Online Appendix S4.5), so this comparison mainly tested the no-jump
dynamics that both variants share. The fitted Laplace baseline matched or exceeded both HMM
variants on the KS and AD pass rates and had equal or smaller Wasserstein-1 and Hellinger distances.


                                                   12
Its excess kurtosis of 2.8, however, remained well below the observed 6.9, whereas both HMM
variants reached 6.2 (Table 2). To check whether the single-asset fit carried over to other stocks, we
repeated the fitting and calibration of HMM-WJ for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM on the same training and
holdout windows (Table S6; Online Appendix S8). Training KS pass rates were near 99% for all
three, but holdout rates ranged from 74.0% for JNJ to 97.7% for NVDA. These findings supported
the HMM variants as sources of heavy-tailed asset paths whose holdout fit varied by asset. On the
SPY holdout, their advantage over the Laplace baseline lay in kurtosis rather than in pass rates or
distances. The jump mechanism lowered autocorrelation error mainly in training and at a small cost
in pass rates. The six-method composition comparison used the no-jump variant to keep per-ticker
jump calibration out of the comparison, and a separate multi-asset comparison evaluated the transfer
of jump settings.

4.2   Variance-corrected multi-asset composition

We compared six composition methods on the 423 non-market assets of the training universe to test
whether hybrid composition preserved generator variance while recovering the calibrated market
loading (Table 3; Fig. 4). The GARCH(1,1)-t method covered the 393 assets with eligible fits. With
observed training SPY as the market path, the index trackers QQQ and SPYG used the R2 -preserving
branch, and the other 421 assets, which we call non-tracker assets, used the variance-preserving
branch without clipping (Table S7). The two trackers targeted the calibrated R2 rather than the
generator variance, so we marked them separately (Fig. 4). We paired each composed path with its
uncomposed generator draw to measure the ratio of composed to generator variance directly. Across
the non-tracker assets, the median of the per-ticker median ratios was 1.3171 for naive composition
and 1.0001 for hybrid composition, and the hybrid ticker medians ranged from 0.9933 to 1.0072
(Fig. 4b). Individual hybrid paths still varied around the target, because the correction assumes zero
covariance between the scaled asset draw and the market path, whereas a finite path has a small
nonzero sample covariance. Across non-tracker hybrid paths, the median absolute departure of the
variance ratio from one was 1.12%, and its 95th percentile was 3.41% (Fig. 4b). The correlation
between generator draws and the observed market path averaged near zero for every asset (Fig. S1),
which supported the zero-covariance assumption on average but not on each path.
The methods differed more in distributional fit than in factor recovery (Table 3; Fig. S6). All six
methods had median absolute β errors between 0.017 and 0.022. The naive and hybrid methods had a
median absolute intercept error of 0.002 yr−1 , but this small error followed from centering, not from
any ability of the generators to reproduce each asset’s mean. The OLS fit to observed data passes
through the sample means, so without clipping, the centered composed paths on the observed training
SPY path reproduced each observed asset mean exactly. Refitting OLS to a composed path then gave
an intercept error equal to the loading error times the market mean. The three residual-fit methods
(JumpHMM-on-residuals, block bootstrap, and GARCH(1,1)-t) resampled or fitted the OLS residual
series. These methods and Gaussian SIM did not center their draws, and their intercept errors were
an order of magnitude larger. Gaussian SIM had the lowest median R2 error but almost never passed
the distribution tests, and its median excess kurtosis was an order of magnitude below the observed
median of about 13 across the 423 assets. Hybrid composition passed 73.6% of KS comparisons,
against 7.1% for naive composition, and its advantage held across the calibrated β range. Its own pass
rate nonetheless fell from 87.6% in the lowest β quartile to 65.9% in the highest (Fig. 4a; Table S10).
It retained heavy tails, with median excess kurtosis of 7.2 and a median Hill estimate of 0.37, reported
as the reciprocal tail index so that larger values indicate heavier tails (Fig. S6). Its kurtosis was similar
to that of naive composition, so the variance rescaling did not cause its shortfall from the observed
median. Its median R2 error fell between those of the naive and Gaussian methods, because the
variance-preserving branch targets the generator variance rather than the observed R2 .
Fitting the OLS residuals directly needs no correction, because those residuals already exclude
the market component, and two of the three residual-fit methods were competitive with hybrid
composition (Tables 3 and S14). The six-method comparison used each method’s native mean
treatment: naive and hybrid paths were centered to a zero residual sum, whereas the other four
methods kept each draw’s realized residual mean. Under native treatment, the residual-fit KS pass
rates ranged from 67.4% for GARCH(1,1)-t on its 393 assets to 75.8% for JumpHMM-on-residuals.
The hybrid rate lay about two percentage points below JumpHMM-on-residuals and slightly above
block bootstrap (Table 3). The JumpHMM-on-residuals and block-bootstrap methods had slightly
lower Wasserstein distances than hybrid composition, and JumpHMM-on-residuals matched the


                                                    13
hybrid kurtosis and Hill estimate almost exactly. The block-bootstrap and GARCH residual fits
produced lighter tails than hybrid composition and JumpHMM-on-residuals, with lower kurtosis and
Hill estimates. Subtracting each residual mean over the full training horizon from all six methods,
on the common 393-asset set, changed the KS ranking (Table S14). On that set, the hybrid KS pass
rate was 74.4% with or without centering. The JumpHMM-on-residuals rate, 75.8% on this set as
on the full set, rose to 78.4%, and the block-bootstrap rate rose from 73.5% to 76.7%. Those two
methods also had higher AD pass rates and lower Wasserstein distances than hybrid composition after
centering, while Gaussian SIM and GARCH remained below it on both pass rates. Centering brought
every method’s median intercept error to 0.002 yr−1 at the reported precision and left each path’s
variance, recovered loading, R2 , and excess kurtosis unchanged to numerical tolerance. The pass-
rate gains therefore came from removing each draw’s realized residual mean. Hybrid composition
kept its advantage over naive composition under this control because both were already centered.
The correction therefore improved on naive composition without a second generator fit, but hybrid
composition trailed JumpHMM-on-residuals under both mean treatments and also trailed block
bootstrap after centering.
The emission fallback rules were triggered only in full-return generators, so they could have handi-
capped naive and hybrid composition relative to the residual-fit methods (Tables S17 and S18). Each
of 39 non-market models had one state filled by days with unchanged closing prices, whose zero
growth rates gave the state a sample spread below the fallback threshold. The fallback replaced that
spread with the asset’s full-series scale. The same 39 models held 11 further fallback states that were
empty or held one observation, and these kept the fallback throughout. The SPY model and every
JumpHMM-on-residuals fit needed no fallback, and the block-bootstrap and GARCH methods have
no emission states. To measure the effect, we gave each zero-return state its own near-zero empirical
spread, which stopped that state from emitting widely spread draws around zero. We then replayed
the same 100 training paths per affected asset with all other model components and random draws
unchanged. The change raised hybrid KS pass rates from 68.6% to 78.9% and AD pass rates from
56.2% to 70.2% on those assets (Table S18). The fallback rule therefore lowered the training fit of
the full-return methods on these assets without affecting the residual-fit comparators. Spread over all
423 assets, the change would have raised the aggregate hybrid KS pass rate by about one percentage
point, less than its gap to JumpHMM-on-residuals. The main comparisons retain the original rule,
because the alternative was a post hoc training diagnostic that was not evaluated on held-out histories.
Both the main comparison and this check used training histories only; the holdout evaluation tested
whether the advantage of hybrid over naive composition persisted beyond the fitting period.

4.3   Holdout performance and VaR coverage

To test out-of-sample generalization without refitting or look-ahead bias, we froze the fitted models
and SIM parameters before evaluating them against 416 complete asset histories from 2025 (Table 4).
Hybrid composition reached an 87.0% mean cross-ticker KS pass rate, against 83.5% for naive
composition. Its median ratio of synthetic to observed 2025 variance was 0.97, whereas naive
composition inflated that ratio to 1.29. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method reached an 86.2% KS
pass rate and a slightly lower median W1 than hybrid composition. As in training, these holdout
comparisons used each method’s native mean treatment, and no common-centering control was run
on the holdout. Every method passed more often in 2025 than in the full-length training comparison,
and the gap between hybrid and naive composition shrank (Tables 3 and 4). For every method, the
pass rate against matched-length 249-day training blocks lay between its full-length training rate
and its 2025 rate. The shorter sample, which lowers test power, therefore accounted for much of
the shrinking gap and part of the rise, although the matched blocks were also scored with simulated
rather than observed SPY. The rest of the rise in 2025 pass rates remained unexplained. The average
pass rates also concealed substantial differences among tickers (Fig. 5). Most ticker-level rates were
high, but every method had a lower tail of weakly fitted tickers. For hybrid composition, about three
quarters of tickers passed more often in 2025 than against their matched-length training blocks, and
about one quarter passed less often (Fig. 5b). The rise from training blocks to 2025 therefore did not
hold for every asset.
To test whether the holdout improvement over naive composition extended to reliable tail thresholds,
we estimated per-ticker one-day left-tail Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds from 5,000 separate 249-day
paths and counted exceedances on the observed 2025 histories (Table 5). Doubling the threshold
pool from 2,500 to 5,000 paths changed every method’s mean exceedance rate by less than 0.006


                                                  14
percentage points at either coverage level (Table S15). The coverage level α = 0.95 has a nominal
exceedance rate of 1 − α = 5% and gave each asset about 12 expected exceedances. At that level,
hybrid composition had a mean exceedance rate of 5.48%, closer to nominal than the 4.04% of naive
composition. At α = 0.99, the hybrid rate of 1.12% and the naive rate of 0.88% lay equally close
to the 1% nominal rate on opposite sides. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method had rates of 5.62%
and 1.16%, close to hybrid composition, and Gaussian SIM had the largest 99% rate at 1.72%. The
advantage of hybrid over naive composition thus appeared at the 95% level but not at the 99% level.
Neither comparison established a ranking, because each asset contributed few exceedances, only
about 2–3 expected at the 99% level, and all assets shared one market period, so their exceedances
were not independent. The hybrid mean rates exceeded nominal at both coverage levels, so its
thresholds were slightly too permissive on average, and the descriptive check could not confirm their
calibration. The cross-asset standard deviations of the exceedance rates described how realized rates
varied among assets; they did not test miscalibration or validate coverage in other periods.

4.4   Jump-enabled multi-asset composition

To test whether jump episodes improved the temporal fit of the composed paths, we compared jumps
disabled, market-only jumps, and market-plus-asset jumps under naive and hybrid composition
(Table 6, where hybrid composition is labeled Corrected). Enabled generators used the SPY jump
settings without per-asset tuning. This comparison simulated the market path in both windows and
used each simulated path’s variance in the correction, whereas the six-method comparisons used
observed SPY in training and the training market variance in the holdout. With these conventions
and different seeds and replication counts, its no-jump pass rates differ by up to about one and a half
percentage points from those of the six-method comparisons. Its temporal error is the path-averaged
ACF-MAE, which differs from the ensemble-average error of the single-asset comparison, so the
two are not directly comparable. With hybrid composition, market-only jumps reduced the training
ACF-MAE over lags 1–25 by 5.3%, and the 95% Monte Carlo interval for that reduction excluded
zero. The KS pass rate fell by only 0.3 percentage points, and the interval for that change included
zero (Table S12). Enabling jumps in both the market and asset generators reduced the ACF-MAE
by 14.6% from the no-jump setting but lowered the KS pass rate from 73.9% to 61.7%. That cost
was far larger than the one jumps imposed on SPY, whose settings were tuned. Across all settings
and both windows, the mean ratio of composed to generator variance under hybrid composition
differed from one by less than 0.05%. The correction therefore held the variance target with jumps
enabled; both jump settings lowered training autocorrelation error, but only adding jumps to the asset
generators materially lowered the KS pass rate. The training-period temporal improvement did not
carry into the 2025 holdout (Table 6). With hybrid composition, market-only jumps raised the 25-lag
ACF-MAE by 2.7% and market-plus-asset jumps raised it by 6.9%. The 95% Monte Carlo intervals
excluded zero for both increases, and the 60-lag errors also rose (Tables 6 and S12). The holdout
KS pass rate changed little under market-only jumps and fell by about 1.5 percentage points under
market-plus-asset jumps. Only 2.2% of the simulated one-year market paths contained an episode
(Table S13). The holdout therefore judged the transferred settings on few episode paths and a single
year of observed data, and it could not establish their behavior in other market periods.

4.5   Sensitivity analyses

We examined how the branch-selection threshold Rpreserve     assigned assets to branches in the observed
universe and whether any value of the variance floor f triggered clipping (Figs. S4 and S5; Table S7).
The R2 -preserving branch applied only to QQQ and SPYG, whose hybrid compositions passed every

explored thresholds from 0.70 to 0.90 at the default floor, only the QQQ assignment changed, moving
to the variance-preserving branch at 0.90. At the default threshold, no value of f from 0.05 to 0.30
caused any asset to be clipped. The empirical evaluation of the R2 -preserving branch was therefore
limited to two assets, and the observed market variance never activated clipping.

The decline of hybrid fit with calibrated β (Table S10; Fig. 4a) follows the market loading ratio ρi ,
the market’s share of the target variance, which a larger loading raises for an asset of given variance.
The scaled draw is a full-return path that already carries the asset’s whole shape, and composition
adds an independent market term to it. As ρi grows, the market’s shape therefore makes up more
of the blend, and the composed distribution departs further from the asset’s observed one; Online
Appendix S9.1 derives this dilution for kurtosis. A more volatile market raises ρi in the same way
and, once ρi exceeds 1 − f , activates clipping, which the observed market variance never did. To
measure when clipping activates, we multiplied deviations of the observed training SPY path from its
mean by γ ∈ {1, 2, 3}, which preserved the market mean and multiplied its standard deviation by γ.
We then recomposed all 423 assets with the scaled path’s variance in the correction, holding the fitted
marginals, intercepts, and calibrated loadings fixed (Table S9). Clipping applied to no composed path
at γ = 1 and to 76.4% and 95.2% of composed paths at γ = 2 and 3. Because clipped paths retained
their fitted intercepts while the market mean stayed unchanged and nonzero, their means shifted by
(βieff − βi )g m by construction. The stress test therefore characterized when clipping activated and
how it moved asset means; it did not evaluate fit under the scaled markets.

5   Discussion
The variance correction allowed fitted full-return generators to be reused in a single-index model
without adding market variance twice (Fig. 4; Table 3). Its practical value was avoiding a separate
generator fit for each asset’s regression residuals while improving on naive composition. The corrected
method passed 73.6% of the full-length training KS comparisons and retained heavy tails (Table 3).
On the 2025 holdout, it raised the mean cross-ticker KS pass rate from 83.5% under centered naive
composition to 87.0% and held the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance at 0.97 (Table 4).
JumpHMM-on-residuals had a slightly higher pass rate in training and a slightly lower rate in the
holdout. The comparison supported reuse as a practical option when full-return generators are already
available; fitting directly to residuals remained a competitive alternative. Under equivalent centering
on the common training assets, JumpHMM-on-residuals and block bootstrap both outperformed
hybrid on distribution-test pass rates and Wasserstein distance (Table S14). The original comparison
therefore partly reflected different mean constraints. Neither method was best for every asset, and
the average improvement concealed a subset of corrected paths whose fit deteriorated relative to
matched-length training blocks. The validation established closer marginal fit, but it did not establish
reliable tail coverage (Table 4; Table 5). The corrected method and JumpHMM-on-residuals had
pooled-threshold one-day left-tail 99% VaR exceedance rates of 1.12% and 1.16%, respectively,
against the nominal 1% rate (Table 5). The larger simulation ensemble reduced threshold-estimation
error while retaining each 249-day path’s construction. The remaining differences were descriptive:
the 249-day holdout contained too few expected extreme events per asset for a precise ranking or
a general calibration claim. Distributional pass rates also require care because the two-sample KS
and AD tests assume independent observations, whereas several evaluated generators produced serial
dependence. We therefore interpreted pass rates alongside distributional distances, tail summaries,
and autocorrelation errors. Matched-length training blocks reduced the difference in test power
between the training and holdout comparisons, but the observed 2025 history still represented only
one market period. The evidence supported improved variance and distributional fit across the
evaluated universe, with substantial uncertainty about performance in other periods and for individual
assets.
The jump comparison showed that improving the single-asset generator did not automatically improve
the composed paths (Table 2; Table 6). On SPY, HMM-WJ reduced absolute-return autocorrelation
error relative to HMM-NJ while retaining high distributional pass rates. Other generators achieved


                                                  16
lower autocorrelation error or higher pass rates, so the single-asset result supported a tradeoff between
temporal and marginal fit (Table 2). With multi-asset composition, market-only jumps improved
training temporal fit with little change in the KS pass rate, whereas adding asset-level episodes
produced a larger temporal improvement at a substantial marginal-fit cost. Both settings increased
autocorrelation error in 2025, although the variance correction remained effective (Table 6). These
tests transferred one SPY calibration from volume-weighted average prices to closing-price models.
Separate calibrations for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM had strong in-sample fit, but three assets did not
establish a general calibration rule (Table S6). The asset episodes also came from full-return models
and were not identified separately as idiosyncratic events. Per-asset tuning or distinct shared and
asset-specific episode models could change the tradeoff, but neither was tested here.
The correction’s effect on the return distribution depended on the asset’s market exposure (Fig. 4;
Table S10). Corrected KS pass rates declined from 87.6% in the lowest β quartile to 65.9% in the
highest because the market path accounted for more of the composed variance (Table S10). Preserving
total variance therefore did not preserve the complete shape of the original return distribution. The
loading adjustment was unnecessary at observed market variance but activated for most assets when
market volatility was doubled or tripled (Table S9). That stress transformation preserved the market
mean. Clipping retained the fitted intercept while reducing the magnitude of the loading, so it
changed the asset mean by (βieff − βi )g m . The clipped branch therefore traded retention of the
calibrated loading and mean for the variance budget and residual floor. Only two tracker assets used
the separate R2 -preserving branch (Table S7). The Gaussian grid supplemented that limited empirical
evidence with a branch check at unit residual scale (Table S8); it did not establish performance
of the full fitted pipeline across the grid. Its nominal KS pass rates also lacked calibration for
the shared-market comparison. These properties depended on generating asset and market draws
independently. If their covariance were nonzero, the variance calculation would need an additional
cross-term. The correction thus addressed a specific composition problem, with explicit assumptions
about the supplied paths and adjustments when the market term consumed too much of the target
variance. Marginal-fit conclusions also depended on how populated constant states were represented
(Table S18). The paired control improved training fit when those states retained their empirical
spread. The main results retain the original global-scale fallback, and the alternative requires holdout
evaluation before its performance can be generalized. Dependence among asset-specific residuals
remained outside the composition rule (Algorithm 2; Online Appendix S2). A shared market path
linked same-day asset returns and could produce simultaneous large moves, but independent residual
draws omitted the sector and style dependence present in real assets. Omitting positive residual
covariance can overstate the diversification return from periodic rebalancing [42, 13, 43]; Online
Appendix S2 derives the contribution for this construction. The limitation also applies to the residual-
fit baselines when their residuals are generated independently. Shifting paths leaves cross-asset
covariance unchanged, and rescaling independent residuals cannot create the missing dependence.
A synchronized multivariate block bootstrap, residual factor model, or copula could address that
omission, while separate univariate models would improve only within-asset behavior. The present
evaluation measured per-asset fit and VaR coverage, so it did not validate portfolio-level risk or
rebalancing performance. Those uses require a joint residual model and direct evaluation of the
resulting dependence. There is also a separate temporal limitation: per-path centering forces each
residual sum to zero at the chosen terminal horizon (Online Appendix S9). Conditional on the market
path and effective loading, terminal returns therefore contain no residual uncertainty. Cross-sectional
rank reordering preserves that sum and cannot restore cumulative residual variation. The reported
daily-fit and one-day tail diagnostics do not validate cumulative-return risk. Such applications
require a construction that retains cumulative residual uncertainty as well as validation of cross-asset
dependence.

6   Conclusion
We developed a variance-corrected single-index method that reuses full-return generators without
counting market variance twice (Fig. 4; Table 3). Centering and rescaling allowed the generated asset
paths to be combined with a shared market path without fitting a second generator to each asset’s
regression residuals. Across 423 non-market assets, the method recovered calibrated market loadings
with low error and retained heavy-tailed return distributions. On 416 complete asset histories from
2025, it improved the mean KS pass rate over centered naive composition and brought the median ratio
of synthetic to observed variance close to one (Table 4). Residual-fit methods remained competitive.


                                                  17
With pooled thresholds, the corrected method and JumpHMM-on-residuals had 99% VaR exceedance
rates of 1.12% and 1.16%, respectively, compared with the nominal 1% rate (Table 5). The short
holdout did not establish calibration or a precise tail-performance ranking. The results supported reuse
of existing generators as a practical alternative to fitting separate residual models, with validation
still needed for each intended application. The jump experiments established that the correction
also worked when the generators included extended episodes in extreme-return states (Table 6). The
SPY jump settings improved multi-asset temporal fit in training but worsened it in the 2025 holdout,
showing that the single-asset benefit did not transfer automatically. Correcting variance also left
dependence among asset-specific residuals unresolved. Independent residual draws omit relationships
that matter for portfolio risk and can overstate the return from rebalancing when positive residual
covariance is missing. The composition rule therefore provides one component of a multi-asset
simulator: it controls how generator variance is combined with the market factor, while portfolio-level
applications require a joint residual model and validation of both cross-asset dependence and temporal
behavior. Per-path centering also removes terminal residual uncertainty over the chosen horizon.
Clipping retains the fitted intercept but changes the composed mean when the market path has a
nonzero mean. The present evidence therefore supports the constrained construction for daily-fit
comparisons, while cumulative-risk applications require a method that retains cumulative residual
variation.

S3    Validation metric definitions
To distinguish marginal agreement from temporal agreement, we used separate distributional, tail,
and autocorrelation summaries (Tables 2 and 6). This section defines those summaries; the factor-
recovery errors and variance ratios of the composition scorecard are defined in the main text with
that experiment. For one observed series x = (x1 , . . . , xn ) and R synthetic paths y (r) of the same
length, each distributional comparison used one complete synthetic path and the fixed observed
history. The two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic is the largest absolute difference
between their empirical cumulative distribution functions, DKS = supz |Fx (z) − Fy(r) (z)| [44, 45].
The Anderson–Darling (AD) comparison gives greater weight to differences near the tails [46]. We
used the approximate two-sample KS test and the modified, tie-aware two-sample AD calculation
in HypothesisTests.jl, with asymptotic p-values. A path passed when p > 0.05, and the
pass percentage was 100 times the fraction of passing paths. These percentages are not calibrated
significance levels, for three reasons: returns are serially dependent, whereas the tests assume
independent observations; every path is compared with the same observed series, so the comparisons
are not independent of one another; and in sample, the generator was fitted to that series. A high pass
rate therefore describes agreement with the supplied history and is not evidence that the model and
the data share a distribution.

S3.1   Six-method composition evaluation

The four residual-based comparators used the observed asset’s ordinary least-squares (OLS) residuals
to set their distributions (Table 3). The Gaussian single-index model (SIM) used independent Gaussian
draws with the OLS residual variance. The residual-fit hidden Markov model, labeled JumpHMM-on-
residuals after the software package, fitted a no-jump generator to a synthetic price path obtained by
cumulative compounding of the residuals. The Politis–Romano stationary bootstrap [47] used a mean
block length of 50 days in both training and holdout. The generalized autoregressive conditional
heteroskedasticity model, GARCH(1,1)-t, fitted conditional variance to the residual series [15]. We
accepted a fit only if optimization converged, the likelihood and coefficients were finite, the variance
intercept was positive, and the nonnegative ARCH and GARCH coefficients summed to less than one.
The Student-t degrees of freedom had to exceed two, the unconditional variance had to be finite and
positive, and a trial simulated path had to be finite and nonconstant. All 423 optimizations converged,
but 30 failed the coefficient-sum condition, leaving 393 eligible assets; stored fits were rechecked
before evaluation. All four residual comparators entered composition without further centering or
rescaling.

rather than recovery of a common target for every branch (Tables 3, S7, and S10). Lower absolute

S4.5   Jump-episode arithmetic

The jump mechanism overlays the fast-mixing chain with rare, long episodes, and its operating
characteristics follow from renewal arguments. On each step with no active episode, an episode with
at least one forced step begins with probability pe = ϵ(1 − e−λ ), since a trigger whose Poisson draw

Jump episodes act on kurtosis in the opposite direction to the ACF: they lower it. Treating a jump path
as a two-regime mixture with weights (1 − λ/T, λ/T ) and evaluating the fourth-moment expression
of Eq. (S38) with the forced-step distribution u in place of π̄ for the episode regime gives a population
excess kurtosis of 7.20 for a single-episode jump path, against 8.34 with no jumps (Table S2). Forced
steps raise the variance of a single-episode jump path by about 17% relative to the no-jump model
variance, proportionally more than they raise the fourth moment: u draws uniformly across the five
states of each tail set, so forced steps add many large returns rather than more extreme ones, and
kurtosis, the fourth moment divided by the squared variance, falls. Adding forced steps therefore
lowers kurtosis, first toward the observed value of 7.72 and then below it, while it raises the slow ACF
component. The jump-share sweep reported in the main text traces the same tradeoff empirically,
with the Hill tail index flat across the sweep because the tail exponent is set by ν, not by the forced
steps. The objective of Eq. (3) balances these two effects.
The same expressions show how the two jump parameters are identified, and where they are not.
Within a single-episode jump path, the ACF component of Eq. (S44) has height proportional to λ/T
and decays linearly over λ lags, so over the 25 lags of the objective both its height and its slope
depend on λ; the kurtosis shift depends on the same forced fraction. Every statistic entering Eq. (3)
for such a path is therefore controlled by λ. The parameter ϵ moves the objective only through the
incidence of jump paths, which the conditioning on jump paths removes, and through the small
fraction of multi-episode paths. The objective therefore varies little with ϵ, and the tuned value is not
sharply determined by the data: the tuned ϵ = 10−4 lies at the lower edge of the explored range for
SPY and for all three cross-asset tickers, and the tuned optima vary with the Monte Carlo sample
(Online Appendix S8).

S6    Emission distribution and semi-Markov comparison
To compare the emission and duration specifications, we evaluated three variants on SPY (Table S4).
The first is a hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla [11]. It
partitions the series into eight Laplace-quantile states, the count with the highest in-sample KS pass
rate among 3, 4, 5, 6, and 8 states. Each state’s dwell time is negative binomial, fitted by the method
of moments when the state has at least five visits with overdispersed lengths, and geometric otherwise,
and its emissions are Student-t with ν = 5. The other two variants are HMM-WJ with Normal
emissions and HMM-WJ with Student-t emissions, both with N = 100 states. In sample, both
HMM-WJ variants had higher KS and AD pass rates than the HSMM, but the HMM-WJ variants use
100 states and the HSMM uses 8, so this comparison does not assign the pass-rate difference solely
to the duration model. Out of sample, the HSMM matched or exceeded both HMM-WJ variants on

S7    State-resolution sensitivity
To test whether the results depended on state resolution, we swept the partition over N ∈
{30, 60, 90, 100, 150, 200} and re-evaluated the in-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) and Anderson–
Darling (AD) pass rates and the mean absolute error in the absolute-return autocorrelation function
(ACF-MAE) for the hidden Markov model with no jumps (HMM-NJ) and the hidden Markov model
with jumps (HMM-WJ), holding the tail-set size Ntail fixed (Table S5). HMM-NJ was insensitive to
the partition: its pass rates moved by less than one percentage point and its ACF-MAE was unchanged
at the reported precision, as expected for a model with no duration override whose behavior could
depend on the width of a tail state. HMM-WJ traded the two metrics against each other. Because Ntail
is fixed, a finer partition makes the tail set a narrower quantile band, so each forced step lands further
out, and ACF-MAE improved from 0.057 at N = 30 to 0.045 at N = 200. The same narrowing left
fewer observations behind each tail state’s emission parameters, and the AD pass rate, which weights
tail discrepancies heavily, moved the other way, from 97.1% at N = 30 to 82.8% at N = 200. The
intermediate values were not monotone, which is consistent with re-running the (ϵ, λ) grid search
on a finite path ensemble at each N . Finer partitions can produce states with too few observations
to estimate their emissions locally. The implemented fallback rules keep those emissions defined
(Table S17); missing state-level information does not make the fitted parameters undefined. We
adopted N = 100 for all subsequent experiments as a midpoint that held the KS pass rate above
98% and the AD pass rate within a few percentage points of its best value while capturing part of the
ACF-MAE improvement.

calibration also implies αi = g i,real − βi g m,real , where the bars with subscript real denote observed

S9.1   Role of the centered full-return draw as the residual

The hybrid composition places εi = si g̃ic , a centered and rescaled draw of asset i’s full return, in

distributed like the centered total return. Its variance and tails include the part that, in observed data,
comes from co-movement with the market, although the draw itself is simulated independently of
the market path. This subsection states what the substitution preserves exactly, what it preserves
approximately, and how its kurtosis changes as the market loading grows.

term alone set the conditional mean. The second is the variance not already contributed by the market

S10    Uncertainty in the multi-asset jump comparison
The jump comparison used four seeds and 1,000 market replications per window, with all fits
frozen (Table 6). Within each setting, naive and corrected composition shared the same asset and
market draws. The no-jump and market-only settings reused the no-jump asset draws; the market-
only and market-plus-asset settings reused the jump-enabled market draws. Enabled and disabled
models used separate random-number streams, so matching replication identifiers did not imply
identical innovations. Primary summaries included every path; jump-active subsets only described
the mechanism. For each metric, we averaged across tickers within each replication and formed the
difference between settings (Table S12). The √  Monte Carlo standard error was the sample standard
deviation of the 1,000 differences divided by 1,000, and intervals used the mean difference plus
or minus 1.96 standard errors (Table S12). Keeping the cross-section together retained dependence
from the shared market draw. The intervals did not treat assets as independent market histories or
include uncertainty from fitting or historical-window selection. Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) non-
rejection remained descriptive because returns were temporally dependent. The temporal contrast
between training and holdout remained clear in the replication-level uncertainty estimates (Table S12).
The episode frequencies matched the rare-event setting (Table S13): 247 of 1,000 training-length
SPY paths and 22 of 1,000 holdout-length paths contained an episode. The corresponding shares
across enabled asset generators were 24.24% and 2.46%. Market-only jumps reduced average 25-lag
autocorrelation error for 99.5% of training assets but only 2.6% of holdout assets. Enabling jumps
in both components reduced that error for 96.9% and 0.5%, respectively. These fractions described
ticker-level mean changes, not significance tests. The temporal contrast therefore extended across
most assets, within the limits of the short holdout and transferred SPY calibration.

S12     Pooled one-day tail thresholds
To estimate each generator’s one-day tail without relying on the few extreme observations in a
single 249-day path, we pooled returns only after constructing separate paths (Table S15). For every
frozen training asset and composition method, we generated B = 5,000 paths of length 249 and
calculated linearly interpolated empirical quantiles from all 249B daily log returns. A market path
was shared across assets and methods within each replication. Each asset draw retained its own
centering and variance correction, with the observed training market variance used in the hybrid rule.
The hidden Markov models started from their fitted stationary state distributions, jumps remained
disabled, and the generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) simulator
retained its 100-step warmup. We calibrated thresholds for all 423 training assets, including 393
with accepted GARCH fits, before reading the holdout histories. The resulting quantile estimated
the return distribution of a uniformly selected day within this finite-horizon construction. It did not
estimate a horizon-independent path law or restore cumulative residual uncertainty.
To distinguish numerical stability from holdout performance, we fixed pool sizes of 100, 500, 1,000,
2,500, and 5,000 paths before scoring the observed returns (Table S15). An independent ensemble of
1,000 paths per asset and method checked the simulated probability of falling below each threshold.
Disjoint random streams separated calibration and validation, while preserving the shared market
within each ensemble. Doubling the pool from 2,500 to 5,000 changed the median asset’s 99%
threshold by 0.12–0.30%, depending on method. The corresponding 95th percentiles of absolute
relative change were 0.37–1.03%. Individual thresholds were less stable than aggregate rates: the
largest 99% change was 3.95%, for a GARCH asset. Every method’s mean observed exceedance rate
changed by less than 0.006 percentage points at both coverage levels. Mean exceedance rates on
independent simulated paths were 5.03–5.04% and 1.01–1.02%, close to the respective 5% and 1%
targets. These checks assessed Monte Carlo stability under the fitted generators; they did not establish
coverage on real returns. The reported cross-asset standard deviations were not standard errors,
and no coverage significance test treated shared market histories or dependent days as independent
observations.
