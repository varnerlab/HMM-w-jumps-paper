Synthetic multi-asset equity data must reproduce each asset's return distribution and its relationship with the market. Reusing a generator fitted to full asset returns creates a problem: adding its draws to a market factor counts market variance twice. We derived a correction that centers and rescales each draw over a fixed horizon before adding the market factor, allowing reuse without fitting a second generator to regression residuals. We tested the correction on $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset United States equity and exchange-traded-fund universe, using hidden Markov generators with heavy-tailed emissions. The corrected paths retained heavy tails and recovered the calibrated market loadings with low error. On $416$ complete asset histories held out from $2025$, the correction improved the mean Kolmogorov--Smirnov pass rate over naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one. Its one-day left-tail $99%$ Value-at-Risk exceedance rate, using thresholds pooled across separate simulated paths, was $1.12%$, compared with $1.16%$ for the residual-fit hidden Markov comparator and a nominal rate of $1%$. We also tested a jump-duration mechanism that extended visits to extreme-return states and improved volatility clustering for the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY. The multi-asset correction remained effective with jumps enabled, but transferring the SPY jump settings improved temporal fit in training and worsened it in the $2025$ holdout. The method provides a way to reuse fitted asset generators for daily-fit comparisons. Its zero-sum residual constraint removes terminal residual uncertainty, and unmodeled dependence among asset-specific residuals can lead to overstated rebalancing returns. Code, cached inputs, result summaries, and instructions for fitting the per-asset models accompany the paper.

Researchers and practitioners use synthetic equity paths to test risk models, evaluate portfolio strategies, and examine market scenarios beyond those observed in a historical sample. A multi-asset simulator must represent both the behavior of each asset and the relationships among assets. Factor models link many assets through a shared market path, a time series of market returns. The simplest of these, the single-index model, separates each asset's return into an intercept, a market component scaled by the asset's fitted loading, and a zero-mean asset-specific residual. A synthetic asset path can then be composed by adding a simulated residual to the intercept and market component. If single-asset generators have already been fitted to each asset's full returns, their paths might be reused as residuals. Each generator, however, already represents its asset's total variance, including the part driven by the market. Adding its paths to the market component therefore counts market variance a second time. Subtracting each path's mean removes its mean shift but leaves this excess variance. Fitting a second generator to each asset's regression residuals avoids the problem but repeats the fitting for every asset. Reusing the existing generators would avoid that second fit, provided their paths could be transformed to supply only the residual contribution. The question is whether such a transformation can control the mean and variance of the composed paths while retaining the generator's heavy tails and volatility clustering.

The transformation is useful only if each generator reproduces the behavior of its own asset. We illustrate the features a generator must capture with the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY, which also supplied the market path in our multi-asset experiments. Its $2014$--$2024$ returns show heavy tails and volatility clustering (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]). Most returns lie close to zero, but extreme values occur much more often than a Gaussian model predicts (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]a,b), a widely documented feature of financial returns. Raw returns show little correlation across days, while absolute returns remain positively correlated across many lags (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]c,d). Large and small absolute returns therefore cluster in time, a property that synthetic financial data often miss even when their return histogram appears realistic.

A hidden Markov model (HMM) offers an interpretable way to represent both features: it draws returns from state-dependent distributions and uses transitions between states to represent temporal behavior. Each state in the model used here has a heavy-tailed Student-$t$ return distribution. The next state depends only on the current state, not on earlier returns. Fitted to SPY, it leaves states with the most negative or positive mean returns quickly, and the autocorrelation of its absolute returns decays faster than in the data. A duration mechanism that occasionally keeps drawing from extreme-return states for consecutive days can slow that decay. Adding the market component, however, changes the distribution and temporal behavior of the composed returns. An improvement in a single-asset generator must therefore be checked again after composition.

In this study, we developed a variance correction to reuse full-return generators in a single-index model without counting market variance twice. The correction centered and rescaled each simulated path to remove the mean shift and account for the variance supplied by the market component (Fig. [fig:preservation]). We applied it to heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators for the $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset universe, using $2014$--$2024$ for fitting and $2025$ as a holdout. We compared the correction with naive composition, which centered the paths without rescaling, with Gaussian residuals, and with generators fitted directly to regression residuals. The correction improved agreement with observed return distributions over naive composition while retaining heavy tails in training (Table [tab:aggregate]). With all fits held fixed, the correction also improved distributional fit over naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one on the $416$ assets with complete holdout histories (Table [tab:oos_composition]). The residual-fit hidden Markov generator and the correction gave similar agreement with observed return distributions in both periods. Without the duration mechanism, the correction left absolute-return autocorrelation error essentially unchanged from naive composition (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). Transferring SPY's duration settings to the composed paths improved their autocorrelation agreement in training but worsened it in the holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). The correction imposed a zero residual sum over each simulated path, removing cumulative residual uncertainty at the path endpoint. Neither reuse nor residual fitting modeled dependence among different assets' residuals, which portfolio evaluation also requires. These results support reuse as a practical option for daily-return comparisons when full-return generators already exist.

Single-asset return models treat changing volatility, market regimes, and extreme moves in different ways. Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) and generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models allow conditional variance to change over time. Even with Gaussian innovations, the changing variance can produce an unconditional return distribution with excess kurtosis. Heavy-tailed innovations can improve the tail fit when Gaussian GARCH understates the frequency of extreme returns. Stochastic-volatility models make volatility a latent process. Jump-diffusion models add discontinuous price moves, but a jump component alone does not explain persistent volatility. Markov-switching ARCH and GARCH models combine discrete regimes with changing conditional variance. Hidden-state models represent returns with a small number of market regimes. In a hidden Markov model (HMM), each latent state has a separate return distribution. Gaussian-emission HMMs can reproduce heavy tails and weak raw-return autocorrelation, but may still miss volatility clustering. Hidden semi-Markov models allow more flexible state durations and can improve volatility persistence. An HMM does not create a jump process by itself. Rare extreme moves must come from the state-dependent return distributions or from a transition rule designed for such events. Deep generative models learn return distributions and temporal features without specifying a small set of market regimes or a parametric return process. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are one class of deep generative model. Quant GANs have reproduced several financial time-series features, including volatility clustering and leverage effects. Sig-Wasserstein GANs use path signatures to learn features that depend on the order of observations. In contrast, evaluations of GAN paths, including paths from the time-series generative adversarial network (TimeGAN), have found too little autocorrelation in absolute returns. The conflicting results show that flexible architectures do not guarantee a match to financial stylized facts. Model design, training, and evaluation all matter.

Multi-asset generation also requires a model of cross-asset dependence. Multivariate GARCH models estimate time-varying covariance matrices. The dynamic conditional correlation model reduces the number of parameters by separating individual volatility models from correlation dynamics. Copulas separate each asset's return distribution from the dependence model. Dynamic copulas allow cross-asset dependence to change over time, while vine copulas provide flexible high-dimensional constructions. Factor models reduce dimension by linking each asset to a small number of shared factors. Shared-state HMMs and multivariate GANs can also generate joint market moves. A standard factor model can resample historical residuals or fit a separate residual generator for every asset. This paper addresses a narrower problem. We start with single-asset generators already fitted to full returns and ask whether they can be reused in a factor model. The relevant comparison is with methods fitted directly to regression residuals: those methods remove the market component before fitting, whereas reuse adjusts an existing full-return draw during composition. Neither approach specifies cross-asset residual dependence unless that dependence is modeled separately.

For each asset, the hidden Markov generator divided the range of growth rates, which we also call returns, into $N=100$ ordered intervals and treated the interval that contained a day's return as that day's state (Table [tab:hyperparams]). We write $t$ for the day index $j$ and omit the ticker index $i$ when discussing one asset. At each simulated time step, the model first selected a state and then drew a return from that state's distribution (Fig. [fig:model_architecture]). We represented the model as $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathbf{T}, \mathbf{E}, \bar{\pi})$ . The hidden state $S_t \in \mathcal{S} = \{1,\dots,N\}$ identifies the interval on trading day $t$, and the observation space $\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}$ contains the excess growth rates $G_t$ of Eq. Equation (eq:growth_rate). The transition matrix $\mathbf{T}$ sets the probabilities of moving between states, $\mathbf{E}$ gives the return distribution within each state, and $\bar{\pi}$ gives the initial state probabilities. We ordered the states from the most negative to the most positive returns and placed their boundaries at the quantiles of a Laplace distribution, fitted to the asset's in-sample returns, at the equally spaced probability levels $k/N$. We used the Laplace distribution because its sharp peak matches the concentration of small price movements in Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]a . Its quantile function also has a closed form, so the boundaries for all $424$ tickers required no iterative fitting. Specifically, $Q_k = F_L^{-1}(k/N;\, \mu_L, b_L)$, where $F_L^{-1}$ is the Laplace quantile function and $\mu_L$ and $b_L$ are the fitted location and scale. We set $Q_0=-\infty$ and $Q_N=+\infty$ and assigned return $G_t$ to state $k$ when $Q_{k-1}\le G_t<Q_k$. Because each training return determines its state, the fitted state sequence is known; the states are hidden only in simulated paths, where the model generates the state sequence and reports only the returns.

The within-state emission followed a location-scale Student-$t$ distribution: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} G_t \mid S_t = k \;\sim\; \mu_k + \sigma_k \cdot t_{\nu}, \qquad \nu = 5,  \end{equation} where $t_\nu$ denotes a standard Student-$t$ random variable with $\nu$ degrees of freedom. For observations assigned to state $k$, we used the sample mean for $\mu_k$ and the sample standard deviation for $\sigma_k$, subject to two fallback rules. A state with fewer than two observations used the mean and standard deviation of that asset's full training series. A state with at least two observations and sample standard deviation below $10^{-12}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ retained its state mean but used the full-series standard deviation as its scale. These exceptions kept emission parameters defined but could broaden states containing repeated returns (Table [tab:fallback_assets]); neither applied to SPY, whose states all held many observations. The same rules applied to the generators that the multi-asset comparison fitted to regression residuals. A standard $t_\nu$ has variance $\nu/(\nu-2)$, so $\sigma_k$ is a scale parameter rather than the emission standard deviation. At $\nu=5$, each state's emission standard deviation is $\sigma_k\sqrt{5/3}$, larger than the sample standard deviation of the returns assigned to that state. The emission is not truncated to the state's interval, so a simulated return can fall outside it. We selected $\nu = 5$ from a sensitivity sweep and used $\nu = \infty$, which gives Normal emissions, as a baseline; Table [tab:hyperparams] gives the explored range. For SPY, holding the rest of the model fixed, the Student-$t$ emission raised simulated kurtosis toward the observed value and raised the Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) and Anderson--Darling (AD) pass rates relative to the Normal emission (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]). A pass rate is the percentage of simulated paths whose two-sample test against the observed returns gave $p>0.05$.

We estimated the transition matrix $\mathbf{T}$ by counting observed transitions between consecutive states and dividing each row by its total count. A state with no observed outgoing transition used a uniform row, $\mathbf{T}_{kj}=1/N$ for every destination state $j$. Because the training state sequence is known, these row frequencies are the maximum-likelihood estimates, and counting avoided the initialization sensitivity of iterative expectation-maximization. We obtained the initial-state distribution $\bar{\pi}$ by solving $\bar{\pi}\mathbf{T}=\bar{\pi}$ with $\sum_k\bar{\pi}_k=1$. Because $\mathbf{T}$ was estimated by counting, $\bar{\pi}$ equals the vector of in-sample state frequencies up to end effects, so the model's stationary growth-rate distribution is the frequency-weighted mixture of the state emission distributions. The counted probability of remaining in the same state was low (Fig. [fig:model_internals] in Online Appendix [sec:supp-transitions]), but volatility clustering requires the chain to stay in the tail region, not in one state. Counting reproduces the observed frequencies of consecutive state pairs. At longer lags, however, the chain's absolute-return autocorrelation decays geometrically at a rate set by the counted matrix, much faster than the slow decay in Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]d. The counted chain therefore forgets within a few steps whether it was in the tails and cannot reproduce the observed volatility clustering. Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator] derives the fitted generator's stationary law and autocorrelation structure and quantifies both this rapid mixing and the autocorrelation added by the jump mechanism.

We added jump episodes to extend visits to extreme-return states and call the resulting generator the hidden Markov model with jumps (HMM-WJ). When no jump episode was active, a jump was triggered with probability $\epsilon$ at each step, and its duration was drawn as $K\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the mean episode length. A draw of $K=0$ produced no episode, and the chain made an ordinary transition. For $K>0$, the episode consisted of $K$ \emph{forced tail steps}, beginning with the step that triggered it. At each forced step, the model replaced the ordinary transition by first choosing one of two tail sets and then drawing a state uniformly from that set. The set $\mathcal{S}_{-}=\{1,\dots,N_{\rm tail}\}$ contained the most negative states, and $\mathcal{S}_{+}=\{N-N_{\rm tail}+1,\dots,N\}$ contained the most positive states. The model chose the negative set with probability $p_{\rm neg}$ and the positive set otherwise. Because $p_{\rm neg}$ is slightly above one half, negative extreme returns occurred slightly more often than positive ones. The choice was repeated at each forced step, so one episode can contain returns of both signs. Table [tab:hyperparams] lists $\epsilon$, $\lambda$, $p_{\rm neg}$, and $N_{\rm tail}$; we tuned $\epsilon$ and $\lambda$ and fixed $p_{\rm neg}$ and $N_{\rm tail}$. Jump-diffusion models use Poisson arrivals to add instantaneous price jumps. Our model instead uses the Poisson distribution for the episode length, and an episode is a sustained visit to the tail states rather than a single price move. Setting $\epsilon=0$ removes the mechanism; we call this variant the hidden Markov model with no jumps (HMM-NJ). Two closed-form approximations describe how much of a path, and how many paths, the mechanism affects (Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]). Forced steps occupy a long-run fraction of approximately $\epsilon\lambda/(1+\epsilon\lambda)$ of all steps. A path of $T$ steps contains at least one episode with probability approximately $1 - e^{-\epsilon T}$, so $\epsilon$ sets how many paths contain an episode, while $\lambda$ sets how long an episode lasts. The jump-augmented process is Markov only in the pair formed by the state and the number of remaining forced steps. The solved $\bar{\pi}$ is therefore the exact stationary distribution of the ordinary chain but only an approximate stationary start for the jump-augmented process. The full simulation procedure appears in Algorithm [alg:hmmwj]. Each step followed $\mathbf{T}$ or continued a jump episode and then drew a return from the selected state's emission distribution.

We tuned the jump probability $\epsilon$ and mean duration $\lambda$ on the volume-weighted SPY training series to match its absolute-return autocorrelation and excess kurtosis; the multi-asset comparisons either disabled jumps or transferred the SPY values to the closing-price generators (Table [tab:hyperparams]). A path with no episode follows HMM-NJ, so its autocorrelation and kurtosis errors do not depend on $(\epsilon,\lambda)$. We scored only \emph{jump-active} paths, those containing at least one episode, so the objective measures how well an episode reproduces the observed statistics, not how often episodes occur. At each point of a grid of $(\epsilon,\lambda)$ values, we simulated $200$ independent paths of $2,766$ trading days. We minimized an objective that averaged, over the jump-active paths, the squared errors in the autocorrelation function (ACF) of absolute returns through lag $L$ plus a weighted squared error in excess kurtosis: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} J(\epsilon, \lambda) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_J|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_J} \left[ \sum_{\tau=1}^{L} \left( \mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau) - \mathrm{ACF}_{r}(\tau) \right)^2 + w_\kappa \left( \kappa_{\rm obs} - \kappa_{r} \right)^2 \right],  \end{equation} where $\mathcal{R}_J$ is the set of jump-active simulated paths, $\mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ and $\kappa_{\rm obs}$ are the empirical absolute-growth autocorrelation at lag $\tau$ and excess kurtosis, $\mathrm{ACF}_{r}(\tau)$ and $\kappa_r$ are the corresponding values for path $r$, and $w_\kappa$ sets the relative weight of the kurtosis error. When $\epsilon$ is small, as at the selected value, most jump-active paths contain a single episode, whose forced steps make up a fraction of about $\lambda/T$ of the path. The episode adds a slowly decaying component to the absolute-return autocorrelation, and $\lambda$ sets both its height and the number of lags over which it persists. The kurtosis penalty limits $\lambda$, because forced steps make tail returns common within a path rather than rare; they raise the variance proportionally more than the fourth moment and so lower the path kurtosis. The parameter $\epsilon$ acts mainly through the share of paths that contain an episode, which the restriction to jump-active paths removes. The objective therefore constrains $\epsilon$ only weakly, and the selected $\epsilon$ lay at the lower edge of its search range (Table [tab:hyperparams]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]). The search ranges, $w_\kappa$, $L$, and the remaining hyperparameters are reported in Table [tab:hyperparams]. We fixed the selected hyperparameters before running the generator comparisons and the holdout evaluation.

Beyond the Normal-emission variant, we compared HMM-WJ with two benchmarks that model persistence differently. A hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla lengthens state visits through explicit dwell-time distributions instead of jump episodes (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-emission-hsmm]). The Normal-emission comparison isolated the effect of heavy-tailed emissions, and the HSMM comparison contrasted explicit dwell times with the jump-duration mechanism, although the HSMM also used $8$ states rather than $100$ and so differed in more than its duration model. To compare the hidden Markov generator with a neural autoregressive model, we included a gated recurrent unit (GRU) benchmark (Table [tab:model_comparison]). The network took a $50$-return input window and had two recurrent layers of $64$ hidden units each. We standardized the training series with its mean and standard deviation, and separate output heads predicted the mean and log variance of the next standardized return. We fitted the network on training SPY only, selected weights by training loss, and sampled recursively from the predicted Gaussian distributions. Online Appendix [sec:supp-gru] specifies the optimizer, stopping rule, initialization, and recorded computational environment. We scored all single-asset generators, including these benchmarks, by their KS and AD pass rates and by the mean absolute error between the observed and the ensemble-average simulated absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE), over lags $1$--$252$ in training and $1$--$248$ in the $249$-day holdout (Eq. Equation (eq:acf-ensemble-error) in Online Appendix [sec:supp-validation]).

The single-asset model generated each ticker separately and did not specify cross-asset dependence. For the multi-asset model, we added a shared market path as a common factor, using the single-index model (SIM) of Sharpe. We write $g_i(t)$ for the growth rate $G_{i,t}$ of asset $i$ on days $t=1,\dots,T$. The horizon $T$ is $2,766$ days in the training comparisons and $249$ days in the holdout. The same symbols denote observed paths when we estimate parameters and synthetic paths when we compose them: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} g_i(t) \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta_i\, g_m(t) \;+\; \eps_i(t), \qquad t = 1,\dots,T.  \end{equation} Here $\alpha_i$ is the intercept for asset $i$, $g_m$ is the growth-rate path of the market, SPY, $\beta_i$ is the asset's market loading, and $\eps_i$ is its asset-specific residual path. We use $\eps_i$ for the residual path and $\epsilon$ for the jump probability in the single-asset model. We estimated the intercept and market loading $(\alpha_i,\beta_i)$ from observed data by ordinary least squares (OLS). The choice of $\eps_i$ determined the composition method. A standard SIM draws independent Gaussian residuals with the variance estimated by OLS. These Gaussian residuals preserve the fitted market relationship but lack the heavy tails, and with jumps enabled the volatility clustering, that the single-asset generator produces. We called the Gaussian-residual method \emph{Gaussian SIM} and used it as one of the compared methods.

\paragraph{Naive full-path composition.}

Using a full synthetic growth-rate path $\tg_i$ drawn from the asset's own single-asset generator, the \emph{generator draw}, directly as the residual would give: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} g_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i\, g_m + \tg_i. \end{equation} The fitted intercept and market term already set the conditional mean of asset $i$, while the generator draw generally has $\E[\tg_i]\ne 0$. Equation Equation (eq:naive-composition) therefore has conditional mean $\alpha_i+\E[\tg_i]+\beta_i g_m$, not $\alpha_i+\beta_i g_m$. We therefore centered every generator draw before composition, subtracting its realized path mean: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \overline{\tg}_i \equiv \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tg_i(t), \qquad \tg_i^{\mathrm c}(t) \equiv \tg_i(t)-\overline{\tg}_i.  \end{equation} Therefore, $T^{-1}\sum_t\tg_i^{\mathrm c}(t)=0$ exactly. Subtracting the realized rather than the population mean makes the centering exact on every path. Subtracting a constant leaves both the sample variance and the sample covariance with $g_m$ unchanged. We used the centered construction $g_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i g_m+\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$, which corrects the mean but not the variance, as the \emph{naive} benchmark in all comparisons. The sample variance of each draw, $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2=\Var(\tg_i)=\Var(\tg_i^{\mathrm c})$, is the \emph{generator variance}; it is recomputed for every draw. It already represents the asset's total variance, so adding the market term adds market variance a second time. During composition, $g_m$ was the observed SPY path in the training comparison and a path simulated from SPY's closing-price generator in the holdout and jump comparisons, with one market path per replication shared by all assets, and in both cases it was independent of the asset draws. The covariance of a centered draw with the market path is zero in population and close to zero on a long realized path. We approximated the naive composed variance accordingly: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \Var(g_i) \;\approx\; \beta_i^2\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_{\gen,i}^2, \qquad \Cov(\tg_i^{\mathrm c},g_m)\approx 0,  \end{equation} where $\sigma_m^2=\Var(g_m)$ is the sample variance of the market path used in composition. The naive composed variance therefore exceeds the generator variance by $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2$, an excess that grows with the square of the market loading.

\paragraph{Variance correction.}

We set $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ as the target total variance for asset $i$. The market term contributed $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2$, so the residual term could contribute only the remaining variance. We defined $s_i$ as the nonnegative asset-specific residual scale, set $\eps_i=s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$, and required $\Var(\beta_i g_m+s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c})=\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$. Under the zero-covariance assumption, solving for $s_i$ gives the correction: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \boxed{\;s_i^2 \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}\;}  \end{equation} We call the market term's share of the target variance the \emph{market loading ratio}: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \rho_i \;\equiv\; \frac{\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2},  \end{equation} so that $s_i^2=1-\rho_i$ under the zero-covariance assumption. At $\rho_i=1$, the rule of Eq. Equation (eq:scale) leaves no asset-specific variance; for $\rho_i>1$, it has no real-valued solution.

With the observed variances, $\rho_i$ would equal the coefficient of determination of the regression, which is below one. Because $\rho_i$ instead uses the generator and market-path variances, it can reach or exceed one when the generator variance is low or the market path is more volatile than the observed SPY training series, particularly for assets with large $\beta_i$. We therefore required the asset-specific draw to contribute at least a fraction $f$ of the target variance, the \emph{floor}, and set $\rho_i^{\max}=1-f$. We applied the correction through an \emph{effective loading} $\beta_i^{\eff}$, the loading used in composition, which equals $\beta_i$ unless $\rho_i>\rho_i^{\max}$: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \beta_i^{\eff} \;=\; \begin{cases} \beta_i & \text{if } \rho_i \le \rho_i^{\max} \\[4pt] \sign(\beta_i)\,\sqrt{(1 - f)\,\dfrac{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}{\sigma_m^2}} & \text{if } \rho_i > \rho_i^{\max} \end{cases} \qquad s_i^2 \;=\; \begin{cases} 1 - \rho_i & \text{if } \rho_i \le \rho_i^{\max} \\[4pt] f & \text{if } \rho_i > \rho_i^{\max} \end{cases}  \end{equation} We call the second case of Eq. Equation (eq:clip) the \emph{clipped} case. In it, the correction reduced the magnitude of the loading but kept the sign of the calibrated $\beta_i$. We used $f=0.10$, so the asset-specific draw contributed at least $10%$ of the target variance for every asset under this rule (Table [tab:hyperparams]). We retained the fitted intercept $\alpha_i$ when clipping changed the loading. Because the scaled residual had zero sample mean, the composed mean was $\overline{g}_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i^{\eff}\overline{g}_m$, where $\overline{g}_m$ is the mean of the market path. Clipping therefore changed the mean by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$ relative to the calibrated linear mean on that same market path. The clipped case thus kept the intercept and the target variance and set the residual share to exactly $f$, but changed the loading and, unless $\overline{g}_m=0$, the mean. Online Appendix [sec:supp-residual-choice] examines the substitution of a full-return draw for the residual: it derives what the construction preserves exactly and how much of the generator's excess kurtosis survives composition as $\rho_i$ grows.

\paragraph{High-$R^2$ index trackers.}

The coefficient of determination $R^2_{i,\real}$, from the OLS regression of the observed asset on observed SPY, measures the fraction of the asset's variance explained by the market. The rule of Eq. Equation (eq:clip) targets total generator variance, not $R^2_{i,\real}$. Under the zero-covariance assumption, the composed $R^2$ under that rule equals $\rho_i$, capped at $1-f$ by the floor. It therefore cannot exceed $1-f$, and it matches $R^2_{i,\real}$ only when $\sigma_m^2/\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ equals the ratio of the observed SPY variance to the observed asset variance. Index-tracking funds, such as the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ and the S&P 500 growth fund SPYG, have $R^2$ against SPY close to or above the cap $1-f$, so that rule could not reliably reproduce their co-movement with the market. We therefore used two branches: the rule of Eq. Equation (eq:clip), the \emph{variance-preserving branch}, for most assets, and a separate \emph{$R^2$-preserving branch} when $R^2_{i,\real}$ was at least $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$. We set $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}=0.80$, which lies in a wide gap of the observed $R^2$ distribution between the index trackers and all other assets in our universe (Fig. [fig:r2_distribution]). This branch keeps $\beta_i^{\eff}=\beta_i$ and uses the same market-path variance $\sigma_m^2$ as the variance-preserving branch. We computed the residual variance required to recover $R^2_{i,\real}$ under the zero-covariance assumption: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \boxed{\;\sigma_{\eps,\mathrm{target}}^2 \;=\; \beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2 \cdot \frac{1 - R^2_{i,\real}}{R^2_{i,\real}}\;}  \end{equation} We rescaled the generator draw to the target residual variance: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \eps_i \;=\; \left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{\eps,\mathrm{target}}^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}}\,\right)\tg_i^{\mathrm c}.  \end{equation} This branch preserves $R^2_{i,\real}$ rather than the generator variance: the composed variance becomes $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2/R^2_{i,\real}$. The scale factor in Eq. Equation (eq:r2-rescale) exceeds one when the generator variance is below the target residual variance; the composed variance then exceeds the generator variance.

\paragraph{Complete composition rule.}

The complete rule has three outcomes, recorded by the branch flag $b_i$ in Algorithm [alg:hybrid]. Index trackers used the $R^2$-preserving branch; all other assets used the variance-preserving branch, with the calibrated loading or, in the clipped case, a reduced loading. Each composed path is then $g_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i^{\eff}g_m+\eps_i$. We call this complete rule the \emph{hybrid} composition, the label used in the tables and figures, and all results reported for the variance correction refer to it. Membership in the $R^2$-preserving branch depends only on $R^2_{i,\real}$ and is fixed for each asset. Everything else is computed per draw: because $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ is recomputed for each draw, so are $s_i$, $\rho_i$, and $\beta_i^{\eff}$, and one asset can be clipped in some draws and not in others. Because the draws are independent of the market path, OLS estimates from composed paths converge, as the horizon grows, to the intercept $\alpha_i$ and the effective loading $\beta_i^{\eff}$, which equals $\beta_i$ unless the draw is clipped. The variance target and the loading recovery are exact only when the sample covariance between the draw and the market path is zero; on a finite path, they deviate by that covariance term (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). Because centering and scaling fix the residual sum, every branch imposes a zero residual sum over the horizon $T$, which is stronger than a zero population mean. For a fixed market path and effective loading, the terminal cumulative log return therefore has no asset-specific residual uncertainty, although daily and intermediate returns still vary. In the clipped case, the effective loading depends on the variances of each draw, so it can vary between draws and produce terminal variation through the market term. Because centering and scaling use statistics of the entire draw, the construction also depends on the chosen horizon. We therefore evaluated it as a constrained finite-horizon construction for daily distributional fit and recovery of the market loading; it does not preserve cumulative residual uncertainty.

The construction links assets only through same-day market returns. A large market move can therefore affect several assets together, but the rule does not separately fit lead--lag effects or a common volatility factor in the residuals. Algorithm [alg:hybrid] also allows an optional rank reorder that adds residual dependence across assets; the six-method comparison did not use it. The reorder draws one joint vector per day from a copula fitted to the observed OLS residuals and permutes each asset's residual values over days so that their ranks over time match the ranks of that asset's copula coordinate. The reorder imposes cross-asset residual dependence and keeps each path's values and variance, but it replaces the generator's day-to-day ordering and with it any volatility clustering in the draw.

[DISPLAY] \begin{algorithm}[t] \caption{Hybrid single-index model (SIM) composition with variance correction.}  \small \begin{algorithmic}[1] \REQUIRE Nonconstant $g_m,\tg_i\in\R^T$, $i=1,\dots,N$; each $\tg_i$ is independent of $g_m$. \REQUIRE Parameters $(\alpha_i,\beta_i,R^2_{i,\real})$; floor $f\in(0,1)$; threshold $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}\in(0,1]$. \ENSURE Composed paths $g_i\in\R^T$ and branch flags $b_i$. \STATE $\sigma_m^2 \gets \Var(g_m)$. \FOR{each asset $i = 1,\dots,N$} \STATE $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2 \gets \Var(\tg_i)$; $\tg_i^{\mathrm c} \gets \tg_i-\overline{\tg}_i$. \IF{$R^2_{i,\real} \ge R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$} \STATE $v_i \gets \beta_i^2\sigma_m^2(1-R^2_{i,\real})/R^2_{i,\real}$. \STATE $s_i \gets \sqrt{v_i/\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}$; $\beta_i^{\eff} \gets \beta_i$; $b_i\gets\textsc{r2-preserve}$. \ELSE \STATE $\rho_i \gets \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 / \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ \IF{$\rho_i \le 1 - f$} \STATE $s_i \gets \sqrt{1-\rho_i}$; $\beta_i^{\eff}\gets\beta_i$; $b_i\gets\textsc{variance}$. \ELSE \STATE $s_i\gets\sqrt{f}$; $\beta_i^{\eff}\gets\sign(\beta_i) \sqrt{(1-f)\sigma_{\gen,i}^2/\sigma_m^2}$. \STATE $b_i\gets\textsc{clipped}$. \ENDIF \ENDIF \STATE $\eps_i \gets s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$. \ENDFOR \STATE \textit{Optional:} jointly rank-reorder $\{\eps_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$. \FOR{each asset $i = 1,\dots,N$} \STATE $g_i \gets \alpha_i + \beta_i^{\eff}\, g_m + \eps_i$ \hfill \COMMENT{Eq. Equation (eq:sim-composition)} \ENDFOR \end{algorithmic} \end{algorithm}
