--- Paragraph introduction.P1 (introduction.tex:1) ---
[introduction.P1.S1] Researchers and practitioners use synthetic equity paths to test risk models, evaluate portfolio strategies, and examine market scenarios beyond those observed in a historical sample.
[introduction.P1.S2] A multi-asset simulator must represent both the behavior of each asset and the relationships among assets.
[introduction.P1.S3] Factor models provide a simple way to link many assets through a shared market path, a time series of market returns.
[introduction.P1.S4] The simplest of these, the single-index model, regresses each asset's return on the market path.
[introduction.P1.S5] It separates the return into an intercept, a market component equal to the market path scaled by the fitted loading, and a zero-mean asset-specific residual.
[introduction.P1.S6] A synthetic path for the asset can then be composed by adding a simulated residual to the intercept and market component.
[introduction.P1.S7] Suppose single-asset generators, models that simulate return paths for one asset, have already been fitted to each asset's full returns rather than to its regression residuals.
[introduction.P1.S8] Their simulated paths are then an obvious candidate for that residual.
[introduction.P1.S9] Each such generator, however, already represents its asset's total variance, including the part driven by the market.
[introduction.P1.S10] Adding its paths to the market component as though they were residuals therefore counts market variance a second time.
[introduction.P1.S11] The paths also generally have a nonzero mean, unlike residuals; subtracting each path's mean removes that shift but leaves market variance counted twice.
[introduction.P1.S12] Fitting a second generator to each asset's regression residuals avoids the problem but repeats the model fitting for every asset.
[introduction.P1.S13] Reusing the existing generators would avoid that second fit, provided their paths could be transformed to supply only the residual contribution.
[introduction.P1.S14] The question is whether such a transformation can control the mean and variance of the composed paths while retaining the generator's heavy tails and volatility clustering.

--- Paragraph introduction.P2 (introduction.tex:29) ---
[introduction.P2.S1] The transformation is useful only if each generator reproduces the behavior of its own asset.
[introduction.P2.S2] We illustrate the features a generator must capture with the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY, which also supplied the market path in our multi-asset experiments.
[introduction.P2.S3] Throughout the paper, including in the generators and the regression, returns are excess growth rates.
[introduction.P2.S4] Each day's log price change is multiplied by $252$ trading days to express it as an annual rate, and a constant annual risk-free rate is subtracted.
[introduction.P2.S5] For SPY from $2014$--$2024$, these growth rates show heavy tails and volatility clustering (Fig.
[introduction.P2.S6] [fig:empirical_motivation]).
[introduction.P2.S7] Most growth rates lie close to zero, but extreme values occur much more often than a Gaussian model predicts (Fig.
[introduction.P2.S8] [fig:empirical_motivation]a,b), a widely documented feature of financial returns.
[introduction.P2.S9] Raw growth rates show little correlation across days, while absolute growth rates remain positively correlated across many lags (Fig.
[introduction.P2.S10] [fig:empirical_motivation]c,d).
[introduction.P2.S11] Large and small absolute growth rates therefore cluster in time, a property that synthetic financial data often miss even when their return histogram appears realistic.

--- Paragraph introduction.P3 (introduction.tex:49) ---
[introduction.P3.S1] A hidden Markov model (HMM) offers an interpretable way to represent both features: it draws growth rates from state-dependent distributions and uses transitions between states to represent temporal behavior.
[introduction.P3.S2] In the model used here, the states are ordered bins of growth rates, from the most negative to the most positive.
[introduction.P3.S3] Each observed day's state is read from its bin rather than inferred, and transition probabilities come from counting day-to-day moves between bins.
[introduction.P3.S4] Each state draws growth rates from a Student-$t$ distribution whose location and scale come from the days in its bin; because that distribution is unbounded, simulated values can fall outside the bin.
[introduction.P3.S5] The chain remembers only the current day's bin, not whether recent days were volatile.
[introduction.P3.S6] Fitted to SPY, the model leaves the extreme-return states at either end quickly, and the autocorrelation of its absolute growth rates decays geometrically with the lag, faster than in the data.
[introduction.P3.S7] A duration mechanism that occasionally holds the model in the extreme-return states for several consecutive days can slow that decay.
[introduction.P3.S8] Adding the market component, however, changes the distribution and temporal behavior of the composed returns, so an improvement from such a mechanism, whether added to SPY's generator or to the asset generators, must be checked again after composition.

--- Paragraph introduction.P4 (introduction.tex:73) ---
[introduction.P4.S1] In this study, we developed a variance correction for combining full-return generators, each fitted to one asset, with a shared market path (Fig.
[introduction.P4.S2] [fig:preservation]; Table [tab:aggregate]).
[introduction.P4.S3] The correction first centered each simulated full-return path by subtracting its own mean over the simulated days.
[introduction.P4.S4] The centered path, used as the residual, therefore summed to zero over those days, so the intercept and market component alone set the composed mean.
[introduction.P4.S5] The same constraint removes asset-specific uncertainty from each path's cumulative return over the simulated days.
[introduction.P4.S6] The correction then multiplied the centered path by a factor below one.
[introduction.P4.S7] For each simulated path, the factor was chosen so that the market component's variance plus the scaled path's variance equaled the path's variance before scaling, which represents the asset's total variance.
[introduction.P4.S8] Because each asset path was simulated independently of the market path, their sample covariance was small, and the composed variance approximately equaled that total.
[introduction.P4.S9] We applied the correction to heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators for the other $423$ assets in the $424$-asset universe, first without the duration mechanism to isolate the correction.
[introduction.P4.S10] The market path came from SPY: observed in the $2014$--$2024$ training comparison, and simulated from SPY's fitted generator in the $2025$ holdout so that no $2025$ data entered the simulation.
[introduction.P4.S11] We compared the correction with naive composition, which only centered the paths, and with Gaussian residuals whose variance came from the regression.
[introduction.P4.S12] We also compared it with three methods that fitted a separate generator to each asset's regression residuals: a hidden Markov generator, a block bootstrap, and a generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity model.
[introduction.P4.S13] This main comparison measured distributional fit, variance, and Value-at-Risk thresholds.
[introduction.P4.S14] Volatility clustering of the composed paths, which the duration mechanism targets, was measured in a separate comparison with and without that mechanism.

--- Paragraph introduction.P5 (introduction.tex:101) ---
[introduction.P5.S1] The Kolmogorov--Smirnov pass rate measured distributional fit as the share of simulated paths that a two-sample test at the $5%$ level did not distinguish from an asset's observed growth rates.
[introduction.P5.S2] In the training period, the correction passed the Kolmogorov--Smirnov test far more often than naive composition and kept heavy tails, whereas paths with Gaussian residuals had much lower kurtosis (Table [tab:aggregate]).
[introduction.P5.S3] The residual-fit hidden Markov generator had a slightly higher training pass rate than the correction.
[introduction.P5.S4] With all $2014$--$2024$ fits held fixed, we then compared the composed paths with the $416$ of the $423$ assets that had complete $2025$ histories.
[introduction.P5.S5] Against naive composition, the correction raised the mean pass rate across assets and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance from well above one to close to one (Table [tab:oos_composition]).
[introduction.P5.S6] In this holdout, the residual-fit hidden Markov generator had a slightly lower pass rate than the correction, and every other comparator had a lower pass rate than either.
[introduction.P5.S7] One-day Value-at-Risk checks at the $95%$ and $99%$ levels counted how often each asset's observed $2025$ losses exceeded thresholds estimated from the simulated paths (Table [tab:var]).
[introduction.P5.S8] For both the correction and the residual-fit hidden Markov generator, exceedances were slightly more frequent than the nominal $5%$ and $1%$ rates.
[introduction.P5.S9] Because the assets shared one market and holdout days were not independent, we treated these rates as descriptive rather than as a formal test of calibration.

--- Paragraph introduction.P6 (introduction.tex:125) ---
[introduction.P6.S1] We assessed the SPY hidden Markov generator in more detail, together with a jump-duration variant that implements the duration mechanism (Table [tab:model_comparison]).
[introduction.P6.S2] In this variant, a jump is not an instantaneous price change but an episode.
[introduction.P6.S3] With a small probability each day, the model entered an episode lasting a random number of consecutive days.
[introduction.P6.S4] On each episode day, the state was drawn from either the most negative or the most positive states, with the side chosen at random each day.
[introduction.P6.S5] In the SPY training period, the variant had a smaller autocorrelation error, the gap between simulated and observed autocorrelations of absolute growth rates, than the model without jumps; in the $2025$ holdout, the two errors were similar.
[introduction.P6.S6] The main comparison excluded jumps, so the separate comparison tested whether this training improvement carried over to the composed paths (Table [tab:jump_ablation]).
[introduction.P6.S7] The main comparison used observed SPY in training, whereas this one simulated the market path from the SPY generator in both periods, so that jumps in that generator could affect the market component.
[introduction.P6.S8] It contrasted no jumps with two configurations: jumps in the SPY generator only, and jumps in that generator and every asset generator.
[introduction.P6.S9] Both configurations used the jump parameters calibrated on SPY's $2014$--$2024$ data, without recalibration for each asset, and each configuration was run with both naive and corrected composition.

--- Paragraph introduction.P7 (introduction.tex:146) ---
[introduction.P7.S1] Both jump configurations reduced the autocorrelation error of the composed paths in training and raised it in the $2025$ holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation]).
[introduction.P7.S2] Without jumps, the correction left the autocorrelation error essentially where naive composition left it, so the rescaling neither damaged nor improved clustering.
[introduction.P7.S3] With jumps, the composed variance still matched the generator variance to within a fraction of a percent on average.
[introduction.P7.S4] Across these experiments, the correction removed the double-counted market variance, kept heavy tails, and left clustering where naive composition left it.
[introduction.P7.S5] These results support reuse as a practical option when full-return generators already exist, with residual fitting as a competitive alternative.
[introduction.P7.S6] Neither reuse nor residual fitting, as tested here, modeled dependence among the residuals of different assets, which portfolio evaluation also requires.
