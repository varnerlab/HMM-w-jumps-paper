Synthetic multi-asset equity data must reproduce each asset's return distribution and its relationship with the market. Reusing a generator fitted to full asset returns creates a problem: adding its draws to a market factor counts market variance twice. We derived a correction that centers and rescales each draw over a fixed horizon before adding the market factor, allowing reuse without fitting a second generator to regression residuals. We tested the correction on $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset United States equity and exchange-traded-fund universe, using hidden Markov generators with heavy-tailed emissions. The corrected paths retained heavy tails and recovered the calibrated market loadings with low error. On $416$ complete asset histories held out from $2025$, the correction improved the mean Kolmogorov--Smirnov pass rate over naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one. Its one-day left-tail $99%$ Value-at-Risk exceedance rate, using thresholds pooled across separate simulated paths, was $1.12%$, compared with $1.16%$ for the residual-fit hidden Markov comparator and a nominal rate of $1%$. We also tested a jump-duration mechanism that extended visits to extreme-return states and improved volatility clustering for the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY. The multi-asset correction remained effective with jumps enabled, but transferring the SPY jump settings improved temporal fit in training and worsened it in the $2025$ holdout. The method provides a way to reuse fitted asset generators for daily-fit comparisons. Its zero-sum residual constraint removes terminal residual uncertainty, and unmodeled dependence among asset-specific residuals can lead to overstated rebalancing returns. Code, cached inputs, result summaries, and instructions for fitting the per-asset models accompany the paper.

Researchers and practitioners use synthetic equity paths to test risk models, evaluate portfolio strategies, and examine market scenarios beyond those observed in a historical sample. A multi-asset simulator must represent both the behavior of each asset and the relationships among assets. Factor models link many assets through a shared market path, a time series of market returns. The simplest of these, the single-index model, separates each asset's return into an intercept, a market component scaled by the asset's fitted loading, and a zero-mean asset-specific residual. A synthetic asset path can then be composed by adding a simulated residual to the intercept and market component. If single-asset generators have already been fitted to each asset's full returns, their paths might be reused as residuals. Each generator, however, already represents its asset's total variance, including the part driven by the market. Adding its paths to the market component therefore counts market variance a second time. Subtracting each path's mean removes its mean shift but leaves this excess variance. Fitting a second generator to each asset's regression residuals avoids the problem but repeats the fitting for every asset. Reusing the existing generators would avoid that second fit, provided their paths could be transformed to supply only the residual contribution. The question is whether such a transformation can control the mean and variance of the composed paths while retaining the generator's heavy tails and volatility clustering.

The transformation is useful only if each generator reproduces the behavior of its own asset. We illustrate the features a generator must capture with the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY, which also supplied the market path in our multi-asset experiments. Its $2014$--$2024$ returns show heavy tails and volatility clustering (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]). Most returns lie close to zero, but extreme values occur much more often than a Gaussian model predicts (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]a,b), a widely documented feature of financial returns. Raw returns show little correlation across days, while absolute returns remain positively correlated across many lags (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]c,d). Large and small absolute returns therefore cluster in time, a property that synthetic financial data often miss even when their return histogram appears realistic. To represent heavy tails and volatility clustering, a hidden Markov model (HMM) draws returns from state-dependent distributions and uses transitions between states to represent temporal behavior. Each state in the model used here has a heavy-tailed Student-$t$ return distribution, and the next state depends only on the current state, not on earlier returns. Fitted to SPY, the model leaves states with the most negative or positive mean returns quickly, and the autocorrelation of its absolute returns decays faster than in the data. A duration mechanism that occasionally keeps drawing from extreme-return states for consecutive days can slow that decay. Adding the market component, however, changes the distribution and temporal behavior of the composed returns. An improvement in a single-asset generator must therefore be checked again after composition.

In this study, we developed a variance correction to reuse full-return generators in a single-index model without counting market variance twice. The correction centered and rescaled each simulated path to remove the mean shift and account for the variance supplied by the market component (Fig. [fig:preservation]). We applied it to heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators for the $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset universe, using $2014$--$2024$ for fitting and $2025$ as a holdout. We compared the correction with naive composition, which centered the paths without rescaling, with Gaussian residuals, and with generators fitted directly to regression residuals. The correction improved agreement with observed return distributions over naive composition while retaining heavy tails in training (Table [tab:aggregate]). With all fits held fixed, the correction also improved distributional fit over naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one on the $416$ assets with complete holdout histories (Table [tab:oos_composition]). The residual-fit hidden Markov generator and the correction gave similar agreement with observed return distributions in both periods. Without the duration mechanism, the correction left absolute-return autocorrelation error essentially unchanged from naive composition (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). Transferring SPY's duration settings to the composed paths improved their autocorrelation agreement in training but worsened it in the holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). The correction imposed a zero residual sum over each simulated path, removing cumulative residual uncertainty at the path endpoint. Neither reuse nor residual fitting modeled dependence among different assets' residuals, which portfolio evaluation also requires. These results support reuse as a practical option for daily-return comparisons when full-return generators already exist.

[conclusion.P1.S1] We developed a variance-corrected single-index method that centers and rescales full-return draws for reuse without counting market variance twice or fitting separate residual generators (Fig.

[conclusion.P1.S3] The method retained heavy tails and improved distributional fit over centered naive composition in training and the $2025$ holdout (Table [tab:oos_composition]), but did not establish an advantage over direct residual fitting.
[conclusion.P1.S4] Transferring SPY's jump settings improved temporal fit in training but worsened it in the holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation]), showing that single-asset temporal gains did not transfer reliably.
[conclusion.P1.S5] These findings support reuse for daily-return comparisons, without establishing calibrated tail coverage (Table [tab:var]) or validating broader risk applications.
[conclusion.P1.S6] Independent residual draws omitted cross-asset dependence, and per-path centering removed residual uncertainty from terminal cumulative log returns conditional on the market path and the loading used in composition.
[conclusion.P1.S7] Portfolio and cumulative-risk applications therefore require a joint residual model and a construction that retains cumulative residual variation.
