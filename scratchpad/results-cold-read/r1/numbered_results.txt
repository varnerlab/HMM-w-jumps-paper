
\subsection{Single-asset generator validation}


--- Paragraph results.P1 (results.tex:3) ---
[results.P1.S1] We first characterized the SPY series and the HMM-WJ calibrated to its training window (Fig.
[results.P1.S2] [fig:empirical_motivation]; Table [tab:descriptive_stats]).
[results.P1.S3] The observed series had heavy tails and persistent absolute-return autocorrelation in both the training and holdout windows.
[results.P1.S4] Individual raw-return correlations were small, although the Ljung--Box test detected joint serial dependence (Table [tab:descriptive_stats]).
[results.P1.S5] Under the counted transition matrix, every state had an expected residence time of only $1$--$2$ steps, and the implied absolute-return autocorrelation decayed to near zero by lag $5$ (Fig.
[results.P1.S6] [fig:model_internals]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]).
[results.P1.S7] Jump episodes extended visits to the tail states and so provided a mechanism for more persistent volatility (Algorithm [alg:hmmwj]).
[results.P1.S8] Calibration against the $2014$--$2024$ history selected mean episode duration $\lambda=90$ and jump probability $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ (Eq.
[results.P1.S9] Equation (eq:grid_search); Table [tab:hyperparams]).
[results.P1.S10] Because $\epsilon$ mainly sets how many paths contain an episode and the objective scored only jump-active paths, the objective depended only weakly on $\epsilon$, and the search selected its smallest grid value (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]).
[results.P1.S11] The duration $\lambda$ was likewise a fitted parameter of the objective, not an estimate of how long observed high-volatility periods last.

--- Paragraph results.P2 (results.tex:28) ---
[results.P2.S1] We compared the calibrated HMM-WJ with seven alternative generators on the training window, using $1,000$ paths of $2,766$ trading days (Table [tab:model_comparison]; Fig.
[results.P2.S2] [fig:model_comparison]).
[results.P2.S3] Three alternatives drew independent returns: bootstrap resampling of the training returns with replacement, and Gaussian and Laplace distributions fitted to them.
[results.P2.S4] The other four were HMM-NJ, a GARCH(1,1) model with Gaussian innovations, the GRU benchmark, and the HSMM baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla.
[results.P2.S5] The HMM-NJ and HMM-WJ models had KS pass rates within about one percentage point of the fitted Laplace baseline but came much closer to the observed kurtosis, which Laplace underestimated at $3.0$ against $7.7$.
[results.P2.S6] Both HMM variants had lower Wasserstein-1 distances than Laplace, whereas Laplace had the lowest Hellinger distance among the fitted generators, so the ranking depended on the metric.
[results.P2.S7] Bootstrap resampling passed every KS test because it reused the observed returns, while Gaussian sampling passed neither distribution test.
[results.P2.S8] The GARCH(1,1) model had the lowest mean absolute error between the observed and ensemble-average absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE) but rarely passed the distribution tests, and the GRU showed the same tradeoff.
[results.P2.S9] The HSMM baseline passed only $82%$ of KS tests and underestimated kurtosis by more than one third, and its ACF-MAE equaled the HMM-NJ value despite its flexible state durations.
[results.P2.S10] A separate ablation within HMM-WJ isolated the emission distribution:
[results.P2.S11] Student-$t$ emissions produced kurtosis of $7.5$, close to the observed $7.7$, whereas Normal ($\nu=\infty$) emissions underestimated it by about $29%$ (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]).
[results.P2.S12] Both HMM variants also reproduced the observed skewness of $-0.75$ without a skewness parameter, because the state means and scales estimated from the asymmetric series carried its asymmetry into the mixture (Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]; Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).

\subsection{Variance-corrected multi-asset composition}

[results.P16.S4] As $\rho_i$ grows, the market's shape therefore makes up more of the blend, and the composed distribution departs further from the asset's observed one; Online Appendix [sec:supp-residual-choice] derives this dilution for kurtosis.
[results.P16.S5] A more volatile market raises $\rho_i$ in the same way and, once $\rho_i$ exceeds $1-f$, activates clipping, which the observed market variance never did.
[results.P16.S6] To measure when clipping activates, we multiplied deviations of the observed training SPY path from its mean by $\gamma\in\{1,2,3\}$, which preserved the market mean and multiplied its standard deviation by $\gamma$.
[results.P16.S7] We then recomposed all $423$ assets with the scaled path's variance in the correction, holding the fitted marginals, intercepts, and calibrated loadings fixed (Table [tab:stress]).
[results.P16.S8] Clipping applied to no composed path at $\gamma=1$ and to $76.4%$ and $95.2%$ of composed paths at $\gamma=2$ and $3$.
[results.P16.S9] Because clipped paths retained their fitted intercepts while the market mean stayed unchanged and nonzero, their means shifted by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$ by construction.
[results.P16.S10] The stress test therefore characterized when clipping activated and how it moved asset means; it did not evaluate fit under the scaled markets.
