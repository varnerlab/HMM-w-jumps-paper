Synthetic multi-asset equity data must reproduce each asset's return distribution and its relationship with the market. Reusing a generator fitted to full asset returns creates a problem: adding its draws to a market factor counts market variance twice. We derived a correction that centers and rescales each draw over a fixed horizon before adding the market factor, allowing reuse without fitting a second generator to regression residuals. We tested the correction on $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset United States equity and exchange-traded-fund universe, using hidden Markov generators with heavy-tailed emissions. The corrected paths retained heavy tails and recovered the calibrated market loadings with low error. On $416$ complete asset histories held out from $2025$, the correction improved the mean Kolmogorov--Smirnov pass rate over naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one. Its one-day left-tail $99%$ Value-at-Risk exceedance rate, using thresholds pooled across separate simulated paths, was $1.12%$, compared with $1.16%$ for the residual-fit hidden Markov comparator and a nominal rate of $1%$. We also tested a jump-duration mechanism that extended visits to extreme-return states and improved volatility clustering for the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY. The multi-asset correction remained effective with jumps enabled, but transferring the SPY jump settings improved temporal fit in training and worsened it in the $2025$ holdout. The method provides a way to reuse fitted asset generators for daily-fit comparisons. Its zero-sum residual constraint removes terminal residual uncertainty, and unmodeled dependence among asset-specific residuals can lead to overstated rebalancing returns. Code, cached inputs, result summaries, and instructions for fitting the per-asset models accompany the paper.

Researchers and practitioners use synthetic equity paths to test risk models, evaluate portfolio strategies, and examine market scenarios beyond those observed in a historical sample. A multi-asset simulator must represent both the behavior of each asset and the relationships among assets. Factor models link many assets through a shared market path, a time series of market returns. The simplest of these, the single-index model, separates each asset's return into an intercept, a market component scaled by the asset's fitted loading, and a zero-mean asset-specific residual. A synthetic asset path can then be composed by adding a simulated residual to the intercept and market component. If single-asset generators have already been fitted to each asset's full returns, their paths might be reused as residuals. Each generator, however, already represents its asset's total variance, including the part driven by the market. Adding its paths to the market component therefore counts market variance a second time. Subtracting each path's mean removes its mean shift but leaves this excess variance. Fitting a second generator to each asset's regression residuals avoids the problem but repeats the fitting for every asset. Reusing the existing generators would avoid that second fit, provided their paths could be transformed to supply only the residual contribution. The question is whether such a transformation can control the mean and variance of the composed paths while retaining the generator's heavy tails and volatility clustering.

The transformation is useful only if each generator reproduces the behavior of its own asset. We illustrate the features a generator must capture with the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY, which also supplied the market path in our multi-asset experiments. Its $2014$--$2024$ returns show heavy tails and volatility clustering (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]). Most returns lie close to zero, but extreme values occur much more often than a Gaussian model predicts (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]a,b), a widely documented feature of financial returns. Raw returns show little correlation across days, while absolute returns remain positively correlated across many lags (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]c,d). Large and small absolute returns therefore cluster in time, a property that synthetic financial data often miss even when their return histogram appears realistic. To represent heavy tails and volatility clustering, a hidden Markov model (HMM) draws returns from state-dependent distributions and uses transitions between states to represent temporal behavior. Each state in the model used here has a heavy-tailed Student-$t$ return distribution, and the next state depends only on the current state, not on earlier returns. Fitted to SPY, the model leaves states with the most negative or positive mean returns quickly, and the autocorrelation of its absolute returns decays faster than in the data. A duration mechanism that occasionally keeps drawing from extreme-return states for consecutive days can slow that decay. Adding the market component, however, changes the distribution and temporal behavior of the composed returns. An improvement in a single-asset generator must therefore be checked again after composition.

In this study, we developed a variance correction to reuse full-return generators in a single-index model without counting market variance twice. The correction centered and rescaled each simulated path to remove the mean shift and account for the variance supplied by the market component (Fig. [fig:preservation]). We applied it to heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators for the $423$ non-market assets in a $424$-asset universe, using $2014$--$2024$ for fitting and $2025$ as a holdout. We compared the correction with naive composition, which centered the paths without rescaling, with Gaussian residuals, and with generators fitted directly to regression residuals. The correction improved agreement with observed return distributions over naive composition while retaining heavy tails in training (Table [tab:aggregate]). With all fits held fixed, the correction also improved distributional fit over naive composition and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one on the $416$ assets with complete holdout histories (Table [tab:oos_composition]). The residual-fit hidden Markov generator and the correction gave similar agreement with observed return distributions in both periods. Without the duration mechanism, the correction left absolute-return autocorrelation error essentially unchanged from naive composition (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). Transferring SPY's duration settings to the composed paths improved their autocorrelation agreement in training but worsened it in the holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). The correction imposed a zero residual sum over each simulated path, removing cumulative residual uncertainty at the path endpoint. Neither reuse nor residual fitting modeled dependence among different assets' residuals, which portfolio evaluation also requires. These results support reuse as a practical option for daily-return comparisons when full-return generators already exist.

Single-asset return models treat changing volatility, market regimes, and extreme moves in different ways. Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) and generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models allow conditional variance to change over time. Even with Gaussian innovations, the changing variance can produce an unconditional return distribution with excess kurtosis. Heavy-tailed innovations can improve the tail fit when Gaussian GARCH understates the frequency of extreme returns. Stochastic-volatility models make volatility a latent process. Jump-diffusion models add discontinuous price moves, but a jump component alone does not explain persistent volatility. Markov-switching ARCH and GARCH models combine discrete regimes with changing conditional variance. Hidden-state models represent returns with a small number of market regimes. In a hidden Markov model (HMM), each latent state has a separate return distribution. Gaussian-emission HMMs can reproduce heavy tails and weak raw-return autocorrelation, but may still miss volatility clustering. Hidden semi-Markov models allow more flexible state durations and can improve volatility persistence. An HMM does not create a jump process by itself. Rare extreme moves must come from the state-dependent return distributions or from a transition rule designed for such events. Deep generative models learn return distributions and temporal features without specifying a small set of market regimes or a parametric return process. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are one class of deep generative model. Quant GANs have reproduced several financial time-series features, including volatility clustering and leverage effects. Sig-Wasserstein GANs use path signatures to learn features that depend on the order of observations. In contrast, evaluations of GAN paths, including paths from the time-series generative adversarial network (TimeGAN), have found too little autocorrelation in absolute returns. The conflicting results show that flexible architectures do not guarantee a match to financial stylized facts. Model design, training, and evaluation all matter.

Multi-asset generation also requires a model of cross-asset dependence. Multivariate GARCH models estimate time-varying covariance matrices. The dynamic conditional correlation model reduces the number of parameters by separating individual volatility models from correlation dynamics. Copulas separate each asset's return distribution from the dependence model. Dynamic copulas allow cross-asset dependence to change over time, while vine copulas provide flexible high-dimensional constructions. Factor models reduce dimension by linking each asset to a small number of shared factors. Shared-state HMMs and multivariate GANs can also generate joint market moves. A standard factor model can resample historical residuals or fit a separate residual generator for every asset. This paper addresses a narrower problem. We start with single-asset generators already fitted to full returns and ask whether they can be reused in a factor model. The relevant comparison is with methods fitted directly to regression residuals: those methods remove the market component before fitting, whereas reuse adjusts an existing full-return draw during composition. Neither approach specifies cross-asset residual dependence unless that dependence is modeled separately.

For each asset, the hidden Markov generator divided the range of growth rates, which we also call returns, into $N=100$ ordered intervals and treated the interval that contained a day's return as that day's state (Table [tab:hyperparams]). We write $t$ for the day index $j$ and omit the ticker index $i$ when discussing one asset. At each simulated time step, the model first selected a state and then drew a return from that state's distribution (Fig. [fig:model_architecture]). We represented the model as $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathbf{T}, \mathbf{E}, \bar{\pi})$ . The hidden state $S_t \in \mathcal{S} = \{1,\dots,N\}$ identifies the interval on trading day $t$, and the observation space $\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}$ contains the excess growth rates $G_t$ of Eq. Equation (eq:growth_rate). The transition matrix $\mathbf{T}$ sets the probabilities of moving between states, $\mathbf{E}$ gives the return distribution within each state, and $\bar{\pi}$ gives the initial state probabilities. We ordered the states from the most negative to the most positive returns and placed their boundaries at the quantiles of a Laplace distribution, fitted to the asset's in-sample returns, at the equally spaced probability levels $k/N$. We used the Laplace distribution because its sharp peak matches the concentration of small price movements in Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]a . Its quantile function also has a closed form, so the boundaries for all $424$ tickers required no iterative fitting. Specifically, $Q_k = F_L^{-1}(k/N;\, \mu_L, b_L)$, where $F_L^{-1}$ is the Laplace quantile function and $\mu_L$ and $b_L$ are the fitted location and scale. We set $Q_0=-\infty$ and $Q_N=+\infty$ and assigned return $G_t$ to state $k$ when $Q_{k-1}\le G_t<Q_k$. Because each training return determines its state, the fitted state sequence is known; the states are hidden only in simulated paths, where the model generates the state sequence and reports only the returns.

The within-state emission followed a location-scale Student-$t$ distribution: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} G_t \mid S_t = k \;\sim\; \mu_k + \sigma_k \cdot t_{\nu}, \qquad \nu = 5,  \end{equation} where $t_\nu$ denotes a standard Student-$t$ random variable with $\nu$ degrees of freedom. For observations assigned to state $k$, we used the sample mean for $\mu_k$ and the sample standard deviation for $\sigma_k$, subject to two fallback rules. A state with fewer than two observations used the mean and standard deviation of that asset's full training series. A state with at least two observations and sample standard deviation below $10^{-12}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ retained its state mean but used the full-series standard deviation as its scale. These exceptions kept emission parameters defined but could broaden states containing repeated returns (Online Appendix [sec:supp-fallback]); neither applied to SPY, whose states all held many observations. The same rules applied to the generators that the multi-asset comparison fitted to regression residuals. A standard $t_\nu$ has variance $\nu/(\nu-2)$, so $\sigma_k$ is a scale parameter rather than the emission standard deviation. At $\nu=5$, each state's emission standard deviation is $\sigma_k\sqrt{5/3}$, larger than the sample standard deviation of the returns assigned to that state. The emission is not truncated to the state's interval, so a simulated return can fall outside it. We selected $\nu = 5$ from a sensitivity sweep and used $\nu = \infty$, which gives Normal emissions, as a baseline; Table [tab:hyperparams] gives the explored range. For SPY, holding the rest of the model fixed, the Student-$t$ emission raised simulated kurtosis toward the observed value and raised the Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) and Anderson--Darling (AD) pass rates relative to the Normal emission (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]). A pass rate is the percentage of simulated paths whose two-sample test against the observed returns gave $p>0.05$.

We estimated the transition matrix $\mathbf{T}$ by counting observed transitions between consecutive states and dividing each row by its total count. A state with no observed outgoing transition used a uniform row, $\mathbf{T}_{kj}=1/N$ for every destination state $j$. Because the training state sequence is known, these row frequencies are the maximum-likelihood estimates, and counting avoided the initialization sensitivity of iterative expectation-maximization. We obtained the initial-state distribution $\bar{\pi}$ by solving $\bar{\pi}\mathbf{T}=\bar{\pi}$ with $\sum_k\bar{\pi}_k=1$. Because $\mathbf{T}$ was estimated by counting, $\bar{\pi}$ equals the vector of in-sample state frequencies up to end effects, so the model's stationary growth-rate distribution is the frequency-weighted mixture of the state emission distributions. The counted probability of remaining in the same state was low (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-nojump]), but volatility clustering requires the chain to stay in the tail region, not in one state. Counting reproduces the observed frequencies of consecutive state pairs. At longer lags, however, the chain's absolute-return autocorrelation decays geometrically at a rate set by the counted matrix, much faster than the slow decay in Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]d. The counted chain therefore forgets within a few steps whether it was in the tails and cannot reproduce the observed volatility clustering. Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator] derives the fitted generator's stationary law and autocorrelation structure and quantifies both this rapid mixing and the autocorrelation added by the jump mechanism.

We added jump episodes to extend visits to extreme-return states and call the resulting generator the hidden Markov model with jumps (HMM-WJ). When no jump episode was active, a jump was triggered with probability $\epsilon$ at each step, and its duration was drawn as $K\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the mean episode length. A draw of $K=0$ produced no episode, and the chain made an ordinary transition. For $K>0$, the episode consisted of $K$ \emph{forced tail steps}, beginning with the step that triggered it. At each forced step, the model replaced the ordinary transition by first choosing one of two tail sets and then drawing a state uniformly from that set. The set $\mathcal{S}_{-}=\{1,\dots,N_{\rm tail}\}$ contained the most negative states, and $\mathcal{S}_{+}=\{N-N_{\rm tail}+1,\dots,N\}$ contained the most positive states. The model chose the negative set with probability $p_{\rm neg}$ and the positive set otherwise. Because $p_{\rm neg}$ is slightly above one half, negative extreme returns occurred slightly more often than positive ones. The choice was repeated at each forced step, so one episode can contain returns of both signs. Table [tab:hyperparams] lists $\epsilon$, $\lambda$, $p_{\rm neg}$, and $N_{\rm tail}$; we tuned $\epsilon$ and $\lambda$ and fixed $p_{\rm neg}$ and $N_{\rm tail}$. Jump-diffusion models use Poisson arrivals to add instantaneous price jumps. Our model instead uses the Poisson distribution for the episode length, and an episode is a sustained visit to the tail states rather than a single price move. Setting $\epsilon=0$ removes the mechanism; we call this variant the hidden Markov model with no jumps (HMM-NJ). Two closed-form approximations describe how much of a path, and how many paths, the mechanism affects (Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]). Forced steps occupy a long-run fraction of approximately $\epsilon\lambda/(1+\epsilon\lambda)$ of all steps. A path of $T$ steps contains at least one episode with probability approximately $1 - e^{-\epsilon T}$, so $\epsilon$ sets how many paths contain an episode, while $\lambda$ sets how long an episode lasts. The jump-augmented process is Markov only in the pair formed by the state and the number of remaining forced steps. The solved $\bar{\pi}$ is therefore the exact stationary distribution of the ordinary chain but only an approximate stationary start for the jump-augmented process. The full simulation procedure appears in Algorithm [alg:hmmwj]. Each step followed $\mathbf{T}$ or continued a jump episode and then drew a return from the selected state's emission distribution.

We tuned the jump probability $\epsilon$ and mean duration $\lambda$ on the volume-weighted SPY training series to match its absolute-return autocorrelation and excess kurtosis; the multi-asset comparisons either disabled jumps or transferred the SPY values to the closing-price generators (Table [tab:hyperparams]). A path with no episode follows HMM-NJ, so its autocorrelation and kurtosis errors do not depend on $(\epsilon,\lambda)$. We scored only \emph{jump-active} paths, those containing at least one episode, so the objective measures how well an episode reproduces the observed statistics, not how often episodes occur. At each point of a grid of $(\epsilon,\lambda)$ values, we simulated $200$ independent paths of $2,766$ trading days. We minimized an objective that averaged, over the jump-active paths, the squared errors in the autocorrelation function (ACF) of absolute returns through lag $L$ plus a weighted squared error in excess kurtosis: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} J(\epsilon, \lambda) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_J|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_J} \left[ \sum_{\tau=1}^{L} \left( \mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau) - \mathrm{ACF}_{r}(\tau) \right)^2 + w_\kappa \left( \kappa_{\rm obs} - \kappa_{r} \right)^2 \right],  \end{equation} where $\mathcal{R}_J$ is the set of jump-active simulated paths, $\mathrm{ACF}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ and $\kappa_{\rm obs}$ are the empirical absolute-growth autocorrelation at lag $\tau$ and excess kurtosis, $\mathrm{ACF}_{r}(\tau)$ and $\kappa_r$ are the corresponding values for path $r$, and $w_\kappa$ sets the relative weight of the kurtosis error. When $\epsilon$ is small, as at the selected value, most jump-active paths contain a single episode, whose forced steps make up a fraction of about $\lambda/T$ of the path. The episode adds a slowly decaying component to the absolute-return autocorrelation, and $\lambda$ sets both its height and the number of lags over which it persists. The kurtosis penalty limits $\lambda$, because forced steps make tail returns common within a path rather than rare; they raise the variance proportionally more than the fourth moment and so lower the path kurtosis. The parameter $\epsilon$ acts mainly through the share of paths that contain an episode, which the restriction to jump-active paths removes. The objective therefore constrains $\epsilon$ only weakly, and the selected $\epsilon$ lay at the lower edge of its search range (Table [tab:hyperparams]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]). The search ranges, $w_\kappa$, $L$, and the remaining hyperparameters are reported in Table [tab:hyperparams]. We fixed the selected hyperparameters before running the generator comparisons and the holdout evaluation.

Beyond the Normal-emission variant, we compared HMM-WJ with two benchmarks that model persistence differently. A hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla lengthens state visits through explicit dwell-time distributions instead of jump episodes (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-emission-hsmm]). The Normal-emission comparison isolated the effect of heavy-tailed emissions, and the HSMM comparison contrasted explicit dwell times with the jump-duration mechanism, although the HSMM also used $8$ states rather than $100$ and so differed in more than its duration model. To compare the hidden Markov generator with a neural autoregressive model, we included a gated recurrent unit (GRU) benchmark (Table [tab:model_comparison]). The network took a $50$-return input window and had two recurrent layers of $64$ hidden units each. We standardized the training series with its mean and standard deviation, and separate output heads predicted the mean and log variance of the next standardized return. We fitted the network on training SPY only, selected weights by training loss, and sampled recursively from the predicted Gaussian distributions. Online Appendix [sec:supp-gru] specifies the optimizer, stopping rule, initialization, and recorded computational environment. We scored all single-asset generators, including these benchmarks, by their KS and AD pass rates and by the mean absolute error between the observed and the ensemble-average simulated absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE), over lags $1$--$252$ in training and $1$--$248$ in the $249$-day holdout (Eq. Equation (eq:acf-ensemble-error) in Online Appendix [sec:supp-validation]).

The single-asset model generated each ticker separately and did not specify cross-asset dependence. For the multi-asset model, we added a shared market path as a common factor, using the single-index model (SIM) of Sharpe. We write $g_i(t)$ for the growth rate $G_{i,t}$ of asset $i$ on days $t=1,\dots,T$. The horizon $T$ is $2,766$ days in the training comparisons and $249$ days in the holdout. The same symbols denote observed paths when we estimate parameters and synthetic paths when we compose them: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} g_i(t) \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta_i\, g_m(t) \;+\; \eps_i(t), \qquad t = 1,\dots,T.  \end{equation} Here $\alpha_i$ is the intercept for asset $i$, $g_m$ is the growth-rate path of the market, SPY, $\beta_i$ is the asset's market loading, and $\eps_i$ is its asset-specific residual path. We use $\eps_i$ for the residual path and $\epsilon$ for the jump probability in the single-asset model. We estimated the intercept and market loading $(\alpha_i,\beta_i)$ from observed data by ordinary least squares (OLS). The choice of $\eps_i$ determined the composition method. A standard SIM draws independent Gaussian residuals with the variance estimated by OLS. These Gaussian residuals preserve the fitted market relationship but lack the heavy tails, and with jumps enabled the volatility clustering, that the single-asset generator produces. We called the Gaussian-residual method \emph{Gaussian SIM} and used it as one of the compared methods.

\paragraph{Naive full-path composition.}

Using a full synthetic growth-rate path $\tg_i$ drawn from the asset's own single-asset generator, the \emph{generator draw}, directly as the residual would give: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} g_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i\, g_m + \tg_i. \end{equation} The fitted intercept and market term already set the conditional mean of asset $i$, while the generator draw generally has $\E[\tg_i]\ne 0$. Equation Equation (eq:naive-composition) therefore has conditional mean $\alpha_i+\E[\tg_i]+\beta_i g_m$, not $\alpha_i+\beta_i g_m$. We therefore centered every generator draw before composition, subtracting its realized path mean: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \overline{\tg}_i \equiv \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tg_i(t), \qquad \tg_i^{\mathrm c}(t) \equiv \tg_i(t)-\overline{\tg}_i.  \end{equation} Therefore, $T^{-1}\sum_t\tg_i^{\mathrm c}(t)=0$ exactly. Subtracting the realized rather than the population mean makes the centering exact on every path. Subtracting a constant leaves both the sample variance and the sample covariance with $g_m$ unchanged. We used the centered construction $g_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i g_m+\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$, which corrects the mean but not the variance, as the \emph{naive} benchmark in all comparisons. The sample variance of each draw, $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2=\Var(\tg_i)=\Var(\tg_i^{\mathrm c})$, is the \emph{generator variance}; it is recomputed for every draw. It already represents the asset's total variance, so adding the market term adds market variance a second time. During composition, $g_m$ was the observed SPY path in the training comparison and a path simulated from SPY's closing-price generator in the holdout and jump comparisons, with one market path per replication shared by all assets, and in both cases it was independent of the asset draws. The covariance of a centered draw with the market path is zero in population and close to zero on a long realized path. We approximated the naive composed variance accordingly: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \Var(g_i) \;\approx\; \beta_i^2\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_{\gen,i}^2, \qquad \Cov(\tg_i^{\mathrm c},g_m)\approx 0,  \end{equation} where $\sigma_m^2=\Var(g_m)$ is the sample variance of the market path used in composition. The naive composed variance therefore exceeds the generator variance by $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2$, an excess that grows with the square of the market loading.

\paragraph{Variance correction.}

We set $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ as the target total variance for asset $i$. The market term contributed $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2$, so the residual term could contribute only the remaining variance. We defined $s_i$ as the nonnegative asset-specific residual scale, set $\eps_i=s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$, and required $\Var(\beta_i g_m+s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c})=\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$. Under the zero-covariance assumption, solving for $s_i$ gives the correction: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \boxed{\;s_i^2 \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}\;}  \end{equation} We call the market term's share of the target variance the \emph{market loading ratio}: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \rho_i \;\equiv\; \frac{\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2},  \end{equation} so that $s_i^2=1-\rho_i$ under the zero-covariance assumption. At $\rho_i=1$, the rule of Eq. Equation (eq:scale) leaves no asset-specific variance; for $\rho_i>1$, it has no real-valued solution.

With the observed variances, $\rho_i$ would equal the coefficient of determination of the regression, which is below one. Because $\rho_i$ instead uses the generator and market-path variances, it can reach or exceed one when the generator variance is low or the market path is more volatile than the observed SPY training series, particularly for assets with large $\beta_i$. We therefore required the asset-specific draw to contribute at least a fraction $f$ of the target variance, the \emph{floor}, and set $\rho_i^{\max}=1-f$. We applied the correction through an \emph{effective loading} $\beta_i^{\eff}$, the loading used in composition, which equals $\beta_i$ unless $\rho_i>\rho_i^{\max}$: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \beta_i^{\eff} \;=\; \begin{cases} \beta_i & \text{if } \rho_i \le \rho_i^{\max} \\[4pt] \sign(\beta_i)\,\sqrt{(1 - f)\,\dfrac{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}{\sigma_m^2}} & \text{if } \rho_i > \rho_i^{\max} \end{cases} \qquad s_i^2 \;=\; \begin{cases} 1 - \rho_i & \text{if } \rho_i \le \rho_i^{\max} \\[4pt] f & \text{if } \rho_i > \rho_i^{\max} \end{cases}  \end{equation} We call the second case of Eq. Equation (eq:clip) the \emph{clipped} case. In it, the correction reduced the magnitude of the loading but kept the sign of the calibrated $\beta_i$. We used $f=0.10$, so the asset-specific draw contributed at least $10%$ of the target variance for every asset under this rule (Table [tab:hyperparams]). We retained the fitted intercept $\alpha_i$ when clipping changed the loading. Because the scaled residual had zero sample mean, the composed mean was $\overline{g}_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i^{\eff}\overline{g}_m$, where $\overline{g}_m$ is the mean of the market path. Clipping therefore changed the mean by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$ relative to the calibrated linear mean on that same market path. The clipped case thus kept the intercept and the target variance and set the residual share to exactly $f$, but changed the loading and, unless $\overline{g}_m=0$, the mean. Online Appendix [sec:supp-residual-choice] examines the substitution of a full-return draw for the residual: it derives what the construction preserves exactly and how much of the generator's excess kurtosis survives composition as $\rho_i$ grows.

\paragraph{High-$R^2$ index trackers.}

The coefficient of determination $R^2_{i,\real}$, from the OLS regression of the observed asset on observed SPY, measures the fraction of the asset's variance explained by the market. The rule of Eq. Equation (eq:clip) targets total generator variance, not $R^2_{i,\real}$. Under the zero-covariance assumption, the composed $R^2$ under that rule equals $\rho_i$, capped at $1-f$ by the floor. It therefore cannot exceed $1-f$, and it matches $R^2_{i,\real}$ only when $\sigma_m^2/\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ equals the ratio of the observed SPY variance to the observed asset variance. Index-tracking funds, such as the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ and the S&P 500 growth fund SPYG, have $R^2$ against SPY close to or above the cap $1-f$, so that rule could not reliably reproduce their co-movement with the market. We therefore used two branches: the rule of Eq. Equation (eq:clip), the \emph{variance-preserving branch}, for most assets, and a separate \emph{$R^2$-preserving branch} when $R^2_{i,\real}$ was at least $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$. We set $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}=0.80$, which lies in a wide gap of the observed $R^2$ distribution between the index trackers and all other assets in our universe (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). This branch keeps $\beta_i^{\eff}=\beta_i$ and uses the same market-path variance $\sigma_m^2$ as the variance-preserving branch. We computed the residual variance required to recover $R^2_{i,\real}$ under the zero-covariance assumption: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \boxed{\;\sigma_{\eps,\mathrm{target}}^2 \;=\; \beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2 \cdot \frac{1 - R^2_{i,\real}}{R^2_{i,\real}}\;}  \end{equation} We rescaled the generator draw to the target residual variance: [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \eps_i \;=\; \left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{\eps,\mathrm{target}}^2}{\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}}\,\right)\tg_i^{\mathrm c}.  \end{equation} This branch preserves $R^2_{i,\real}$ rather than the generator variance: the composed variance becomes $\beta_i^2\sigma_m^2/R^2_{i,\real}$. The scale factor in Eq. Equation (eq:r2-rescale) exceeds one when the generator variance is below the target residual variance; the composed variance then exceeds the generator variance.

\paragraph{Complete composition rule.}

The complete rule has three outcomes, recorded by the branch flag $b_i$ in Algorithm [alg:hybrid]. Index trackers used the $R^2$-preserving branch; all other assets used the variance-preserving branch, with the calibrated loading or, in the clipped case, a reduced loading. Each composed path is then $g_i=\alpha_i+\beta_i^{\eff}g_m+\eps_i$. We call this complete rule the \emph{hybrid} composition, the label used in the tables and figures, and all results reported for the variance correction refer to it. Membership in the $R^2$-preserving branch depends only on $R^2_{i,\real}$ and is fixed for each asset. Everything else is computed per draw: because $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ is recomputed for each draw, so are $s_i$, $\rho_i$, and $\beta_i^{\eff}$, and one asset can be clipped in some draws and not in others. Because the draws are independent of the market path, OLS estimates from composed paths converge, as the horizon grows, to the intercept $\alpha_i$ and the effective loading $\beta_i^{\eff}$, which equals $\beta_i$ unless the draw is clipped. The variance target and the loading recovery are exact only when the sample covariance between the draw and the market path is zero; on a finite path, they deviate by that covariance term (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). Because centering and scaling fix the residual sum, every branch imposes a zero residual sum over the horizon $T$, which is stronger than a zero population mean. For a fixed market path and effective loading, the terminal cumulative log return therefore has no asset-specific residual uncertainty, although daily and intermediate returns still vary. In the clipped case, the effective loading depends on the variances of each draw, so it can vary between draws and produce terminal variation through the market term. Because centering and scaling use statistics of the entire draw, the construction also depends on the chosen horizon. We therefore evaluated it as a constrained finite-horizon construction for daily distributional fit and recovery of the market loading; it does not preserve cumulative residual uncertainty.

The construction links assets only through same-day market returns. A large market move can therefore affect several assets together, but the rule does not separately fit lead--lag effects or a common volatility factor in the residuals. Algorithm [alg:hybrid] also allows an optional rank reorder that adds residual dependence across assets; the six-method comparison did not use it. The reorder draws one joint vector per day from a copula fitted to the observed OLS residuals and permutes each asset's residual values over days so that their ranks over time match the ranks of that asset's copula coordinate. The reorder imposes cross-asset residual dependence and keeps each path's values and variance, but it replaces the generator's day-to-day ordering and with it any volatility clustering in the draw.

[DISPLAY] \begin{algorithm}[t] \caption{Hybrid single-index model (SIM) composition with variance correction.}  \small \begin{algorithmic}[1] \REQUIRE Nonconstant $g_m,\tg_i\in\R^T$, $i=1,\dots,N$; each $\tg_i$ is independent of $g_m$. \REQUIRE Parameters $(\alpha_i,\beta_i,R^2_{i,\real})$; floor $f\in(0,1)$; threshold $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}\in(0,1]$. \ENSURE Composed paths $g_i\in\R^T$ and branch flags $b_i$. \STATE $\sigma_m^2 \gets \Var(g_m)$. \FOR{each asset $i = 1,\dots,N$} \STATE $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2 \gets \Var(\tg_i)$; $\tg_i^{\mathrm c} \gets \tg_i-\overline{\tg}_i$. \IF{$R^2_{i,\real} \ge R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$} \STATE $v_i \gets \beta_i^2\sigma_m^2(1-R^2_{i,\real})/R^2_{i,\real}$. \STATE $s_i \gets \sqrt{v_i/\sigma_{\gen,i}^2}$; $\beta_i^{\eff} \gets \beta_i$; $b_i\gets\textsc{r2-preserve}$. \ELSE \STATE $\rho_i \gets \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 / \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ \IF{$\rho_i \le 1 - f$} \STATE $s_i \gets \sqrt{1-\rho_i}$; $\beta_i^{\eff}\gets\beta_i$; $b_i\gets\textsc{variance}$. \ELSE \STATE $s_i\gets\sqrt{f}$; $\beta_i^{\eff}\gets\sign(\beta_i) \sqrt{(1-f)\sigma_{\gen,i}^2/\sigma_m^2}$. \STATE $b_i\gets\textsc{clipped}$. \ENDIF \ENDIF \STATE $\eps_i \gets s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$. \ENDFOR \STATE \textit{Optional:} jointly rank-reorder $\{\eps_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$. \FOR{each asset $i = 1,\dots,N$} \STATE $g_i \gets \alpha_i + \beta_i^{\eff}\, g_m + \eps_i$ \hfill \COMMENT{Eq. Equation (eq:sim-composition)} \ENDFOR \end{algorithmic} \end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}

To test variance correction against alternative residual models, we compared six composition methods using a dataset of $424$ United States equities and exchange-traded funds from Polygon.io (Table [tab:aggregate]). Each ticker had $2,767$ daily closing prices from January $2014$ through December $2024$, yielding $2,766$ growth rates; this complete-case selection conditioned the analysis on survival and data coverage. We fitted the same hidden Markov generator to each asset, including SPY (Table [tab:hyperparams]; $r_f=0$), and regressed the other $423$ assets on observed SPY to estimate $\alpha_i$, $\beta_i$, $R^2_{i,\real}$, and residual variance. All generators had jumps disabled to isolate composition from jump calibration. The naive and hybrid methods shared a centered full-return draw, with variance correction applied only by the hybrid method (Algorithm [alg:hybrid]). Four alternatives generated residuals from a Gaussian distribution, a residual-fit hidden Markov model (labeled JumpHMM-on-residuals), a stationary block bootstrap, or a GARCH(1,1)-$t$ model, and added them to $\alpha_i+\beta_i g_m$ without centering or rescaling. Comparator fitting and eligibility rules are given in Online Appendix [sec:supp-composition-evaluation]; $30$ non-stationary GARCH fits left $393$ eligible assets for that method. Using observed SPY as the common market path, we ran $100$ replications per asset with seed $1234$ and pooled training scores across tickers. We measured factor recovery by the absolute OLS errors $|\hat\alpha-\alpha_i|$, $|\hat\beta-\beta_i|$, and $|\hat R^2-R^2_{i,\real}|$, and naive and hybrid variance preservation by the composed-to-generator variance ratio. Distributional scores were KS and AD pass rates, Wasserstein-1 distance, excess kurtosis, and the Hill index of absolute returns (Online Appendix [sec:supp-validation]). Because only naive and hybrid forced the residual mean to zero, a paired centering control on the common $393$ assets separated this constraint from model fitting (Table [tab:centering_control]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-centering-control]).

To test performance without refitting, we froze all $2014$--$2024$ fits and evaluated $100$ simulated paths per asset against the $249$ growth rates observed in $2025$ (Table [tab:oos_composition]). Complete holdout histories were available for $416$ assets, including $386$ with accepted GARCH fits. Each replication used one simulated $249$-day no-jump SPY path shared across asset compositions. The correction used the observed training market variance in Eqs. Equation (eq:scale)--Equation (eq:r2-rescale), while each asset's generator variance remained draw-specific. Consequently, variation in simulated market variance could move composed variance away from the generator target even at zero sample cross-covariance. Observed $2025$ returns were used only for scoring. We repeated the KS, AD, Wasserstein-1, and kurtosis evaluations and measured variance for all six methods relative to observed $2025$ variance. Because goodness-of-fit tests have less power on short samples, we also compared the same simulated paths with randomly selected contiguous $249$-day blocks of observed training returns as a length-matched training reference. We averaged replications within ticker before summarizing across tickers, giving each asset equal weight.

To estimate one-day Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds without relying on a single short path, we pooled daily log returns, $\Delta t$ times the growth rates, from $5,000$ separately composed $249$-day paths per asset and method (Table [tab:var]). Each replication shared one simulated SPY path across assets and methods and followed the holdout centering and scaling conventions. We fixed the $95%$ and $99%$ coverage thresholds before scoring observed $2025$ returns; no holdout data entered fitting or threshold calibration. These unconditional thresholds describe a uniformly selected day within the $249$-day construction, retain its horizon dependence, and do not remove the zero-sum residual constraint of naive and hybrid paths. We reported mean exceedance rates and cross-ticker standard deviations descriptively, without treating dependent days or assets sharing a market as independent replications. Pool-size convergence and an independent simulation check are reported in Online Appendix [sec:supp-var-ensemble].

\paragraph{Jump-enabled multi-asset comparison.}

To test whether the temporal benefit of the single-asset jump mechanism survived composition, we compared naive and hybrid composition under three jump settings (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). Unlike the six-method comparison, which used observed SPY in training, this comparison simulated the SPY market path in both the $2,766$-day training window and the $249$-day holdout, so a jump setting could apply to the market generator. Jumps were disabled, enabled only in the SPY market generator, or enabled in both SPY and every asset generator. We froze the closing-price hidden Markov generators and SIM calibrations of the six-method comparison. Enabled models received the SPY settings of Table [tab:hyperparams], $(\epsilon,\lambda)=(10^{-4},90)$, $p_{\rm neg}=0.52$, and $N_{\rm tail}=5$, without per-asset tuning. These settings were tuned on volume-weighted SPY prices and applied here to generators fitted to closing prices. At these settings, most paths contain no episode, especially over the $249$-day holdout, so the jump effects are carried by a minority of paths. In both windows and for all three jump settings, the correction used each simulated market path's variance and each asset draw's variance. This differs from the six-method holdout, which fixed the market variance at its training value.

For each jump setting, naive and hybrid composition received the same asset and market draws, and one market path was shared across all assets in each replication. We generated $250$ paths for each of four seeds ($1234$, $2345$, $3456$, and $4567$), giving $1,000$ replications per asset, setting, and method. We used a path-level version of ACF-MAE, computed on each path and then averaged across paths (Eq. Equation (eq:acf-path-error) in Online Appendix [sec:supp-validation]); the single-asset ACF-MAE instead averages the path ACFs before measuring the error. We used lags $1$--$25$, the lag window of the tuning objective, with lags $1$--$60$ as a secondary check, and repeated the marginal and variance diagnostics. The temporal scores of the single-asset and jump comparisons therefore have different estimands and cannot be compared directly. We included all paths, not only jump-active ones, in the summaries; jump-active subsets only described the mechanism (Online Appendix [sec:supp-jump-composition]). For Monte Carlo uncertainty, we averaged each metric across tickers within each replication, which kept assets together under their shared market path. Jump settings were paired by replication, sharing the draws of any component whose jump setting did not change, and the approximate $95%$ confidence intervals were the mean of the $1,000$ replication-level differences plus or minus $1.96$ standard errors (Table [tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty]). These intervals describe simulation uncertainty conditional on the fitted models and observed histories, not uncertainty across market regimes.


\subsection{Single-asset generator validation}


The SPY returns had heavy tails and persistent absolute-return autocorrelation in both the training and holdout windows, providing two targets for generator validation (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]). Individual raw-return correlations were small, although the Ljung--Box test detected joint serial dependence. We compared HMM-WJ with seven alternatives on the training window, using $1,000$ paths of $2,766$ trading days (Table [tab:model_comparison]; Fig. [fig:model_comparison]). The alternatives were independent bootstrap, Gaussian, and Laplace draws; HMM-NJ; GARCH(1,1) with Gaussian innovations; the GRU benchmark; and the HSMM baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla. Both HMM variants had KS pass rates within about one percentage point of the fitted Laplace baseline but came much closer to the observed excess kurtosis, which Laplace underestimated at $3.0$ against $7.7$. Both also had lower Wasserstein-1 distances than Laplace, whereas Laplace had the lowest Hellinger distance among the fitted generators. Bootstrap resampling passed every KS test while reusing the observed returns, and Gaussian sampling passed neither distribution test. The GARCH model had the lowest mean absolute error between the observed and ensemble-average absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE) but rarely passed the distribution tests. The GRU also had lower ACF-MAE than the HMM variants but rarely passed the distribution tests. The HSMM passed $82%$ of KS tests, underestimated excess kurtosis by more than one third, and had the same ACF-MAE as HMM-NJ despite its flexible state durations. A controlled emission ablation confirmed that Student-$t$ emissions brought HMM-WJ excess kurtosis closer to the observed value than Normal emissions (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]). The HMM variants therefore captured heavy tails well, but no fitted generator led on every metric.

Jump episodes improved temporal fit at a small cost in marginal fit in training (Table [tab:model_comparison]; Fig. [fig:model_comparison]b). Under the counted transition matrix, every state had an expected residence time of $1$--$2$ steps, and absolute-return autocorrelation decayed to near zero by lag $5$ (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-nojump]). Calibration selected mean episode duration $\lambda=90$ and jump probability $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ (Table [tab:hyperparams]). About $24%$ of training-length HMM-WJ paths contained an episode; paths without one were equivalent to HMM-NJ (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-renewal]). Across all paths, ACF-MAE fell from $0.059$ for HMM-NJ to $0.053$ for HMM-WJ, with small reductions in KS and AD pass rates. The episode frequency and jump-active autocorrelation agreed with the generator calculations (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]). The calibration objective scored only jump-active paths, so it weakly identified $\epsilon$ and selected the smallest grid value; the roughly one-quarter episode share followed from that value and the path length, rather than being a calibration target. Likewise, $\lambda$ was a fitted duration, not an estimate of observed high-volatility spell lengths. Supporting sweeps showed that this tradeoff depended on both the jump-active share and the state count: increasing either improved autocorrelation fit but reduced distribution-test pass rates (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-sensitivity]). The primary choice $N=100$ balanced the training metrics, but the state-count sweep used SPY alone and did not establish a suitable resolution for other assets or shorter histories (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sensitivity]).

The frozen HMM variants retained high distribution-test pass rates on the $249$ held-out SPY returns from $2025$, although the short sample limited discrimination among generators (Table [tab:model_comparison]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-renewal]). We compared those returns with $249$-day simulated paths. The KS pass rates were $96.6%$ for HMM-NJ and $95.4%$ for HMM-WJ, but weaker generators also passed more often than in the longer training comparison. Few paths contained an episode at $\epsilon=10^{-4}$, so the holdout mainly tested the no-jump dynamics shared by the two variants (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-renewal]). The fitted Laplace baseline matched or exceeded both on KS and AD pass rates and had equal or smaller Wasserstein-1 and Hellinger distances. Its excess kurtosis of $2.8$, however, remained well below the observed $6.9$, whereas both HMM variants reached $6.2$. Repeating HMM-WJ fitting and calibration for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM gave training KS pass rates near $99%$ for all three, but holdout rates ranged from $74.0%$ for JNJ to $97.7%$ for NVDA (Online Appendix [sec:supp-cross-asset]). The single-asset evidence supported heavy-tail representation, but did not establish uniform holdout fit across assets or metrics. The six-method composition comparison used the no-jump variant to avoid per-ticker jump calibration, and a separate multi-asset comparison tested the transfer of SPY's jump settings.


\subsection{Variance-corrected multi-asset composition}


We tested whether hybrid composition preserved generator variance while recovering the calibrated market loading across the $423$ non-market assets of the training universe (Table [tab:aggregate]; Fig. [fig:preservation]). The six-method comparison included GARCH(1,1)-$t$ on the $393$ assets with eligible fits. With observed training SPY as the market path, the index trackers QQQ and SPYG used the $R^2$-preserving branch, and the other $421$ assets, which we call non-tracker assets, used the variance-preserving branch without clipping (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). The two trackers targeted the calibrated $R^2$ rather than the generator variance, so we marked them separately (Fig. [fig:preservation]). We paired each composed path with its uncomposed generator draw to measure the ratio of composed to generator variance directly. Across the non-tracker assets, the median of the per-ticker median ratios was $1.3171$ for naive composition and $1.0001$ for hybrid composition, and the hybrid ticker medians ranged from $0.9933$ to $1.0072$ (Fig. [fig:preservation]b). Individual hybrid paths still varied around the target, because the correction assumes zero covariance between the scaled asset draw and the market path, whereas a finite path has a small nonzero sample covariance. Across non-tracker hybrid paths, the median absolute departure of the variance ratio from one was $1.12%$, and its $95$th percentile was $3.41%$ (Fig. [fig:preservation]b). The correlation between generator draws and the observed market path averaged near zero for every asset (Online Appendix [sec:supp-cov]), which supported the zero-covariance assumption on average but not on each path.

The methods differed more in distributional fit than in factor recovery (Table [tab:aggregate]; Fig. [fig:tails]). All six methods had median absolute $\beta$ errors between $0.017$ and $0.022$. Naive and hybrid composition had a median absolute intercept error of $0.002\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, an order of magnitude below the other methods, because their centered residual paths reproduced the observed training asset means exactly when combined with observed SPY without clipping. Refitting OLS left an intercept error equal to the loading error times the market mean. The small intercept errors thus followed from centering, not from predicting asset means. Gaussian SIM had the lowest median $R^2$ error but almost never passed the distribution tests, and its median excess kurtosis was an order of magnitude below the observed median of about $13$. Hybrid composition passed $73.6%$ of KS comparisons, against $7.1%$ for naive composition, and its advantage held across the calibrated $\beta$ range. Its own pass rate nonetheless fell from $87.6%$ in the lowest $\beta$ quartile to $65.9%$ in the highest (Fig. [fig:preservation]a; Table [tab:beta_bucket]). A larger market variance share may help explain this trend by diluting the asset generator's shape (Online Appendix [sec:supp-residual-choice]). Hybrid composition retained heavy tails, with median excess kurtosis of $7.2$ and a median Hill estimate of $0.37$, reported as the reciprocal tail index so that larger values indicate heavier tails (Fig. [fig:tails]). The excess-kurtosis shortfall was also present under naive composition, whose median was similar. Hybrid composition's median $R^2$ error fell between those of the naive and Gaussian methods, because its variance-preserving branch targeted generator variance rather than observed $R^2$.

Direct residual fitting was competitive with reuse, and controlling for residual centering favored the residual-fit HMM and block bootstrap (Tables [tab:aggregate] and [tab:centering_control]). Under each method's native mean treatment, JumpHMM-on-residuals passed $75.8%$ of KS comparisons, slightly above hybrid composition; block bootstrap passed $73.3%$ and GARCH(1,1)-$t$ passed $67.4%$. The residual-fit HMM and block bootstrap had slightly lower Wasserstein distances than hybrid composition. The residual-fit HMM also closely matched hybrid composition in excess kurtosis and Hill estimate, whereas block bootstrap and GARCH produced lighter tails. Only naive and hybrid composition centered their residual draws, so we repeated the comparison after centering all six methods on the common $393$-asset set (Table [tab:centering_control]). The hybrid KS pass rate stayed at $74.4%$ because its paths were already centered; the JumpHMM-on-residuals rate rose from $75.8%$ to $78.4%$, and block bootstrap rose from $73.5%$ to $76.7%$. Both residual methods also had higher AD pass rates and lower Wasserstein distances than hybrid composition after centering. The control brought every method's median intercept error to $0.002\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ while leaving variance, recovered loading, $R^2$, and excess kurtosis unchanged. A separate training diagnostic found that emission fallback rules also disadvantaged the full-return methods on $39$ assets but affected none of the residual-fit models (Table [tab:fallback_sensitivity]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-fallback]). Using empirical scales in their populated zero-return states implied an aggregate hybrid KS gain of about one percentage point, less than the gap to JumpHMM-on-residuals. The main comparisons retained the original rule because this alternative had no holdout validation (Online Appendix [sec:supp-fallback]). Together, these checks supported variance correction over naive reuse without a second fit, but did not establish an advantage over direct residual fitting.


\subsection{Holdout performance and VaR coverage}


To test out-of-sample generalization without refitting or look-ahead bias, we froze the fitted models and SIM parameters before evaluating them against $416$ complete asset histories from $2025$ (Table [tab:oos_composition]). Hybrid composition reached an $87.0%$ mean cross-ticker KS pass rate, against $83.5%$ for naive composition. Its median ratio of synthetic to observed $2025$ variance was $0.97$, whereas naive composition inflated that ratio to $1.29$. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method reached an $86.2%$ KS pass rate and a slightly lower median $W_1$ than hybrid composition. As in training, these holdout comparisons used each method's native mean treatment, and no common-centering control was run on the holdout. Higher holdout pass rates did not by themselves establish better generation: all methods passed more often than in training, and the gap between hybrid and naive composition narrowed (Tables [tab:aggregate] and [tab:oos_composition]). For every method, pass rates against matched-length $249$-day training blocks lay between the full-length training and $2025$ rates, consistent with lower test power in shorter samples contributing to the rise. Those blocks also used simulated rather than observed SPY, so the comparison did not isolate sample length or fully explain the $2025$ increase. Nor did improvement hold for every asset: about three quarters of hybrid tickers passed more often in $2025$ than against their matched training blocks and one quarter passed less often, and every method retained a lower tail of weakly fitted tickers (Fig. [fig:oos_composition]).

To test whether the holdout improvement over naive composition extended to reliable tail thresholds, we estimated per-ticker one-day left-tail Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds from $5,000$ separate $249$-day paths and counted exceedances on the observed $2025$ histories (Table [tab:var]). The coverage level $\alpha=0.95$ has a nominal exceedance rate of $1-\alpha=5%$ and gave each asset about $12$ expected exceedances. At that level, hybrid composition had a mean exceedance rate of $5.48%$, closer to nominal than the $4.04%$ of naive composition. At $\alpha=0.99$, the hybrid rate of $1.12%$ and the naive rate of $0.88%$ lay equally close to the $1%$ nominal rate on opposite sides. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method had rates of $5.62%$ and $1.16%$, close to hybrid composition, and Gaussian SIM had the largest $99%$ rate at $1.72%$. Doubling the threshold pool from $2,500$ to $5,000$ paths changed every method's mean exceedance rate by less than $0.006$ percentage points at either coverage level (Online Appendix [sec:supp-var-ensemble]). The advantage of hybrid over naive composition thus appeared at the $95%$ level but not at the $99%$ level. Neither comparison established a ranking, because each asset contributed few exceedances, only about $2$--$3$ expected at the $99%$ level, and all assets shared one market period, so their exceedances were not independent. The hybrid mean exceedance rates were above nominal at both coverage levels, and this descriptive check could not confirm threshold calibration. The cross-asset standard deviations of the exceedance rates described how realized rates varied among assets; they did not test miscalibration or validate coverage in other periods.


\subsection{Jump-enabled multi-asset composition}




Transferring SPY's jump settings improved temporal fit in training but worsened it in the $2025$ holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation], where hybrid composition is labeled Corrected). We compared jumps disabled, market-only jumps, and market-plus-asset jumps under naive and hybrid composition, without per-asset tuning. This experiment simulated the market in both windows and used each market path's variance in the correction, so its baseline differed from the six-method comparisons. Its ACF-MAE was calculated for each path and then averaged, unlike the single-asset error calculated from the ensemble-average autocorrelation. Under hybrid composition, market-only jumps reduced the training $25$-lag ACF-MAE by $5.3%$; the $95%$ Monte Carlo interval excluded zero. The KS pass rate fell by $0.3$ percentage points, with an interval that included zero (Table [tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty]). Adding asset jumps reduced ACF-MAE by $14.6%$ relative to no jumps but lowered the KS pass rate from $73.9%$ to $61.7%$, a much larger marginal-fit cost than for SPY, whose settings were tuned. In the holdout, market-only and market-plus-asset jumps instead raised the $25$-lag ACF-MAE by $2.7%$ and $6.9%$. Both $95%$ Monte Carlo intervals excluded zero, and the $60$-lag errors also rose (Tables [tab:jump_ablation] and [tab:jump_ablation_uncertainty]). Holdout KS pass rates changed little with market-only jumps; with both market and asset jumps, they fell by about $1.5$ percentage points relative to no jumps. Across all settings and both windows, the mean ratio of composed to generator variance under hybrid composition remained within $0.05%$ of one. This aggregate included the trackers, whose branch targeted $R^2$ rather than generator variance, and did not establish preservation for every asset. Only $2.2%$ of simulated one-year market paths contained an episode (Online Appendix [sec:supp-jump-composition]), limiting evidence about market jumps; the single observed holdout year also could not establish performance in other market periods.


\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}


We examined how the branch-selection threshold $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}}$ assigned assets to branches in the observed universe and whether any value of the variance floor $f$ triggered clipping (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). The $R^2$-preserving branch applied only to QQQ and SPYG, whose hybrid compositions passed every KS test in training. The highest-$R^2$ asset outside these trackers, the individual stock BLK, fell about $0.16$ below the $0.80$ threshold, so the assignment did not depend on a narrow margin. Across the explored thresholds from $0.70$ to $0.90$ at the default floor, only the QQQ assignment changed, moving to the variance-preserving branch at $0.90$. At the default threshold, no value of $f$ from $0.05$ to $0.30$ caused any asset to be clipped. The empirical evaluation of the $R^2$-preserving branch was therefore limited to two assets, and the observed market variance never activated clipping.

A synthetic Gaussian check supplemented the two real assets using the $R^2$-preserving branch: across the tested grid, median recovered loadings and $R^2$ differed from their supplied targets by at most $0.0011$ and $0.0017$, respectively (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). Each reference series combined the observed training SPY path with an independent Gaussian residual whose variance gave the target $R^2$ under Eq. Equation (eq:r2-target). The composer received the true loading and $R^2$, a second independent Gaussian draw, and the target residual variance, which made the residual scale in Eq. Equation (eq:r2-rescale) exactly one. Every composed series passed the KS comparison with its reference, but their shared SPY path made the samples dependent, so the pass rates were descriptive rather than calibrated tests. The grid checked implementation and finite-sample accuracy under this restricted construction; it did not exercise full-return HMM fitting, non-unit residual scaling, or simulated market paths.

Increasing market volatility activated clipping for most assets, although no path clipped under the observed market variance (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). A larger market variance raises the market loading ratio $\rho_i$, the market's share of the target variance; on the variance-preserving branch, clipping begins when $\rho_i>1-f$. To measure when clipping activates, we multiplied deviations of the observed training SPY path from its mean by $\gamma\in\{1,2,3\}$, which preserved the market mean and multiplied its standard deviation by $\gamma$. We then recomposed all $423$ assets with the scaled path's variance in the correction, holding the fitted marginals, intercepts, and calibrated loadings fixed (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). Clipping applied to no composed path at $\gamma=1$ and to $76.4%$ and $95.2%$ of composed paths at $\gamma=2$ and $3$. Because clipped paths retained their fitted intercepts while the market mean stayed unchanged and nonzero, their means shifted by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$ by construction. The stress test therefore characterized when clipping activated and how it moved asset means; it did not evaluate fit under the scaled markets.

Synthetic generators of equity returns should reproduce three stylized facts: heavy tails, weak autocorrelation in raw returns, and volatility clustering (Fig. [fig:empirical_motivation]). We used heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators with optional jump episodes to represent these features, then examined their reuse in a multi-asset model with a shared market path. We developed a correction that centers and rescales each generator's draws to avoid counting market variance twice, without refitting to regression residuals (Fig. [fig:preservation]; Table [tab:aggregate]). Training and $2025$ holdout comparisons tested distributional fit and whether single-asset improvements in volatility clustering survived composition.

The correction's practical value was avoiding a separate residual fit while improving variance and distributional agreement over naive composition (Fig. [fig:preservation]; Table [tab:aggregate]). The corrected method passed $73.6%$ of full-length training KS comparisons and retained heavy tails. On the $2025$ holdout, it raised the mean cross-ticker KS pass rate from $83.5%$ under centered naive composition to $87.0%$ and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance to $0.97$ (Table [tab:oos_composition]). The improvement therefore persisted beyond the fitting period. Direct residual fitting remained competitive: JumpHMM-on-residuals had a slightly higher pass rate in training and a slightly lower rate in the holdout. When all methods' residual draws were centered over the same training horizon on the common asset set, JumpHMM-on-residuals and block bootstrap both outperformed the corrected method on distribution-test pass rates and Wasserstein distance (Table [tab:centering_control]). Before centering was matched, the comparison with residual-fit methods therefore partly reflected different constraints on simulated residual means. For corrected composition, some assets had lower pass rates in the holdout than against matched-length training blocks. Reuse thus offered a way to improve on naive composition without refitting, but did not establish a distributional advantage over fitting the residuals themselves.

The jump comparison showed that improving the single-asset generator did not automatically improve the composed paths (Tables [tab:model_comparison] and [tab:jump_ablation]). On SPY, the generator with jumps reduced absolute-return autocorrelation error relative to its no-jump counterpart while retaining high distributional pass rates. Other generators achieved lower autocorrelation error or higher pass rates, so temporal and marginal fit still required separate assessment. With multi-asset composition, market-only jumps improved training temporal fit with little change in the KS pass rate, whereas adding asset-level episodes produced a larger temporal improvement at a substantial marginal-fit cost. Both settings increased autocorrelation error in $2025$, despite the mean composed-to-generator variance ratio remaining near one (Table [tab:jump_ablation]). Variance control therefore did not ensure that the transferred temporal behavior matched the data. These tests transferred one SPY calibration from volume-weighted average prices to closing-price models. The asset episodes also came from full-return models and were not identified separately as idiosyncratic events. Separate calibrations for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM had strong in-sample fit, but three assets did not establish a general calibration rule (Online Appendix [sec:supp-cross-asset]). Per-asset tuning or distinct shared and asset-specific episode models could change the tradeoff, but neither was tested in the composition comparison.

Preserving total variance did not preserve the complete return distribution, and corrected fit varied with market exposure (Fig. [fig:preservation]; Table [tab:beta_bucket]). Corrected KS pass rates fell from $87.6%$ in the lowest $\beta$ quartile to $65.9%$ in the highest, consistent with a larger market contribution diluting the asset generator's shape. The correction accounted for variance supplied by the market, but combining two independent return distributions still changed the shape of the composed returns. Clipping, the reduction of the market loading to retain some residual variance, was unnecessary at observed market variance but activated for most assets when market volatility was doubled or tripled (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). That stress transformation preserved the market mean. Reducing the loading while retaining the fitted intercept changed the asset mean by $(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m$, where $\overline{g}_m$ is the market mean. The clipped branch therefore targeted total variance while retaining a minimum residual contribution, at the cost of changing the calibrated loading and mean. The stress test did not establish realistic return distributions under the scaled markets. Variance correction therefore required separate checks of distributional shape and calibrated market exposure.

The correction supported daily-return comparisons, but broader simulation uses require additional modeling and validation. The alternative correction for market trackers targeted their calibrated $R^2$, the fraction of return variance explained by the market. Only two observed assets tested it; a Gaussian simulation checked its formula without fitting full-return generators (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). Allowing states whose training observations were identical to generate nearly constant returns improved training fit (Table [tab:fallback_sensitivity]). For those states, the main comparisons retained emissions scaled by the asset's full-series spread, and the alternative still needs holdout validation. Improved marginal fit did not establish reliable tail coverage: the corrected method and JumpHMM-on-residuals had pooled-threshold one-day left-tail $99%$ VaR exceedance rates of $1.12%$ and $1.16%$, against a nominal $1%$ (Table [tab:var]). More simulated paths improved threshold precision without supplying more observed extreme events; the $249$-day holdout was too short for a precise ranking and covered only one shared market period. Serial dependence also limited the interpretation of KS and AD pass rates, whose tests assume independent observations. Variance accounting also assumed independent asset and market draws; nonzero covariance would require an additional cross-term. For portfolio applications, a shared market path did not supply the sector and style dependence omitted by the evaluated independent residual draws (Algorithm [alg:hybrid]; Online Appendix [sec:supp-bias]). Omitting positive residual covariance can overstate the diversification return from periodic rebalancing, including for residual-fit baselines that draw residuals independently. A synchronized multivariate block bootstrap, residual factor model, or copula could address that omission, but the resulting dependence needs direct evaluation. Per-path centering imposed a separate limitation: each residual sum was zero at the chosen horizon, leaving no residual uncertainty in terminal cumulative log returns conditional on the market path and effective loading (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sim-derivations]). Portfolio and cumulative-risk applications therefore require both a joint residual model and a construction that retains cumulative residual variation.

We developed a variance-corrected single-index method that centers and rescales full-return draws for reuse without counting market variance twice or fitting separate residual generators (Fig. [fig:preservation]; Table [tab:aggregate]). The method retained heavy tails and improved distributional fit over centered naive composition in training and the $2025$ holdout (Table [tab:oos_composition]), but did not establish an advantage over direct residual fitting. Transferring SPY's jump settings improved temporal fit in training but worsened it in the holdout (Table [tab:jump_ablation]), showing that single-asset temporal gains did not transfer reliably. These findings support reuse for daily-return comparisons, without establishing calibrated tail coverage (Table [tab:var]) or validating broader risk applications. Independent residual draws omitted cross-asset dependence, and per-path centering removed residual uncertainty from terminal cumulative log returns conditional on the market path and the loading used in composition. Portfolio and cumulative-risk applications therefore require a joint residual model and a construction that retains cumulative residual variation.

--- Paragraph appendix.P4 (appendix.tex:37) ---
[appendix.P4.S1] To identify the portfolio consequence of independent residuals, we derive the diversification return of a daily-rebalanced portfolio.
[appendix.P4.S2] Matching generator variance also matches empirical variance only when the generator reproduces it; neither condition restores cross-asset residual covariance.
[appendix.P4.S3] Residual paths retain their own temporal structure but are generated independently across assets.
[appendix.P4.S4] Consider a daily-rebalanced portfolio with constant target weights $w_i \ge 0$, $\sum_i w_i = 1$, reset to those weights at the start of each trading day.
[appendix.P4.S5] Throughout this derivation $g_i(t)$ is the annualized log-growth rate of Eq.
[appendix.P4.S6] Equation (eq:growth_rate), so the per-period log return on asset $i$ is $g_i(t)\,\Delta t$ with $\Delta t = 1/252$ yr.
[appendix.P4.S7] The wealth process is:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} W_t \;=\; W_{t-1} \sum_{i} w_i \exp\!\big(g_i(t)\,\Delta t\big),  \end{equation}
[appendix.P4.S8] so the per-step log return on the portfolio is $\log(W_t/W_{t-1}) = \log\!\sum_i w_i \exp(g_i(t)\,\Delta t)$.
[appendix.P4.S9] Write $g_i$ for $g_i(t)$.
[appendix.P4.S10] A second-order Taylor expansion of the exponential about zero is $\exp(g_i\,\Delta t) = 1 + g_i\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}(g_i\,\Delta t)^2 + O((g\,\Delta t)^3)$.
[appendix.P4.S11] Substituting it into each summand of $\sum_i w_i \exp(g_i\,\Delta t)$ gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \sum_{i} w_i \exp\!\big(g_i\,\Delta t\big) &= \sum_{i} w_i \!\left[\,1 + g_i\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}(g_i\,\Delta t)^2 + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big)\right] && \text{(Taylor)} \notag \\ &= \sum_{i} w_i + \Delta t \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big) && \text{(distribute)} \notag \\ &= 1 + \Delta t \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big). && \text{(}\textstyle\sum_i w_i = 1\text{)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P4.S12] A second-order Taylor expansion of $\log(1+x)$ about $x=0$ gives $\log(1+x) = x - \tfrac{1}{2}x^2 + O(x^3)$.
[appendix.P4.S13] Identifying $1 + x$ with the right-hand side of Eq.
[appendix.P4.S14] Equation (eq:exp-expand), the part beyond one is $x :=\Delta t \sum_i w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \sum_i w_i\, g_i^2$, and squaring keeps only the leading $(\Delta t)^2$ term:
[appendix.P4.S15] $x^2 = (\Delta t)^2 (\sum_i w_i\, g_i)^2 + O((\Delta t)^3)$ because the cross term has an extra $\Delta t$ factor.
[appendix.P4.S16] Substitution gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \log\!\frac{W_t}{W_{t-1}} &= \log\!\Big(1 + x + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big)\Big) \notag \\ &= x - \tfrac{1}{2}\, x^2 + O\!\big(x^3\big) \notag \\ &= \Delta t \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 \notag \\ &\quad - \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \!\Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i\Big)^{\!2} + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big) \notag \\ &= \Delta t \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t)^2 \!\left[\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 \right. \notag \\ &\hspace{8em}\left.{}- \Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i\Big)^{\!2}\right] + O\!\big((g\,\Delta t)^3\big).  \end{align}
[appendix.P4.S17] Dividing by $\Delta t$ gives the annualized portfolio growth rate $g_p(t) := \Delta t^{-1}\log(W_t/W_{t-1})$:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} g_p \;=\; \sum_{i} w_i\, g_i \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t \!\left(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i^2 \;-\; \Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i\Big)^{\!2}\right) \;+\; O\!\big((\Delta t)^2\big).  \end{equation}

[appendix.P5.S1] To obtain expected annualized portfolio growth, we restored the time argument and took unconditional expectations of Eq.
[appendix.P5.S2] Equation (eq:gp-onestep) under stationarity.
[appendix.P5.S3] Define the mean and variance of each asset's annualized growth rate:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \mu_i \;:=\; \E\!\big(g_i(t)\big), \qquad \sigma_i^2 \;:=\; \Var\!\big(g_i(t)\big), \end{equation}
[appendix.P5.S4] the covariance of the annualized rates $\Sigma_{ij} := \Cov\!\big(g_i(t), g_j(t)\big)$, and the portfolio variance $\sigma_p^2 := \Var\!\big(\textstyle\sum_i w_i g_i(t)\big) = w^\top \Sigma w$.
[appendix.P5.S5] The squared terms $\sum_i w_i\, g_i(t)^2$ and $(\sum_i w_i\, g_i(t))^2$ in the $O(\Delta t)$ correction expand using the following identity for any $X$ with finite second moment:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \E\!\big(X^2\big) \;=\; \E\!\big(X\big)^2 \;+\; \Var(X).  \end{equation}
[appendix.P5.S6] Applying this identity to $X = g_i(t)$ and summing against $w_i$ gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \E\!\Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, g_i(t)^2\Big) &\;=\; \sum_{i} w_i\, \E\!\big(g_i(t)^2\big) && \text{(linearity)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{i} w_i\!\left[\,\mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2\,\right] && \text{(by Eq. Equation (eq:second-moment))} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i^2 \;+\; \sum_{i} w_i\, \sigma_i^2.  \end{align}
[appendix.P5.S7] Applying the identity to $X = \sum_i w_i g_i(t)$, whose mean is $\sum_i w_i\mu_i$ and whose variance is $\sigma_p^2$, gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \E\!\Big(\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i g_i(t)\Big)^{\!2}\Big) &\;=\; \E\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i g_i(t)\Big)^{\!2} \;+\; \Var\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i g_i(t)\Big) && \text{(by Eq. Equation (eq:second-moment))} \notag \\ &\;=\; \Big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i\Big)^{\!2} \;+\; \sigma_p^2.  \end{align}
[appendix.P5.S8] We substituted Eqs.
[appendix.P5.S9] Equation (eq:exp-gi2)--Equation (eq:exp-sum2) into the expectation of Eq.
[appendix.P5.S10] Equation (eq:gp-onestep) and grouped the variance and mean contributions as follows:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \E(g_p) \;={}& \sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t\!\left(\sum_{i} w_i\, \sigma_i^2 - \sigma_p^2\right)}_{\displaystyle D} \\ &+ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t\!\left[\sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i^2 - \Big(\sum_{i} w_i\, \mu_i\Big)^{\!2}\right] + O\!\big((\Delta t)^2\big), \end{aligned}
[appendix.P5.S11] \end{equation} the Booth--Fama decomposition, to second order, of the growth of a daily-rebalanced portfolio in annualized growth-rate units .
[appendix.P5.S12] The middle term, $D$, is the diversification return.
[appendix.P5.S13] The moments $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i^2$ of the annualized rates have units $\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ and $\mathrm{yr}^{-2}$ respectively; the products $\Delta t\,\sigma_i^2$ and $\Delta t\,\sigma_p^2$ in Eq.
[appendix.P5.S14] Equation (eq:booth-fama) are the annualized variances ($\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$).
[appendix.P5.S15] The third term in Eq.
[appendix.P5.S16] Equation (eq:booth-fama) is the cross-sectional dispersion of the per-asset means, of order $\Delta t\,\mu^2$, while $D$ is of order $\Delta t\,\sigma^2$.
[appendix.P5.S17] For SPY, $\mu^2$ lies more than two orders of magnitude below $\sigma^2$ (Online Appendix [sec:supp-generator]), and we drop the third term, treating the same ordering as typical of daily equity growth rates.
[appendix.P5.S18] The realized annualized compound growth rate is $\bar g_p = (T\,\Delta t)^{-1}\log(W_T/W_0) = T^{-1}\sum_{t} g_p(t)$.
[appendix.P5.S19] If the composed growth rates form a stationary ergodic process, $\bar g_p$ converges to $\E(g_p)$ by the ergodic theorem.
[appendix.P5.S20] This limit depends on the single-date joint law of $(g_i(t))_i$, not residual serial dependence.
[appendix.P5.S21] Finite-horizon centering and scaling are outside this stationary-process argument.

[appendix.P6.S1] To show that $D$ is non-negative, we rewrote the bracket in $D$ as a sum over asset pairs.
[appendix.P6.S2] Expanding the portfolio variance, using $1 - w_i = \sum_{j \ne i} w_j$ for weights summing to one, and collecting each pair once gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \sum_i w_i \sigma_i^2 - \sigma_p^2 &\;=\; \sum_i w_i \sigma_i^2 - \sum_i w_i^2 \sigma_i^2 - 2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \Sigma_{ij} && \text{(expand $\sigma_p^2$)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_i w_i (1 - w_i)\, \sigma_i^2 - 2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \Sigma_{ij} && \text{(collect)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_i \sum_{j \ne i} w_i w_j\, \sigma_i^2 - 2 \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \Sigma_{ij} && \text{($1 - w_i = \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} w_j$)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \left(\sigma_i^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 2\Sigma_{ij}\right) && \text{(pair terms)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{i<j} w_i w_j \,\Var(g_i - g_j), && \text{(variance of a difference)}  \end{align}

[appendix.P13.S10] Equation (eq:grid_search).
[appendix.P13.S11] The state-resolution and emission comparisons (Online Appendix [sec:supp-sensitivity]; Table [tab:emission_hsmm]) used the same ensemble convention.
[appendix.P13.S12] The multi-asset jump comparison instead measured error within each path before averaging (Table [tab:jump_ablation]).

    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} E^{\rm path}_L=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R} \left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} |a_\ell(x)-a_\ell(y^{(r)})|\right].  \end{equation}

\subsection{Generative law}


--- Paragraph appendix.P22 (appendix.tex:544) ---

--- Paragraph appendix.P23 (appendix.tex:546) ---
[appendix.P23.S1] The no-jump model generates a hidden state path $S_0, \dots, S_T$ on $\{1, \dots, N\}$ and observations $G_1, \dots, G_T$ with the joint density:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} p(s_0, \dots, s_T,\, g_1, \dots, g_T) \;=\; \bar\pi_{s_0} \prod_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{T}_{s_{t-1},\, s_t}\; f_{s_t}(g_t),  \end{equation}
[appendix.P23.S2] where $\bar\pi_{s_0}$ is the initial probability of state $s_0$, $\mathbf{T}_{s_{t-1}, s_t}$ is the probability of moving from state $s_{t-1}$ to state $s_t$, and $f_k$ is the emission density of state $k$; bold $\mathbf{T}$ is the transition matrix and italic $T$ the number of observations.
[appendix.P23.S3] Because $\bar\pi$ is stationary for $\mathbf{T}$, starting from an unemitted $S_0 \sim \bar\pi$ gives $S_1 \sim \bar\pi$, the same law as drawing $S_1$ from $\bar\pi$ and emitting it, as Algorithm [alg:hmmwj] does.
[appendix.P23.S4] Under Eq.
[appendix.P23.S5] Equation (eq:emission), $f_k$ is the location-scale Student-$t$ density:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} f_k(g) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sigma_k}\, \psi_\nu\!\left(\frac{g - \mu_k}{\sigma_k}\right), \qquad \psi_\nu(x) \;=\; \frac{\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{\nu+1}{2}\big)} {\sqrt{\nu \pi}\; \Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{\nu}{2}\big)} \left(1 + \frac{x^2}{\nu}\right)^{\!-\frac{\nu+1}{2}},  \end{equation}
[appendix.P23.S6] where $\psi_\nu$ is the density of a standard Student-$t$ random variable with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, $\Gamma$ is the gamma function, and $(\mu_k, \sigma_k)$ are the location and scale of state $k$.
[appendix.P23.S7] Given the state path, emissions are independent; serial dependence therefore comes from the state path.

--- Paragraph appendix.P24 (appendix.tex:580) ---
[appendix.P24.S1] The jump-augmented model enlarges the hidden state.
[appendix.P24.S2] Let $J_t \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ count the forced steps remaining after time $t$, starting from $J_0 = 0$, and let $u$ be the forced-step state distribution, which mixes uniform draws over the two tail sets:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} u_k \;=\; \begin{cases} p_{\rm neg}/N_{\rm tail} & k \in \mathcal{S}_{-},\\[2pt] (1 - p_{\rm neg})/N_{\rm tail} & k \in \mathcal{S}_{+},\\[2pt] 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
[appendix.P24.S3] \end{equation} Writing $p_K(m) = e^{-\lambda} \lambda^m / m!$ for the Poisson probability of episode length $m$ and $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ for the indicator function, the pair $(S_t, J_t)$ evolves by the transition kernel:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} &\Pr\big(S_t = k,\, J_t = j \mid S_{t-1} = s,\, J_{t-1} = i\big) \\ &\qquad=\; \begin{cases} u_k\, \mathbf{1}\{j = i - 1\} & i \ge 1,\\[6pt] \big(1 - \epsilon + \epsilon e^{-\lambda}\big)\, \mathbf{T}_{s, k}\, \mathbf{1}\{j = 0\} \;+\; \epsilon\, p_K(j + 1)\, u_k & i = 0, \end{cases}
[appendix.P24.S4] \end{aligned}  \end{equation} and the emission draw depends only on the first coordinate, $G_t \sim f_{S_t}$.
[appendix.P24.S5] While an episode is active ($i \ge 1$), the next state is an independent draw from $u$ and the counter decrements; the tail set and the state within it are re-drawn at every forced step.
[appendix.P24.S6] While no episode is active ($i = 0$), the chain either takes an ordinary transition (no trigger, or a trigger whose Poisson draw returns zero) or starts an episode of length $j + 1 \ge 1$ whose first forced step occurs immediately.
[appendix.P24.S7] The pair $(S_t, J_t)$ is therefore itself a Markov chain, and the HMM-WJ generator is a hidden Markov model on the enlarged state space $\{1, \dots, N\} \times \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$.
[appendix.P24.S8] Algorithm [alg:hmmwj] implements Eq.
[appendix.P24.S9] Equation (eq:supp-kernel) with the episode counter truncated at the simulation horizon.
[appendix.P24.S10] It emits the initial draw from $\bar\pi$ as the first observation and applies the kernel from the second step onward, so the first observed day can never be a forced step; this is the only way its path law differs from the kernel's.
[appendix.P24.S11] When an episode ends, ordinary transitions resume from the last forced tail state, so the chain returns to the central states through the tail rows of $\mathbf{T}$.
[appendix.P24.S12] This return links an episode to the free steps that follow it.


\subsection{Estimators and the one-step empirical law}


--- Paragraph appendix.P25 (appendix.tex:632) ---

--- Paragraph appendix.P26 (appendix.tex:634) ---
[appendix.P26.S1] The partition, transitions, emissions and initial law are estimated from sample summaries; the fitted law need not reproduce the observed return moments.
[appendix.P26.S2] For observations $G_1, \dots, G_T$, the Laplace log-likelihood with location $\mu_L$ and scale $b_L$ is:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \ell(\mu_L, b_L) \;=\; -T \ln (2 b_L) \;-\; \frac{1}{b_L} \sum_{t=1}^{T} |G_t - \mu_L|.  \end{equation}
[appendix.P26.S3] For any fixed $b_L > 0$, maximizing $\ell$ over $\mu_L$ minimizes $\sum_t |G_t - \mu_L|$, so $\hat\mu_L$ is the sample median.
[appendix.P26.S4] Setting the $b_L$ derivative to zero at $\hat\mu_L$ gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b_L} &\;=\; -\frac{T}{b_L} \;+\; \frac{1}{b_L^2} \sum_{t=1}^{T} |G_t - \hat\mu_L| \;=\; 0 && \text{(first-order condition)} \notag \\ \hat b_L &\;=\; \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} |G_t - \hat\mu_L|, && \text{(solve for $b_L$)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P26.S5] the mean absolute deviation about the median.
[appendix.P26.S6] The state boundaries then invert the fitted Laplace cumulative distribution function (CDF).
[appendix.P26.S7] The Laplace CDF is piecewise exponential:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} F_L(x) \;=\; \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2} \exp\!\big((x - \hat\mu_L)/\hat b_L\big) & x \le \hat\mu_L,\\[4pt] 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} \exp\!\big(-(x - \hat\mu_L)/\hat b_L\big) & x > \hat\mu_L, \end{cases}
[appendix.P26.S8] \end{equation} and setting $F_L(Q_k) = k/N$ and solving the lower branch for $k \le N/2$ and the upper branch for $k \ge N/2$ gives the closed-form boundaries:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \frac{k}{N} &\;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, e^{(Q_k - \hat\mu_L)/\hat b_L} \;\Longrightarrow\; Q_k \;=\; \hat\mu_L + \hat b_L \ln\!\frac{2k}{N}, && \text{(lower branch)} \notag \\ \frac{k}{N} &\;=\; 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\, e^{-(Q_k - \hat\mu_L)/\hat b_L} \;\Longrightarrow\; Q_k \;=\; \hat\mu_L - \hat b_L \ln\!\Big(2 - \tfrac{2k}{N}\Big). && \text{(upper branch)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P26.S9] The states are equiprobable under the fitted Laplace, not under the empirical distribution, so the in-sample state counts are not uniform: across the $100$ SPY states, they range from $16$ to $47$ of the $2,766$ observations (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).
[appendix.P26.S10] For SPY, every state therefore held at least $16$ observations with a nonzero spread, and neither fallback rule for the emission parameters applied.

--- Paragraph appendix.P27 (appendix.tex:694) ---
[appendix.P27.S1] Row counting gives the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for the transition likelihood conditional on the initial assigned state.
[appendix.P27.S2] For the assigned state sequence $s_1, \dots, s_T$, let $n_{jk}$ count the transitions from state $j$ to state $k$ and let $n_{j \cdot} = \sum_k n_{jk}$ be the row total.
[appendix.P27.S3] The likelihood of the transition parameters factorizes over rows into multinomials, and maximizing the log-likelihood subject to each row summing to one, with multiplier $c_j$ for row $j$, uses the Lagrangian:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \mathcal{L} \;=\; \sum_{j, k} n_{jk} \ln \mathbf{T}_{jk} \;+\; \sum_{j} c_{j} \Big(1 - \sum_{k} \mathbf{T}_{jk}\Big),  \end{equation}
[appendix.P27.S4] whose first-order conditions solve as follows:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{T}_{jk}} &\;=\; \frac{n_{jk}}{\mathbf{T}_{jk}} - c_j \;=\; 0 && \text{(first-order condition)} \notag \\ \mathbf{T}_{jk} &\;=\; \frac{n_{jk}}{c_j} && \text{(solve for $\mathbf{T}_{jk}$)} \notag \\ c_j &\;=\; \sum_{k} n_{jk} \;=\; n_{j \cdot} && \text{(row constraint $\textstyle\sum_k \mathbf{T}_{jk} = 1$)} \notag \\ \widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{jk} &\;=\; \frac{n_{jk}}{n_{j \cdot}}. && \text{(counted estimator)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P27.S5] For a row with $n_{j\cdot}>0$, the first-order condition applies to cells with positive counts.
[appendix.P27.S6] A cell with $n_{jk}=0$ adds nothing to the log-likelihood, so any probability placed on it is taken from observed transitions and lowers the likelihood; its maximizer is therefore $\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{jk}=0$, which the same formula returns.
[appendix.P27.S7] When $n_{j\cdot}=0$, the observations do not identify that row.
[appendix.P27.S8] The implementation sets every entry to $1/N$; this is a fallback choice, not an estimate of an observed transition frequency.
[appendix.P27.S9] Multiplying $\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{jk}$ by the empirical origin frequency $n_{j\cdot}/(T-1)$ recovers $n_{jk}/(T-1)$, the empirical frequency of the state pair $(j, k)$.
[appendix.P27.S10] Paired with the empirical origin frequencies, the counted estimator therefore reproduces the in-sample joint law of $(S_{t-1}, S_t)$ exactly; everything it asserts about longer horizons is the Chapman--Kolmogorov extension $\mathbf{T}^\tau$ of that one-step law.
[appendix.P27.S11] The third estimator sets the emission parameters: within each state with at least two observations and sample standard deviation at least $10^{-12}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k$ are the sample mean and standard deviation of the assigned observations.
[appendix.P27.S12] Otherwise, the implementation uses the fallback rules stated in the main text and written out in Eq.
[appendix.P27.S13] Equation (eq:emission-fallback) of Online Appendix [sec:supp-fallback].
[appendix.P27.S14] In either case, $\sigma_k$ enters Eq.
[appendix.P27.S15] Equation (eq:supp-emission-density) as the scale, so the emission standard deviation is $\sigma_k \sqrt{\nu/(\nu - 2)}$.
[appendix.P27.S16] The law of total variance in the next subsection quantifies the aggregate effect of this scale convention (Eq.
[appendix.P27.S17] Equation (eq:supp-variance)).

--- Paragraph appendix.P28 (appendix.tex:750) ---
[appendix.P28.S1] The stationary initial distribution is close to empirical state occupancy for the fitted SPY chain.
[appendix.P28.S2] Let $n_k$ count the observations assigned to state $k$ over the $T$ observations, and let $\hat\pi_k = n_k / T$ be the occupancy frequency.
[appendix.P28.S3] The number of transitions out of state $j$ satisfies $n_{j\cdot} = n_j - \mathbf{1}\{s_T = j\}$, and the number of transitions into state $k$ satisfies $n_{\cdot k} = n_k - \mathbf{1}\{s_1 = k\}$, because only the last observation opens no transition and only the first closes none.
[appendix.P28.S4] Substituting both identities, with italic $T$ the number of observations, gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \big(\hat\pi\, \widehat{\mathbf{T}}\big)_k &\;=\; \sum_j \frac{n_j}{T} \cdot \frac{n_{jk}}{n_{j \cdot}} && \text{(definitions)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_j \frac{n_{j \cdot}}{T} \cdot \frac{n_{jk}}{n_{j \cdot}} \;+\; \sum_j \frac{\mathbf{1}\{s_T = j\}}{T} \cdot \frac{n_{jk}}{n_{j \cdot}} && \text{($n_j = n_{j\cdot} + \mathbf{1}\{s_T = j\}$)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \frac{n_{\cdot k}}{T} \;+\; \frac{\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{s_T, k}}{T} && \text{(sum transitions into $k$)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \hat\pi_k \;+\; \frac{\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{s_T, k} - \mathbf{1}\{s_1 = k\}}{T}. && \text{($n_{\cdot k} = n_k - \mathbf{1}\{s_1 = k\}$)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P28.S5] The occupancy frequencies therefore satisfy the stationarity equation $\hat\pi \widehat{\mathbf{T}} = \hat\pi$ up to a fixed-point residual bounded by $1/T$.
[appendix.P28.S6] Because the counted chain mixes within a few steps (Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-nojump]), a residual of this size moves the solution by a comparable amount.
[appendix.P28.S7] For the fitted SPY model, the solved eigenvector $\bar\pi$ deviates from the occupancy frequencies by at most $3.7 \times 10^{-4}$, the same order as $1/T$, against occupancy values between $0.0058$ and $0.017$ (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).
[appendix.P28.S8] Thus $\bar\pi$ differs from empirical occupancy by order $1/T$ for this rapidly mixing fit.

[appendix.P30.S3] Marginalizing the state at a single time gives the mixture density and CDF:

--- Paragraph appendix.P31 (appendix.tex:831) ---
[appendix.P31.S1] The second moment makes the role of the emission scale convention explicit.
[appendix.P31.S2] Conditioning on the state and applying the law of total variance gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \Var(G_t) &\;=\; \E\big[\Var(G_t \mid S_t)\big] \;+\; \Var\big(\E[G_t \mid S_t]\big) && \text{(law of total variance)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2} \sum_k \bar\pi_k\, \sigma_k^2 \;+\; \sum_k \bar\pi_k\, (\mu_k - \bar\mu)^2, && \text{(Student-$t$ variance)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P31.S3] where $\bar\mu = \sum_k \bar\pi_k \mu_k$ is the stationary mean.
[appendix.P31.S4] Define the within-state variance $V_{\rm w} = \sum_k \bar\pi_k \sigma_k^2$ and the between-state variance $V_{\rm b} = \sum_k \bar\pi_k (\mu_k - \bar\mu)^2$.
[appendix.P31.S5] The corresponding empirical decomposition of the sample variance is $V_{\rm b} + V_{\rm w}$, up to the $n_k - 1$ versus $n_k$ denominators of the within-state variances and the $\bar\pi$ versus $\hat\pi$ weights, so the Student-$t$ scale convention adds $2 V_{\rm w} / (\nu - 2)$ relative to the corresponding Gaussian mixture.
[appendix.P31.S6] At the SPY fit (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]), $V_{\rm w} = 0.17 \mathrm{yr}^{-2}$, only $3.6%$ of the total variance of $4.60 \mathrm{yr}^{-2}$: most dispersion lies between states.
[appendix.P31.S7] The inflation is correspondingly small, a model variance of $4.72$ against $4.60 \mathrm{yr}^{-2}$ ($2.17$ against $2.14 \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ in standard deviation), and it is absent under Gaussian emissions, whose model variance of $4.61 \mathrm{yr}^{-2}$ differs from the sample value only through those denominator and weight corrections.

[appendix.P32.S1] The fourth moment explains the emission comparison in Table [tab:emission_hsmm].
[appendix.P32.S2] Write $G_t = \mu_k + \sigma_k X$ within state $k$, where $X$ is a standard Student-$t$ draw, and let $\delta_k = \mu_k - \bar\mu$.
[appendix.P32.S3] Expanding the fourth central moment binomially and dropping the odd-power terms in $X$, which vanish by the symmetry of $\psi_\nu$, gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \E\big[(G_t - \bar\mu)^4\big] &\;=\; \sum_k \bar\pi_k\, \E\big[(\delta_k + \sigma_k X)^4\big] && \text{(condition on the state)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_k \bar\pi_k \Big[ \delta_k^4 + 4\, \delta_k^3 \sigma_k\, \E(X) + 6\, \delta_k^2 \sigma_k^2\, \E(X^2) \notag \\ &\hspace{6em} + 4\, \delta_k \sigma_k^3\, \E(X^3) + \sigma_k^4\, \E(X^4)\Big] && \text{(binomial expansion)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_k \bar\pi_k \left[ \delta_k^4 + 6\, \delta_k^2\, \sigma_k^2\, \E(X^2)\right. \notag \\ &\hspace{6em}\left.{} + \sigma_k^4\, \E(X^4) \right], && \text{($\E X = \E X^3 = 0$)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P32.S4] The standard Student-$t$ moments are finite for $\nu > 4$ and satisfy:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \E(X^2) \;=\; \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}, \qquad \E(X^4) \;=\; \frac{3 \nu^2}{(\nu - 2)(\nu - 4)}.  \end{equation}
[appendix.P32.S5] At $\nu = 5$ these values are $5/3$ and $25$: the within-state kurtosis is $9$ (excess kurtosis $6$), against $3$ (excess kurtosis $0$) for Gaussian emissions.
[appendix.P32.S6] The excess kurtosis of the stationary mixture is $\E[(G_t - \bar\mu)^4] / \Var(G_t)^2 - 3$; evaluating it at the SPY fit gives $8.34$ with Student-$t$ emissions and $5.82$ with Gaussian emissions, against an observed value of $7.72$ (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).
[appendix.P32.S7] The emission choice enters Eq.
[appendix.P32.S8] Equation (eq:supp-fourth) through both moments in Eq.
[appendix.P32.S9] Equation (eq:supp-t-moments); the larger change by far is $\E(X^4)$ rising from $3$ to $25$, which acts on the $\sigma_k^4$ terms that the wide tail states dominate.
[appendix.P32.S10] The simulated HMM-WJ values ($7.5$ and $5.5$) are lower than these no-jump population values because jumps lower kurtosis and finite heavy-tailed samples underestimate it (Table [tab:emission_hsmm]).
[appendix.P32.S11] Asymmetric state locations, scales and occupancies also let the mixture reproduce skewness despite symmetric individual emissions (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).


\subsection{Dependence structure without jumps}


--- Paragraph appendix.P33 (appendix.tex:907) ---

--- Paragraph appendix.P34 (appendix.tex:910) ---
[appendix.P34.S1] The no-jump generator loses return-magnitude dependence too quickly (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).
[appendix.P34.S2] Within state $k$, the sojourn time is geometric: the chain remains for $m$ steps with probability $\mathbf{T}_{kk}^{\,m-1} (1 - \mathbf{T}_{kk})$ and therefore stays for $1/(1 - \mathbf{T}_{kk})$ steps on average, between one and two days for every fitted state under the fitted transitions.
[appendix.P34.S3] Short visits alone do not establish rapid mixing; the chain also leaves either tail set within one to two steps on average.
[appendix.P34.S4] The tail sets were $\mathcal{S}_{-}=\{1,\ldots,5\}$ and $\mathcal{S}_{+}=\{96,\ldots,100\}$.
[appendix.P34.S5] Every SPY state was populated, so empty-row fallbacks did not cause these short visits.
[appendix.P34.S6] Let $a_k = \E\big[\,|G_t| \,\big|\, S_t = k\big]$ be the within-state mean absolute return and $\bar a = \sum_k \bar\pi_k a_k$ its stationary average.
[appendix.P34.S7] Because observations are conditionally independent given the states, the lag-$\tau$ product moment reduces to a state-pair sum:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \E\big[\,|G_t|\, |G_{t+\tau}|\,\big] &\;=\; \sum_{j, k} \Pr(S_t = j,\, S_{t+\tau} = k)\; a_j\, a_k && \text{(conditional independence)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \sum_{j, k} \bar\pi_j\, \big(\mathbf{T}^\tau\big)_{jk}\; a_j\, a_k, && \text{(Chapman--Kolmogorov)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P34.S8] so the autocorrelation function (ACF) of $|G_t|$ is:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \mathrm{ACF}_{|G|}(\tau) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{j, k} \bar\pi_j\, \big(\mathbf{T}^\tau\big)_{jk}\, a_j\, a_k \;-\; \bar a^2} {\Var(|G_t|)}.  \end{equation}
[appendix.P34.S9] For an ergodic, diagonalizable transition matrix, $\mathbf{T}^\tau$ approaches the rank-one limit with rows $\bar\pi$ at geometric rate $|\theta_2|^\tau$, where $\theta_2$ is the second-largest eigenvalue in modulus; the ACF is then bounded by a constant multiple of $|\theta_2|^\tau$.
[appendix.P34.S10] Direct evaluation of Eq.
[appendix.P34.S11] Equation (eq:supp-acf-nj-def) gives the fitted-chain decay reported here.
[appendix.P34.S12] For the fitted SPY matrix (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]), $|\theta_2| = 0.253$, and $|\theta_2|^\tau$ falls below $0.01$ from lag $4$ onward.
[appendix.P34.S13] Evaluating Eq.
[appendix.P34.S14] Equation (eq:supp-acf-nj) gives a model ACF of $0.26$ at lag $1$, close to the observed $0.30$ because the counted matrix pins the one-step state-pair law, but $0.021$ at lag $3$ and effectively zero from lag $5$, while the observed ACF remains near $0.26$ at lag $5$ and $0.14$ at lag $25$ (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]; Fig.
[appendix.P34.S15] [fig:empirical_motivation]d).
[appendix.P34.S16] Matching counted one-step transitions fixes $\mathbf{T}$ and therefore its longer-lag dependence; the small $|\theta_2| = 0.253$ motivates the added duration mechanism.


\subsection{Jump-episode arithmetic}


--- Paragraph appendix.P35 (appendix.tex:949) ---

--- Paragraph appendix.P36 (appendix.tex:951) ---
[appendix.P36.S1] Renewal arguments quantify the frequency and duration of forced-tail episodes.
[appendix.P36.S2] On each step with no active episode, an episode with at least one forced step begins with probability $p_e = \epsilon (1 - e^{-\lambda})$, since a trigger whose Poisson draw returns zero is discarded; conditional on starting, the episode length is the zero-truncated Poisson draw with mean $\E[K \mid K \ge 1] = \lambda / (1 - e^{-\lambda})$.
[appendix.P36.S3] At $\lambda = 90$, the correction $e^{-\lambda}$ is below $10^{-39}$, so $p_e = \epsilon$ and $\E[K \mid K \ge 1] = \lambda$ to machine precision.
[appendix.P36.S4] The long-run fraction of forced steps, a scalar denoted $\pi_J$, follows from the renewal-reward theorem.
[appendix.P36.S5] A renewal cycle consists of the free steps preceding an episode and the episode itself.
[appendix.P36.S6] The number of steps up to and including the trigger is geometric with mean $1/p_e$, but the triggering step is itself the first forced step, so a cycle contains on average $1/p_e - 1 = (1 - p_e)/p_e$ free steps, and the episode contributes $\E[K \mid K \ge 1]$ forced steps in total:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \pi_J &\;=\; \frac{\E[K \mid K \ge 1]} {\dfrac{1 - p_e}{p_e} + \E[K \mid K \ge 1]} && \text{(forced steps over cycle length)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \frac{p_e\, \E[K \mid K \ge 1]} {1 - p_e + p_e\, \E[K \mid K \ge 1]} && \text{(multiply through by $p_e$)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \frac{\epsilon \lambda} {1 - \epsilon + \epsilon e^{-\lambda} + \epsilon \lambda} && \text{($p_e\, \E[K \mid K \ge 1] = \epsilon \lambda$)} \notag \\ &\;\approx\; \frac{\epsilon \lambda}{1 + \epsilon \lambda} \;=\; 0.0089. && \text{(drop $\epsilon e^{-\lambda}$, then $\epsilon$)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P36.S7] Simulation gave $0.0088$ across $400$ paths (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).
[appendix.P36.S8] The product $\epsilon \lambda$ sets this unconditional fraction, so the fraction alone cannot separate the two parameters; Online Appendix [sec:supp-gen-tuning] explains why conditioning on paths with an episode emphasizes duration while leaving the trigger probability weakly determined.

--- Paragraph appendix.P37 (appendix.tex:989) ---
[appendix.P37.S1] At the path level, the same arithmetic determines how episodes are distributed across the ensemble.
[appendix.P37.S2] A simulated path contains no episode only if every one of its $T - 1$ transition steps fails to trigger one, so the jump-path probability at $T = 2,766$ is $1 - (1 - p_e)^{T-1} = 24.2%$; the simulated ensemble gives $24.8%$ ($99$ of $400$ paths; Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]), consistent with the $\sim\!24%$ jump-active share in the main comparison.
[appendix.P37.S3] We call these jump paths.
[appendix.P37.S4] Using the long-run episode rate, dividing the $T - 1$ transition steps by the mean cycle length $(1 - p_e)/p_e + \lambda$ gives about $0.274$ episodes per path in expectation.
[appendix.P37.S5] The free waiting time is approximately $1/\epsilon = 10^4$ trading days, about $40$ trading years, far longer than the $11$-year path, so roughly three quarters follow the HMM-NJ law, while about one jump path in eight contains multiple episodes.
[appendix.P37.S6] Ignoring horizon truncation, one episode of mean duration gives a forced fraction near $\lambda / T = 3.3%$; the simulated mean is $3.6%$ (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]), with multi-episode paths increasing the average.
[appendix.P37.S7] The contrast between forced and free steps explains the added magnitude dependence.
[appendix.P37.S8] Under the forced-step distribution $u$ of Eq.
[appendix.P37.S9] Equation (eq:supp-u), the conditional standard deviation is $5.38 \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ against the free-step $2.17 \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, a variance ratio of $6.1$, and the mean absolute return is $\bar a_J = \sum_k u_k a_k = 4.96 \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ against $\bar a = 1.47 \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ (Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities]).
[appendix.P37.S10] The same split describes the state distribution of the augmented chain.
[appendix.P37.S11] On free steps the state is distributed as $\bar\pi$, except during the few steps after an episode ends, when the chain relaxes from the last forced tail state back to $\bar\pi$ at the rate $|\theta_2|$.
[appendix.P37.S12] The long-run state marginal is therefore approximately $(1 - \pi_J)\,\bar\pi + \pi_J\,u$.
[appendix.P37.S13] Its forced-state contribution has weight $O(\pi_J) \approx 1%$; this is not an error bound for the full augmented initial law, which also sets the episode counter to zero.
[appendix.P37.S14] Thus $\bar\pi$ supplies only an approximate stationary start.


\subsection{Autocorrelation and kurtosis under jumps}


--- Paragraph appendix.P38 (appendix.tex:1015) ---

--- Paragraph appendix.P39 (appendix.tex:1017) ---
[appendix.P39.S1] To explain what $(\epsilon, \lambda)$ control in Eq.
[appendix.P39.S2] Equation (eq:grid_search), define the episode-overlap probability: the probability $h(\tau)$ that, given a step is forced, the step $\tau$ later belongs to the same episode.
[appendix.P39.S3] A uniformly chosen forced step falls in an episode of length $m$ with probability proportional to $m \Pr(K = m \mid K \ge 1)$, because an episode of length $m$ contains $m$ forced steps, and its position within that episode is uniform.
[appendix.P39.S4] Combining the two gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} h(\tau) &\;=\; \sum_{m \ge 1} \frac{m\, \Pr(K = m \mid K \ge 1)}{\E[K \mid K \ge 1]} \cdot \frac{(m - \tau)_+}{m} \notag \\ &\;=\; \frac{\E\big[(K - \tau)_+ \,\big|\, K \ge 1\big]} {\E[K \mid K \ge 1]} && \text{(sum over $m$)} \notag \\ &\;\approx\; \Big(1 - \frac{\tau}{\lambda}\Big)_{\!+}, && \text{($K$ concentrated near $\lambda$)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P39.S5] where $(x)_+ = \max(x, 0)$ and the approximation holds because the Poisson length concentrates within a few multiples of $\sqrt{\lambda} \approx 9.5$ around $\lambda$.
[appendix.P39.S6] The same-episode probability therefore decays almost linearly from one to zero over the horizon $\lambda$.

--- Paragraph appendix.P40 (appendix.tex:1042) ---
[appendix.P40.S1] The ACF follows by conditioning on the forced-step indicator $Z_t = \mathbf{1}\{\text{step $t$ is forced}\}$.
[appendix.P40.S2] Forced states are independent draws from $u$, followed by independent emissions from those states.
[appendix.P40.S3] Dependence between free observations, or between an episode and subsequent free steps, decays at rate $|\theta_2|^\tau$.
[appendix.P40.S4] Neglecting this dependence and post-episode relaxation leaves covariance between the phase means.
[appendix.P40.S5] Write $A_t = |G_t|$ for the absolute return.
[appendix.P40.S6] Under this approximation, the conditional mean is $\E[A_t \mid Z_t] = \bar a + Z_t\, (\bar a_J - \bar a)$, and for rare episodes the joint forced probability is $\Pr(Z_t = 1, Z_{t+\tau} = 1) \approx \pi_J\, h(\tau)$, so:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \Cov\big(A_t, A_{t+\tau}\big) &\;\approx\; \Cov\big(\E[A_t \mid Z_t],\; \E[A_{t+\tau} \mid Z_{t+\tau}]\big) && \text{(independence given $Z$)} \notag \\ &\;=\; (\bar a_J - \bar a)^2\, \Cov\big(Z_t,\, Z_{t+\tau}\big) && \text{(conditional mean in $Z$)} \notag \\ &\;\approx\; (\bar a_J - \bar a)^2\, \pi_J\, h(\tau), && \text{(episodes rare)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P40.S7] so the jump mechanism adds an ACF component of height $\pi_J (\bar a_J - \bar a)^2 / \Var(A_t)$ that decays almost linearly to zero at lag $\lambda$, precisely the slow component the counted chain cannot produce.
[appendix.P40.S8] For a jump path carrying a single episode, the probability that a step is forced is $\lambda / T$ rather than $\pi_J$, because the path contains about $\lambda$ forced steps among its $T$ steps.
[appendix.P40.S9] Replacing $\pi_J$ by $\lambda / T$ and using the absolute-return variance of the fitted two-phase mixture, $\Var(A_t) = 3.01$, gives a predicted height of $0.13$ at short lags, the plateau entry of Table [tab:supp-generator-quantities].
[appendix.P40.S10] Among $99$ jump-active paths out of $400$, the predicted and simulated ACF differed by $0.013$ at lag $5$ and by less than $0.01$ from lag $10$ onward.
[appendix.P40.S11] At lag $1$, the simulation gave $0.35$ against the episode contribution of $0.13$, because the short-lived free-phase contribution was omitted from the approximation.

\section{SIM composition derivations and properties verification}


--- Paragraph appendix.P48 (appendix.tex:1186) ---

--- Paragraph appendix.P49 (appendix.tex:1188) ---
[appendix.P49.S1] The composition identities below use sample moments of the supplied paths.
[appendix.P49.S2] Dropping the market cross-covariance is exact only when its sample value is zero (Online Appendix [sec:supp-cov]).
[appendix.P49.S3] To derive Eq.
[appendix.P49.S4] Equation (eq:scale), first center the full-return draw as in Eq.
[appendix.P49.S5] Equation (eq:center-generator), then set $\eps_i=s_i\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$ for $s_i\in[0,1]$ and require that the composed series have the same variance as the generator path:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \Var(g_i) \;=\; \Var(\alpha_i + \beta_i\, g_m + \eps_i) \;=\; \Var(\beta_i\, g_m + \eps_i) \;=\; \sigma_{\gen,i}^2, \end{equation}
[appendix.P49.S6] The constant $\alpha_i$ drops out; expanding the sum gives:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \Var(\beta_i\, g_m + \eps_i) \;=\; \beta_i^2\, \Var(g_m) \;+\; 2 \beta_i\, \Cov(g_m,\, \eps_i) \;+\; \Var(\eps_i). \end{equation}
[appendix.P49.S7] Substituting $\eps_i = s_i\,\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$ gives $\Var(\eps_i) = s_i^2\,\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ and $\Cov(g_m,\, \eps_i) = s_i\,\Cov(g_m,\, \tg_i^{\mathrm c})$.
[appendix.P49.S8] Under the assumption $\Cov(\tg_i^{\mathrm c},\,g_m)\approx0$, the cross term vanishes and the variance condition becomes $\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2 + s_i^2\, \sigma_{\gen,i}^2 = \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$.
[appendix.P49.S9] Solving for $s_i^2$ gives Eq.
[appendix.P49.S10] Equation (eq:scale).

--- Paragraph appendix.P50 (appendix.tex:1213) ---
[appendix.P50.S1] To derive Eq.
[appendix.P50.S2] Equation (eq:r2-target), use the same zero-cross-covariance assumption and write the $R^2$ of the composed series of Eq.
[appendix.P50.S3] Equation (eq:sim-composition) regressed on the market:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} R^2 \;=\; \frac{\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2}{\beta_i^2\, \sigma_m^2 + \sigma_{\eps,i}^2}. \end{equation}
[appendix.P50.S4] Setting $R^2$ equal to the calibrated $R^2_{i,\real}$ and solving for the residual variance gives the target $\sigma_{\eps,\mathrm{target}}^2$ of Eq.
[appendix.P50.S5] Equation (eq:r2-target).
[appendix.P50.S6] This branch applies only to assets whose calibrated $R^2$ is at least $R^2_{\mathrm{preserve}} = 0.80$.
[appendix.P50.S7] Only QQQ and SPYG crossed this threshold; the other $421$ assets used variance preservation.
[appendix.P50.S8] Across $100$ draws per tracker, median absolute loading and $R^2$ errors were $0.005$ and $0.001$.
[appendix.P50.S9] No training path clipped:
[appendix.P50.S10] $\rho_i = \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 / \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ stayed below $1-f=0.90$.
[appendix.P50.S11] A Gaussian check used $\beta\in\{0.8,1.0,1.2\}$ and $R^2\in\{0.80,0.85,0.90,0.95,0.99\}$, with observed SPY and zero intercept.
[appendix.P50.S12] Reference and composed residuals were independent Gaussian draws with variance $\sigma_{\eps,\mathrm{target}}^2$ from Eq.
[appendix.P50.S13] Equation (eq:r2-target).
[appendix.P50.S14] Supplying this target as $\sigma_{\gen}^2$ made the scale in Eq.
[appendix.P50.S15] Equation (eq:r2-rescale) one; only the composed draw was centered.
[appendix.P50.S16] All cells used the intended branch; across $100$ replications per cell, median loading and $R^2$ errors were at most $0.0011$ and $0.0017$.
[appendix.P50.S17] Independent residuals still caused finite-path differences.
[appendix.P50.S18] This checked the branch formula, not full-return reuse or temporal fit; shared SPY and unequal centering also prevent interpreting its KS pass rates as calibrated tests.

--- Paragraph appendix.P51 (appendix.tex:1225) ---
[appendix.P51.S1] To distinguish intercept retention from mean preservation, let $\overline{g}_m$ be the sample mean of the supplied market path.
[appendix.P51.S2] Centering and scaling give $\overline{\eps}_i=0$ exactly, and the implementation retains the fitted intercept $\alpha_i$ in every branch.
[appendix.P51.S3] The resulting mean and its change from the calibrated linear mean on the same market path are:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \overline{g}_i &= \alpha_i+\beta_i^{\eff}\overline{g}_m, \notag\\ \overline{g}_i-(\alpha_i+\beta_i\overline{g}_m) &= (\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m.  \end{align}
[appendix.P51.S4] Retaining the intercept therefore preserves this linear mean when the loading is unchanged, and a clipped path has a different mean whenever $\overline{g}_m\ne0$.
[appendix.P51.S5] The residual floor determines the clipped loading without an offsetting intercept adjustment.
[appendix.P51.S6] In the training comparison, the OLS calibration also implies $\alpha_i=\overline{g}_{i,\real}-\beta_i\overline{g}_{m,\real}$, where the bars with subscript $\real$ denote observed training means.
[appendix.P51.S7] The difference from the observed asset mean then separates into a change in market mean and a change in loading:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \overline{g}_i-\overline{g}_{i,\real} = \beta_i(\overline{g}_m-\overline{g}_{m,\real}) +(\beta_i^{\eff}-\beta_i)\overline{g}_m.  \end{equation}
[appendix.P51.S8] Using the observed training SPY path with $\beta_i^{\eff}=\beta_i$ therefore matches the observed asset mean exactly.
[appendix.P51.S9] The stress transformation $g_m^{(\gamma)}(t)=\overline{g}_m+\gamma[g_m(t)-\overline{g}_m]$, with $\gamma\in\{1,2,3\}$, preserved the market mean while multiplying its standard deviation by $\gamma$.
[appendix.P51.S10] Holding calibrated marginals fixed, clipping affected $0.0%$, $76.4%$ and $95.2%$ of evaluated composed paths across the $423$ assets, respectively; only clipping changed means in this check.
[appendix.P51.S11] A different supplied market mean can also change asset means without clipping.
[appendix.P51.S12] Small fitted intercept errors therefore reflect the imposed mean constraint (Table [tab:centering_control]).

--- Paragraph appendix.P52 (appendix.tex:1255) ---
[appendix.P52.S1] Retaining $\alpha_i$ also does not make the OLS intercept fitted to a finite composed path equal $\alpha_i$ exactly.
[appendix.P52.S2] Because the composed path is $g_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i^{\eff} g_m + \eps_i$, the OLS slope and intercept on a finite path are:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \hat\beta_i &\;=\; \frac{\Cov(\alpha_i + \beta_i^{\eff} g_m + \eps_i,\, g_m)}{\Var(g_m)} && \text{(OLS slope)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \beta_i^{\eff} + \frac{\Cov(\eps_i,\, g_m)}{\Var(g_m)}, && \text{($\Cov(g_m, g_m) = \Var(g_m)$)} \notag \\ \hat\alpha_i &\;=\; \overline{g}_i-\hat\beta_i\overline{g}_m && \text{(OLS intercept)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \alpha_i-(\hat\beta_i-\beta_i^{\eff})\overline{g}_m. && \text{(Eq. Equation (eq:supp-clipped-mean))} \end{align}
[appendix.P52.S3] With independent generator and market draws, convergent sample moments, and nonzero limiting market variance, the scaled sample cross-covariance vanishes as $T$ grows, so $\hat\beta_i \to \beta_i^{\eff}$ and $\hat\alpha_i \to \alpha_i$.
[appendix.P52.S4] Here $\beta_i^{\eff}=\beta_i$ unless Eq.
[appendix.P52.S5] Equation (eq:clip) clips the market loading.
[appendix.P52.S6] On the variance-preserving branch, the composed variance equals $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ when the sample cross-covariance is zero: without clipping, $\beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 + s_i^2 \sigma_{\gen,i}^2 = \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ by Eq.
[appendix.P52.S7] Equation (eq:scale), and under clipping, Eq.
[appendix.P52.S8] Equation (eq:clip) sets $\beta_i^{\eff}$ to give the same variance.
[appendix.P52.S9] On the $R^2$-preserving branch, the composed variance is $\beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 / R^2_{i,\real}$, the value implied by the calibrated $(\beta_i, R^2_{i,\real})$ rather than by the generator marginal.
[appendix.P52.S10] On the variance-preserving branch, clipped or not, the residual floor guarantees only that $\Var(\eps_i)\geq f\,\sigma_{\gen,i}^2$; it does not guarantee that adding the market term preserves the generator's tail distribution (Online Appendix [sec:supp-residual-choice]).

--- Paragraph appendix.P53 (appendix.tex:1290) ---
[appendix.P53.S1] The zero-sum constraint also fixes the residual contribution to the terminal cumulative log return.
[appendix.P53.S2] With time step $\Delta t$, and because the centered residuals sum to zero, summing the composed growth rates gives the exact identity:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} \Delta t\sum_{t=1}^{T} g_i(t) = T\Delta t\,\alpha_i + \beta_i^{\eff}\Delta t\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_m(t), \qquad \sum_{t=1}^{T}\eps_i(t)=0.  \end{equation}
[appendix.P53.S3] For a fixed market path and effective loading, the residual contributes no terminal uncertainty.
[appendix.P53.S4] Partial sums before $T$ can still vary.
[appendix.P53.S5] If clipping changes the loading between draws, terminal returns can vary through $\beta_i^{\eff}$, but the residual sum remains zero.
[appendix.P53.S6] Centering uses the full horizon and is not equivalent to subtracting a fitted population mean: if the same draw is extended to a longer horizon, its first $T$ days receive a different centering constant and scale.
[appendix.P53.S7] These paths are therefore constrained over a specified horizon and do not represent unconstrained cumulative residual returns.


\subsection{Role of the centered full-return draw as the residual}


--- Paragraph appendix.P54 (appendix.tex:1312) ---

--- Paragraph appendix.P55 (appendix.tex:1314) ---
[appendix.P55.S1] The hybrid composition places $\eps_i = s_i \tg_i^{\mathrm c}$, a centered and rescaled draw of asset $i$'s full return, in the residual slot of Eq.
[appendix.P55.S2] Equation (eq:sim-composition).
[appendix.P55.S3] The distributions differ: the residual of a single-index decomposition is the idiosyncratic component of the return, orthogonal to the market, while $\tg_i^{\mathrm c}$ is distributed like the centered total return.
[appendix.P55.S4] Its variance and tails include observed market co-movement, although the draw itself is independent of the supplied market path.
[appendix.P55.S5] The required residual variance is $\sigma_{\gen,i}^2 - \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2$ on the unclipped variance-preserving branch.
[appendix.P55.S6] Centering gives the residual path an exactly zero sample mean (Eq.
[appendix.P55.S7] Equation (eq:center-generator)).
[appendix.P55.S8] On the unclipped variance-preserving branch, computing $\sigma_m^2$ in Eq.
[appendix.P55.S9] Equation (eq:scale) from the same market path used in the composition, as in training, gives the residual exactly the remaining variance.
[appendix.P55.S10] The composed variance also contains the sample cross-covariance term, which need not vanish on a finite path (Online Appendix [sec:supp-cov]).
[appendix.P55.S11] Using the frozen training market variance, as in the six-method holdout evaluation, adds a further variance difference if the simulated market variance differs from that training value.
[appendix.P55.S12] Standalone generator validation does not validate the reused regression-residual shape.

--- Paragraph appendix.P56 (appendix.tex:1332) ---
[appendix.P56.S1] At $\rho_i = 0$, the loading is zero, Eq.
[appendix.P56.S2] Equation (eq:scale) gives $s_i = 1$, and the composed path is the centered generator path shifted to the mean $\alpha_i$.
[appendix.P56.S3] It therefore has the same centered empirical distribution as the generator path, and it equals the uncentered generator path only if $\alpha_i$ equals the draw's sample mean.
[appendix.P56.S4] For $\rho_i > 0$, the independent market contribution changes even the centered distribution.
[appendix.P56.S5] To describe that change on the unclipped variance-preserving branch, we treat $s_i$ and $\beta_i$ as constants rather than recomputing them from each draw, and we work with population moments conditional on the fitted parameters, which the sample moments of a long path approach.
[appendix.P56.S6] Write the deviation of the composed return from its mean as $X_m + X_i$.
[appendix.P56.S7] The market contribution is $X_m = \beta_i\, g_m^{\mathrm c}$, where $g_m^{\mathrm c}$ is the market growth rate minus its mean, and the asset contribution is $X_i = s_i\, \tg_i^{\mathrm c}$, the scaled asset draw.
[appendix.P56.S8] Their variances are $v_m = \rho_i\, \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$ and $v_i = (1 - \rho_i)\, \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$, and independence factors the fourth-moment cross terms:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \E\big[(X_m + X_i)^4\big] &\;=\; \E(X_m^4) + 4\,\E(X_m^3)\,\E(X_i) \notag \\ &\qquad + 6\,\E(X_m^2)\,\E(X_i^2) + 4\,\E(X_m)\,\E(X_i^3) \notag \\ &\qquad + \E(X_i^4) && \text{(independence)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \E(X_m^4) + 6\, v_m\, v_i + \E(X_i^4). && \text{($\E X_m = \E X_i = 0$)}  \end{align}

--- Paragraph appendix.P57 (appendix.tex:1363) ---
[appendix.P57.S1] Writing $\kappa_m$ and $\kappa_{\gen}$ for the population excess kurtoses of the market and generator laws in this fixed-coefficient approximation, the component fourth moments are $\E(X_m^4) = (\kappa_m + 3)\, v_m^2$ and $\E(X_i^4) = (\kappa_{\gen} + 3)\, v_i^2$, where rescaling by $s_i$ leaves excess kurtosis unchanged.
[appendix.P57.S2] The excess kurtosis of the composed return follows by dividing Eq.
[appendix.P57.S3] Equation (eq:supp-comp-fourth) by the squared total variance:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{align} \kappa(X_m + X_i) &\;=\; \frac{(\kappa_m + 3)\, v_m^2 + 6\, v_m\, v_i + (\kappa_{\gen} + 3)\, v_i^2}{(v_m + v_i)^2} \;-\; 3 \notag \\ &\;=\; \frac{\kappa_m\, v_m^2 + \kappa_{\gen}\, v_i^2 + 3\, (v_m + v_i)^2}{(v_m + v_i)^2} \;-\; 3 && \text{(collect terms)} \notag \\ &\;=\; \kappa_m\, \rho_i^2 \;+\; \kappa_{\gen}\, (1 - \rho_i)^2. && \text{($v_m + v_i = \sigma_{\gen,i}^2$)}  \end{align}
[appendix.P57.S4] The generator's excess kurtosis survives composition with weight $(1 - \rho_i)^2$ and the market's enters with weight $\rho_i^2$; a check combining $200$ independent, standardized SPY-generator path pairs as $\sqrt{\rho_i}\,x+\sqrt{1-\rho_i}\,y$ matched the prediction within $0.03$ in mean excess kurtosis at $\rho_i\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5\}$.
[appendix.P57.S5] For a small market variance share, most generator excess kurtosis survives: at $\rho_i = 0.10$, it retains weight $0.81$.
[appendix.P57.S6] As $\rho_i$ approaches one, the market contribution dominates.

[appendix.P57.S10] On the clipped and $R^2$-preserving branches, the same formula uses the market's actual share of composed variance in place of $\rho_i$.

[appendix.P61.S2] The control used frozen models and ordinary least squares (OLS) calibrations, observed training SPY, seed $1234$, and $100$ draws of length $2,766$ per asset, retaining each method's native scales.

[appendix.P61.S5] Naive and hybrid paths already satisfied the constraint and were reused without modification.

[appendix.P61.S7] This paired translation left sample variance, recovered loading, $R^2$, and excess kurtosis unchanged to numerical tolerance for every evaluated path.
[appendix.P61.S8] The control assessed mean treatment; it did not restore cumulative residual uncertainty or estimate parameter uncertainty.

[appendix.P61.S13] Pass rates remain descriptive under serial dependence and the fixed residual sum.

\section{Emission fallback inventory and sensitivity}


--- Paragraph appendix.P64 (appendix.tex:1501) ---

--- Paragraph appendix.P65 (appendix.tex:1503) ---
[appendix.P65.S1] Emission fallbacks replace within-state moments when a state is sparse or nearly constant.
[appendix.P65.S2] For one asset, let $n_k$ be the number of observations in state $k$, let $\bar G_k$ and $s_k$ be its sample mean and standard deviation when defined, and let $\bar G$ and $s_G$ be the corresponding full-series quantities.
[appendix.P65.S3] The implemented emission rule was:
    [DISPLAY] \begin{equation} (\mu_k,\sigma_k)= \begin{cases} (\bar G,s_G), & n_k<2,\\ (\bar G_k,s_G), & n_k\ge2,\ s_k<\tau,\\ (\bar G_k,s_k), & n_k\ge2,\ s_k\ge\tau, \end{cases}
[appendix.P65.S4] \qquad \tau=10^{-12}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}.
[appendix.P65.S5] \end{equation} The scale $\sigma_k$ multiplies a standard Student-$t$ draw; it is not the emission standard deviation.
[appendix.P65.S6] State intervals included their lower boundary and excluded their upper boundary.
[appendix.P65.S7] A transition row with no observed outgoing transition was set to $1/N$ in every column.
[appendix.P65.S8] Such rows are unidentified by observed transitions.
[appendix.P65.S9] Among the $424$ full-return models, $39$ had at least one fallback emission, affecting $50$ states.
[appendix.P65.S10] Of these, $39$ states contained $11$--$119$ identical observations each, seven were empty, and four contained one observation.
[appendix.P65.S11] The seven empty states also supplied the seven uniform transition rows.
[appendix.P65.S12] Fallback states carried $0.399$--$4.300%$ of stationary probability per affected asset.
[appendix.P65.S13] Sparse-state probability was at most $0.0363%$ per asset; most fallback mass belonged to populated states with repeated returns.
[appendix.P65.S14] SPY required no fallback, and neither did any of the $423$ residual-fit models.
[appendix.P65.S15] Fallback mass is $100\sum_{k\in\mathcal F}\bar\pi_k$, where $\mathcal F$ is the set of fallback states.
[appendix.P65.S16] The per-asset inventory retained in the replication files uses $n_{\rm c}$ for observations in a constant state and $n_0,n_1$ for empty and singleton state counts.
[appendix.P65.S17] Its diagnostic $\Delta V$ is the percentage change in stationary mixture variance after replacing only populated constant-state scales by their empirical values; all fits used $N=100$ and $\nu=5$.

[appendix.P66.S1] To isolate the consequence of broadening populated constant states, we compared the fitted generators with an empirical-scale alternative on all $39$ affected non-market assets (Table [tab:fallback_sensitivity]).
[appendix.P66.S2] The alternative replaced $s_G$ by $s_k$ only where $n_k\ge2$ and $s_k<\tau$; locations, partitions, transitions, initial probabilities, and sparse-state fallbacks were unchanged.
[appendix.P66.S3] A zero empirical scale gives a point mass at the state mean.
[appendix.P66.S4] We replayed the original $100$ paths of $2,766$ observations per asset, paired by state sequence and random draws, with jumps disabled and the observed training SPY path shared by both treatments.
[appendix.P66.S5] We scored the uncomposed generator and the naive and hybrid compositions; each composition used its treatment's generator variance.
[appendix.P66.S6] Pass fractions remain descriptive for these dependent series with ties.
[appendix.P66.S7] The empirical-scale alternative improved marginal-fit scores on this training subset (Table [tab:fallback_sensitivity]).
[appendix.P66.S8] Hybrid KS and AD pass rates rose by $10.28$ and $14.08$ percentage points, with Monte Carlo standard errors of $0.49$ and $0.60$.
[appendix.P66.S9] For the uncomposed generators, the change in stationary mixture variance ranged from $-0.651%$ to $-6.520%$ (Online Appendix [sec:supp-fallback]).
[appendix.P66.S10] The fallback scale therefore affected both the generator's variance and the composed marginal fit.
[appendix.P66.S11] All main comparisons retain the original fallback rule.
