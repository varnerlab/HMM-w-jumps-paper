                                                Abstract
            Synthetic multi-asset equity data must reproduce each asset’s return distribution
            and its relationship with the market. Reusing a generator fitted to full asset returns
            creates a problem: adding its draws to a market factor counts market variance twice.
            We derived a correction that centers and rescales each draw over a fixed horizon
            before adding the market factor, allowing reuse without fitting a second generator
            to regression residuals. We tested the correction on 423 non-market assets in a
            424-asset United States equity and exchange-traded-fund universe, using hidden
            Markov generators with heavy-tailed emissions. The corrected paths retained
            heavy tails and recovered the calibrated market loadings with low error. On 416
            complete asset histories held out from 2025, the correction improved the mean
            Kolmogorov–Smirnov pass rate over naive composition and brought the median
            ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one. Its one-day left-tail 99% Value-
            at-Risk exceedance rate, using thresholds pooled across separate simulated paths,
            was 1.12%, compared with 1.16% for the residual-fit hidden Markov comparator
            and a nominal rate of 1%. We also tested a jump-duration mechanism that extended
            visits to extreme-return states and improved volatility clustering for the broad-
            market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY. The multi-asset correction remained
            effective with jumps enabled, but transferring the SPY jump settings improved
            temporal fit in training and worsened it in the 2025 holdout. The method provides
            a way to reuse fitted asset generators for daily-fit comparisons. Its zero-sum resid-
            ual constraint removes terminal residual uncertainty, and unmodeled dependence
            among asset-specific residuals can lead to overstated rebalancing returns. Code,
            cached inputs, result summaries, and instructions for fitting the per-asset models
            accompany the paper.


Keywords: hidden Markov model; jump-duration mechanism; single-index model; synthetic financial

1   Introduction
Researchers and practitioners use synthetic equity paths to test risk models, evaluate portfolio
strategies, and examine market scenarios beyond those observed in a historical sample [1, 2]. A multi-
asset simulator must represent both the behavior of each asset and the relationships among assets.
Factor models link many assets through a shared market path, a time series of market returns [3–5].
The simplest of these, the single-index model, separates each asset’s return into an intercept, a
market component scaled by the asset’s fitted loading, and a zero-mean asset-specific residual. A
synthetic asset path can then be composed by adding a simulated residual to the intercept and market
component. If single-asset generators have already been fitted to each asset’s full returns, their paths
might be reused as residuals. Each generator, however, already represents its asset’s total variance,
including the part driven by the market. Adding its paths to the market component therefore counts
market variance a second time. Subtracting each path’s mean removes its mean shift but leaves this
excess variance. Fitting a second generator to each asset’s regression residuals avoids the problem
but repeats the fitting for every asset. Reusing the existing generators would avoid that second fit,
provided their paths could be transformed to supply only the residual contribution. The question
is whether such a transformation can control the mean and variance of the composed paths while
retaining the generator’s heavy tails and volatility clustering.
The transformation is useful only if each generator reproduces the behavior of its own asset. We
illustrate the features a generator must capture with the broad-market exchange-traded fund with
ticker SPY, which also supplied the market path in our multi-asset experiments. Its 2014–2024 returns
show heavy tails and volatility clustering (Fig. 1). Most returns lie close to zero, but extreme values
occur much more often than a Gaussian model predicts (Fig. 1a,b), a widely documented feature of
financial returns [6, 7]. Raw returns show little correlation across days, while absolute returns remain
positively correlated across many lags (Fig. 1c,d). Large and small absolute returns therefore cluster
in time, a property that synthetic financial data often miss even when their return histogram appears
realistic [8]. To represent heavy tails and volatility clustering, a hidden Markov model (HMM) draws
returns from state-dependent distributions and uses transitions between states to represent temporal
behavior [9, 10]. Each state in the model used here has a heavy-tailed Student-t return distribution,
and the next state depends only on the current state, not on earlier returns. Fitted to SPY, the model
leaves states with the most negative or positive mean returns quickly, and the autocorrelation of
its absolute returns decays faster than in the data. A duration mechanism that occasionally keeps
drawing from extreme-return states for consecutive days can slow that decay [11]. Adding the market
component, however, changes the distribution and temporal behavior of the composed returns. An
improvement in a single-asset generator must therefore be checked again after composition.
In this study, we developed a variance correction to reuse full-return generators in a single-index
model without counting market variance twice. The correction centered and rescaled each simulated
path to remove the mean shift and account for the variance supplied by the market component (Fig. 4).
We applied it to heavy-tailed hidden Markov generators for the 423 non-market assets in a 424-asset
universe, using 2014–2024 for fitting and 2025 as a holdout. We compared the correction with
naive composition, which centered the paths without rescaling, with Gaussian residuals, and with
generators fitted directly to regression residuals. The correction improved agreement with observed
return distributions over naive composition while retaining heavy tails in training (Table 3). With
all fits held fixed, the correction also improved distributional fit over naive composition and brought
the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance close to one on the 416 assets with complete
holdout histories (Table 4). The residual-fit hidden Markov generator and the correction gave similar
agreement with observed return distributions in both periods. Without the duration mechanism, the
correction left absolute-return autocorrelation error essentially unchanged from naive composition
(Table 6). Transferring SPY’s duration settings to the composed paths improved their autocorrelation
agreement in training but worsened it in the holdout (Table 6). The correction imposed a zero residual
sum over each simulated path, removing cumulative residual uncertainty at the path endpoint. Neither
reuse nor residual fitting modeled dependence among different assets’ residuals, which portfolio
evaluation also requires [12, 13]. These results support reuse as a practical option for daily-return
comparisons when full-return generators already exist.

2   Related Work
Single-asset return models treat changing volatility, market regimes, and extreme moves in different
ways. Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) and generalized autoregressive condi-
tional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models allow conditional variance to change over time [14, 15].
Even with Gaussian innovations, the changing variance can produce an unconditional return distribu-
tion with excess kurtosis. Heavy-tailed innovations can improve the tail fit when Gaussian GARCH
understates the frequency of extreme returns. Stochastic-volatility models make volatility a latent
process [16–18]. Jump-diffusion models add discontinuous price moves [19, 20], but a jump com-
ponent alone does not explain persistent volatility. Markov-switching ARCH and GARCH models
combine discrete regimes with changing conditional variance [21, 22]. Hidden-state models represent
returns with a small number of market regimes. In a hidden Markov model (HMM), each latent state
has a separate return distribution. Gaussian-emission HMMs can reproduce heavy tails and weak
raw-return autocorrelation, but may still miss volatility clustering [23, 10]. Hidden semi-Markov
models allow more flexible state durations and can improve volatility persistence [11]. An HMM
does not create a jump process by itself. Rare extreme moves must come from the state-dependent
return distributions or from a transition rule designed for such events. Deep generative models learn
return distributions and temporal features without specifying a small set of market regimes or a
parametric return process. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are one class of deep generative
model. Quant GANs have reproduced several financial time-series features, including volatility
clustering and leverage effects [24]. Sig-Wasserstein GANs use path signatures to learn features that
depend on the order of observations [25]. In contrast, evaluations of GAN paths, including paths
from the time-series generative adversarial network (TimeGAN), have found too little autocorrelation
in absolute returns [26–28]. The conflicting results show that flexible architectures do not guarantee
a match to financial stylized facts. Model design, training, and evaluation all matter [1, 8].
Multi-asset generation also requires a model of cross-asset dependence. Multivariate GARCH models
estimate time-varying covariance matrices. The dynamic conditional correlation model reduces the
number of parameters by separating individual volatility models from correlation dynamics [29, 30].
Copulas separate each asset’s return distribution from the dependence model. Dynamic copulas allow
cross-asset dependence to change over time, while vine copulas provide flexible high-dimensional
constructions [31, 32]. Factor models reduce dimension by linking each asset to a small number of
shared factors [3–5]. Shared-state HMMs and multivariate GANs can also generate joint market
moves [33, 34]. A standard factor model can resample historical residuals or fit a separate residual
generator for every asset. This paper addresses a narrower problem. We start with single-asset
generators already fitted to full returns and ask whether they can be reused in a factor model. The
relevant comparison is with methods fitted directly to regression residuals: those methods remove
the market component before fitting, whereas reuse adjusts an existing full-return draw during
composition. Neither approach specifies cross-asset residual dependence unless that dependence is
modeled separately.

3   Methods
We fitted a single-asset generator for each asset and combined the generated paths with a variance-
corrected single-index model. The training data contained 424 United States-listed equities and
exchange-traded funds observed from 2014 through 2024, and data from 2025 served as a holdout.
The single-asset analysis focused on the broad-market exchange-traded fund with ticker SPY. It used
2,767 daily volume-weighted average prices for fitting, which yielded 2,766 growth rates, and the
next 250 prices from 2025, which yielded 249 holdout growth rates. The multi-asset analysis used
daily closing prices for all assets, with SPY as the market, so SPY had two fitted generators: one on
volume-weighted prices for the single-asset analysis and one on closing prices for the market path.
Of the other 423 assets in the training universe, 416 had complete 2025 histories and were included
in the multi-asset holdout evaluation. We defined the excess growth rate Gi,j for ticker i between
trading days j − 1 and j from prices Pi,j−1 and Pi,j .

3.1   Per-asset hidden Markov marginal generator

For each asset, the hidden Markov generator divided the range of growth rates, which we also call
returns, into N = 100 ordered intervals and treated the interval that contained a day’s return as that
day’s state (Table 1). We write t for the day index j and omit the ticker index i when discussing one
asset. At each simulated time step, the model first selected a state and then drew a return from that
state’s distribution (Fig. 2). We represented the model as M = (S, O, T, E, π̄) [9]. The hidden
state St ∈ S = {1, . . . , N } identifies the interval on trading day t, and the observation space O ⊂ R
contains the excess growth rates Gt of Eq. (1). The transition matrix T sets the probabilities of
moving between states, E gives the return distribution within each state, and π̄ gives the initial state
probabilities. We ordered the states from the most negative to the most positive returns and placed
their boundaries at the quantiles of a Laplace distribution, fitted to the asset’s in-sample returns, at
the equally spaced probability levels k/N . We used the Laplace distribution because its sharp peak
matches the concentration of small price movements in Fig. 1a [35, 36]. Its quantile function also
has a closed form, so the boundaries for all 424 tickers required no iterative fitting [37]. Specifically,
Qk = FL−1 (k/N ; µL , bL ), where FL−1 is the Laplace quantile function and µL and bL are the fitted
location and scale. We set Q0 = −∞ and QN = +∞ and assigned return Gt to state k when
Qk−1 ≤ Gt < Qk . Because each training return determines its state, the fitted state sequence is
known; the states are hidden only in simulated paths, where the model generates the state sequence
and reports only the returns.
The within-state emission followed a location-scale Student-t distribution:
                              Gt | St = k ∼ µk + σk · tν ,          ν = 5,                            (2)
where tν denotes a standard Student-t random variable with ν degrees of freedom. For observations
assigned to state k, we used the sample mean for µk and the sample standard deviation for σk , subject
to two fallback rules. A state with fewer than two observations used the mean and standard deviation
of that asset’s full training series. A state with at least two observations and sample standard deviation
below 10−12 yr−1 retained its state mean but used the full-series standard deviation as its scale.
These exceptions kept emission parameters defined but could broaden states containing repeated
returns (Online Appendix S12); neither applied to SPY, whose states all held many observations. The
same rules applied to the generators that the multi-asset comparison fitted to regression residuals.
A standard tν has variance ν/(ν − 2), so σk is a scale parameter rather     p than the emission standard
deviation. At ν = 5, each state’s emission standard deviation is σk 5/3, larger than the sample
standard deviation of the returns assigned to that state. The emission is not truncated to the state’s
interval, so a simulated return can fall outside it. We selected ν = 5 from a sensitivity sweep and
used ν = ∞, which gives Normal emissions, as a baseline; Table 1 gives the explored range. For
SPY, holding the rest of the model fixed, the Student-t emission raised simulated kurtosis toward the
observed value and raised the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) and Anderson–Darling (AD) pass rates
relative to the Normal emission (Table S2). A pass rate is the percentage of simulated paths whose
two-sample test against the observed returns gave p > 0.05.

dividing each row by its total count. A state with no observed outgoing transition used a uniform row,
Tkj = 1/N for every destination state j. Because the training state sequence is known, these row
frequencies are the maximum-likelihood estimates, and counting avoided the initialization sensitivity
of iterative expectation-maximization

geometrically at a rate set by the counted matrix, much faster than the slow decay in Fig. 1d. The

We added jump episodes to extend visits to extreme-return states and call the resulting generator
the hidden Markov model with jumps (HMM-WJ). When no jump episode was active, a jump was
triggered with probability ϵ at each step, and its duration was drawn as K ∼ Poisson(λ), where λ
is the mean episode length [40]. A draw of K = 0 produced no episode, and the chain made an
ordinary transition. For K > 0, the episode consisted of K forced tail steps, beginning with the step
that triggered it. At each forced step, the model replaced the ordinary transition by first choosing
one of two tail sets and then drawing a state uniformly from that set. The set S− = {1, . . . , Ntail }
contained the most negative states, and S+ = {N − Ntail + 1, . . . , N } contained the most positive
states. The model chose the negative set with probability pneg and the positive set otherwise. Because
pneg is slightly above one half, negative extreme returns occurred slightly more often than positive
ones. The choice was repeated at each forced step, so one episode can contain returns of both signs.
Table 1 lists ϵ, λ, pneg , and Ntail ; we tuned ϵ and λ and fixed pneg and Ntail . Jump-diffusion models
use Poisson arrivals to add instantaneous price jumps [19, 20]. Our model instead uses the Poisson
distribution for the episode length, and an episode is a sustained visit to the tail states rather than a
single price move. Setting ϵ = 0 removes the mechanism; we call this variant the hidden Markov
model with no jumps (HMM-NJ). Two closed-form approximations describe how much of a path,
and how many paths, the mechanism affects (Online Appendix S4). Forced steps occupy a long-run
fraction of approximately ϵλ/(1 + ϵλ) of all steps. A path of T steps contains at least one episode
with probability approximately 1 − e−ϵT , so ϵ sets how many paths contain an episode, while λ
sets how long an episode lasts. The jump-augmented process is Markov only in the pair formed by
the state and the number of remaining forced steps. The solved π̄ is therefore the exact stationary
distribution of the ordinary chain but only an approximate stationary start for the jump-augmented
process. The full simulation procedure appears in Algorithm 1. Each step followed T or continued a
jump episode and then drew a return from the selected state’s emission distribution.
We tuned the jump probability ϵ and mean duration λ on the volume-weighted SPY training series to
match its absolute-return autocorrelation and excess kurtosis [6, 7, 10]; the multi-asset comparisons
either disabled jumps or transferred the SPY values to the closing-price generators (Table 1). A
path with no episode follows HMM-NJ, so its autocorrelation and kurtosis errors do not depend
on (ϵ, λ). We scored only jump-active paths, those containing at least one episode [40], so the
objective measures how well an episode reproduces the observed statistics, not how often episodes
occur. At each point of a grid of (ϵ, λ) values, we simulated 200 independent paths of 2,766 trading
days. We minimized an objective that averaged, over the jump-active paths, the squared errors in the
autocorrelation function (ACF) of absolute returns through lag L plus a weighted squared error in
excess kurtosis:

3.2   Variance-corrected multi-asset composition

The single-asset model generated each ticker separately and did not specify cross-asset dependence.
For the multi-asset model, we added a shared market path as a common factor, using the single-index
model (SIM) of Sharpe [3]. We write gi (t) for the growth rate Gi,t of asset i on days t = 1, . . . , T .
The horizon T is 2,766 days in the training comparisons and 249 days in the holdout. The same
symbols denote observed paths when we estimate parameters and synthetic paths when we compose
them:
                       gi (t) = αi + βi gm (t) + εi (t),          t = 1, . . . , T.                   (4)
Here αi is the intercept for asset i, gm is the growth-rate path of the market, SPY, βi is the asset’s
market loading, and εi is its asset-specific residual path. We use εi for the residual path and ϵ for the
jump probability in the single-asset model. We estimated the intercept and market loading (αi , βi )
from observed data by ordinary least squares (OLS). The choice of εi determined the composition
method. A standard SIM draws independent Gaussian residuals with the variance estimated by
OLS. These Gaussian residuals preserve the fitted market relationship but lack the heavy tails, and
with jumps enabled the volatility clustering, that the single-asset generator produces. We called the
Gaussian-residual method Gaussian SIM and used it as one of the compared methods.

Complete composition rule. The complete rule has three outcomes, recorded by the branch flag
bi in Algorithm 2. Index trackers used the R2 -preserving branch; all other assets used the variance-

Everything else is computed per draw: because σgen,i is recomputed for each draw, so are si , ρi ,
and βieff , and one asset can be clipped in some draws and not in others. Because the draws are
independent of the market path, OLS estimates from composed paths converge, as the horizon grows,
to the intercept αi and the effective loading βieff , which equals βi unless the draw is clipped. The
variance target and the loading recovery are exact only when the sample covariance between the
draw and the market path is zero; on a finite path, they deviate by that covariance term (Online
Appendix S8). Because centering and scaling fix the residual sum, every branch imposes a zero
residual sum over the horizon T , which is stronger than a zero population mean. For a fixed market
path and effective loading, the terminal cumulative log return therefore has no asset-specific residual
uncertainty, although daily and intermediate returns still vary. In the clipped case, the effective
loading depends on the variances of each draw, so it can vary between draws and produce terminal
variation through the market term. Because centering and scaling use statistics of the entire draw,
the construction also depends on the chosen horizon. We therefore evaluated it as a constrained
finite-horizon construction for daily distributional fit and recovery of the market loading; it does not
preserve cumulative residual uncertainty.

Evaluation protocol. To test variance correction against alternative residual models, we compared
six composition methods using a dataset of 424 United States equities and exchange-traded funds
from Polygon.io (Table 3). Each ticker had 2,767 daily closing prices from January 2014 through
December 2024, yielding 2,766 growth rates; this complete-case selection conditioned the analysis on
survival and data coverage. We fitted the same hidden Markov generator to each asset, including SPY
                                                                                             2
(Table 1; rf = 0), and regressed the other 423 assets on observed SPY to estimate αi , βi , Ri,real , and
residual variance. All generators had jumps disabled to isolate composition from jump calibration.
The naive and hybrid methods shared a centered full-return draw, with variance correction applied
only by the hybrid method (Algorithm 2). Four alternatives generated residuals from a Gaussian
distribution, a residual-fit hidden Markov model (labeled JumpHMM-on-residuals), a stationary block
bootstrap, or a GARCH(1,1)-t model, and added them to αi + βi gm without centering or rescaling.
Comparator fitting and eligibility rules are given in Online Appendix S3.1; 30 non-stationary GARCH
fits left 393 eligible assets for that method. Using observed SPY as the common market path, we ran
100 replications per asset with seed 1234 and pooled training scores across tickers. We measured
factor recovery by the absolute OLS errors |α̂ − αi |, |β̂ − βi |, and |R̂2 − Ri,real
                                                                                 2
                                                                                      |, and naive and
hybrid variance preservation by the composed-to-generator variance ratio. Distributional scores were
KS and AD pass rates, Wasserstein-1 distance, excess kurtosis, and the Hill index of absolute returns
(Online Appendix S3). Because only naive and hybrid forced the residual mean to zero, a paired
centering control on the common 393 assets separated this constraint from model fitting (Table S5;
Online Appendix S10).
To test performance without refitting, we froze all 2014–2024 fits and evaluated 100 simulated
paths per asset against the 249 growth rates observed in 2025 (Table 4). Complete holdout histories
were available for 416 assets, including 386 with accepted GARCH fits. Each replication used one
simulated 249-day no-jump SPY path shared across asset compositions. The correction used the
observed training market variance in Eqs. (8)–(12), while each asset’s generator variance remained
draw-specific. Consequently, variation in simulated market variance could move composed variance
away from the generator target even at zero sample cross-covariance. Observed 2025 returns were
used only for scoring. We repeated the KS, AD, Wasserstein-1, and kurtosis evaluations and measured
variance for all six methods relative to observed 2025 variance. Because goodness-of-fit tests have
less power on short samples, we also compared the same simulated paths with randomly selected



                                                          10
contiguous 249-day blocks of observed training returns as a length-matched training reference. We
averaged replications within ticker before summarizing across tickers, giving each asset equal weight.
To estimate one-day Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds without relying on a single short path, we pooled
daily log returns, ∆t times the growth rates, from 5,000 separately composed 249-day paths per
asset and method (Table 5). Each replication shared one simulated SPY path across assets and
methods and followed the holdout centering and scaling conventions. We fixed the 95% and 99%
coverage thresholds before scoring observed 2025 returns; no holdout data entered fitting or threshold
calibration. These unconditional thresholds describe a uniformly selected day within the 249-day
construction, retain its horizon dependence, and do not remove the zero-sum residual constraint of
naive and hybrid paths. We reported mean exceedance rates and cross-ticker standard deviations
descriptively, without treating dependent days or assets sharing a market as independent replications.
Pool-size convergence and an independent simulation check are reported in Online Appendix S11.

Jump-enabled multi-asset comparison. To test whether the temporal benefit of the single-asset
jump mechanism survived composition, we compared naive and hybrid composition under three
jump settings (Table 6). Unlike the six-method comparison, which used observed SPY in training,
this comparison simulated the SPY market path in both the 2,766-day training window and the
249-day holdout, so a jump setting could apply to the market generator. Jumps were disabled, enabled
only in the SPY market generator, or enabled in both SPY and every asset generator. We froze the
closing-price hidden Markov generators and SIM calibrations of the six-method comparison. Enabled
models received the SPY settings of Table 1, (ϵ, λ) = (10−4 , 90), pneg = 0.52, and Ntail = 5,
without per-asset tuning. These settings were tuned on volume-weighted SPY prices and applied here
to generators fitted to closing prices. At these settings, most paths contain no episode, especially over
the 249-day holdout, so the jump effects are carried by a minority of paths. In both windows and
for all three jump settings, the correction used each simulated market path’s variance and each asset
draw’s variance. This differs from the six-method holdout, which fixed the market variance at its
training value.
For each jump setting, naive and hybrid composition received the same asset and market draws, and
one market path was shared across all assets in each replication. We generated 250 paths for each
of four seeds (1234, 2345, 3456, and 4567), giving 1,000 replications per asset, setting, and method.
We used a path-level version of ACF-MAE, computed on each path and then averaged across paths
(Eq. (S22) in Online Appendix S3); the single-asset ACF-MAE instead averages the path ACFs
before measuring the error. We used lags 1–25, the lag window of the tuning objective, with lags
1–60 as a secondary check, and repeated the marginal and variance diagnostics. The temporal scores
of the single-asset and jump comparisons therefore have different estimands and cannot be compared
directly. We included all paths, not only jump-active ones, in the summaries; jump-active subsets only
described the mechanism (Online Appendix S9). For Monte Carlo uncertainty, we averaged each
metric across tickers within each replication, which kept assets together under their shared market
path. Jump settings were paired by replication, sharing the draws of any component whose jump
setting did not change, and the approximate 95% confidence intervals were the mean of the 1,000
replication-level differences plus or minus 1.96 standard errors (Table S4). These intervals describe
simulation uncertainty conditional on the fitted models and observed histories, not uncertainty across
market regimes.

4     Results
4.1   Single-asset generator validation

The SPY returns had heavy tails and persistent absolute-return autocorrelation in both the training
and holdout windows, providing two targets for generator validation (Fig. 1; Online Appendix S4).
Individual raw-return correlations were small, although the Ljung–Box test detected joint serial
dependence. We compared HMM-WJ with seven alternatives on the training window, using 1,000
paths of 2,766 trading days (Table 2; Fig. 3). The alternatives were independent bootstrap, Gaussian,
and Laplace draws; HMM-NJ; GARCH(1,1) with Gaussian innovations; the GRU benchmark [41];
and the HSMM baseline adapted from Bulla and Bulla [11]. Both HMM variants had KS pass rates
within about one percentage point of the fitted Laplace baseline but came much closer to the observed
excess kurtosis, which Laplace underestimated at 3.0 against 7.7. Both also had lower Wasserstein-1
distances than Laplace, whereas Laplace had the lowest Hellinger distance among the fitted generators.


                                                   11
Bootstrap resampling passed every KS test while reusing the observed returns, and Gaussian sampling
passed neither distribution test. The GARCH model had the lowest mean absolute error between the
observed and ensemble-average absolute-return autocorrelation functions (ACF-MAE) but rarely
passed the distribution tests. The GRU also had lower ACF-MAE than the HMM variants but rarely
passed the distribution tests. The HSMM passed 82% of KS tests, underestimated excess kurtosis by
more than one third, and had the same ACF-MAE as HMM-NJ despite its flexible state durations. A
controlled emission ablation confirmed that Student-t emissions brought HMM-WJ excess kurtosis
closer to the observed value than Normal emissions (Table S2). The HMM variants therefore captured
heavy tails well, but no fitted generator led on every metric.
Jump episodes improved temporal fit at a small cost in marginal fit in training (Table 2; Fig. 3b).
Under the counted transition matrix, every state had an expected residence time of 1–2 steps, and
absolute-return autocorrelation decayed to near zero by lag 5 (Online Appendix S4.4). Calibration
selected mean episode duration λ = 90 and jump probability ϵ = 10−4 (Table 1). About 24%
of training-length HMM-WJ paths contained an episode; paths without one were equivalent to
HMM-NJ (Online Appendix S4.5). Across all paths, ACF-MAE fell from 0.059 for HMM-NJ to
0.053 for HMM-WJ, with small reductions in KS and AD pass rates. The episode frequency and
jump-active autocorrelation agreed with the generator calculations (Table S1; Online Appendix S4.6).
The calibration objective scored only jump-active paths, so it weakly identified ϵ and selected the
smallest grid value; the roughly one-quarter episode share followed from that value and the path
length, rather than being a calibration target. Likewise, λ was a fitted duration, not an estimate of
observed high-volatility spell lengths. Supporting sweeps showed that this tradeoff depended on
both the jump-active share and the state count: increasing either improved autocorrelation fit but
reduced distribution-test pass rates (Online Appendix S4.6; Online Appendix S6). The primary
choice N = 100 balanced the training metrics, but the state-count sweep used SPY alone and did not
establish a suitable resolution for other assets or shorter histories (Online Appendix S6).
The frozen HMM variants retained high distribution-test pass rates on the 249 held-out SPY returns
from 2025, although the short sample limited discrimination among generators (Table 2; Online
Appendix S4.5). We compared those returns with 249-day simulated paths. The KS pass rates were
96.6% for HMM-NJ and 95.4% for HMM-WJ, but weaker generators also passed more often than in
the longer training comparison. Few paths contained an episode at ϵ = 10−4 , so the holdout mainly
tested the no-jump dynamics shared by the two variants (Online Appendix S4.5). The fitted Laplace
baseline matched or exceeded both on KS and AD pass rates and had equal or smaller Wasserstein-1
and Hellinger distances. Its excess kurtosis of 2.8, however, remained well below the observed 6.9,
whereas both HMM variants reached 6.2. Repeating HMM-WJ fitting and calibration for NVDA,
JNJ, and JPM gave training KS pass rates near 99% for all three, but holdout rates ranged from 74.0%
for JNJ to 97.7% for NVDA (Online Appendix S7). The single-asset evidence supported heavy-tail
representation, but did not establish uniform holdout fit across assets or metrics. The six-method
composition comparison used the no-jump variant to avoid per-ticker jump calibration, and a separate
multi-asset comparison tested the transfer of SPY’s jump settings.

4.2   Variance-corrected multi-asset composition

We tested whether hybrid composition preserved generator variance while recovering the calibrated
market loading across the 423 non-market assets of the training universe (Table 3; Fig. 4). The
six-method comparison included GARCH(1,1)-t on the 393 assets with eligible fits. With observed
training SPY as the market path, the index trackers QQQ and SPYG used the R2 -preserving branch,
and the other 421 assets, which we call non-tracker assets, used the variance-preserving branch
without clipping (Online Appendix S8). The two trackers targeted the calibrated R2 rather than the
generator variance, so we marked them separately (Fig. 4). We paired each composed path with its
uncomposed generator draw to measure the ratio of composed to generator variance directly. Across
the non-tracker assets, the median of the per-ticker median ratios was 1.3171 for naive composition
and 1.0001 for hybrid composition, and the hybrid ticker medians ranged from 0.9933 to 1.0072
(Fig. 4b). Individual hybrid paths still varied around the target, because the correction assumes zero
covariance between the scaled asset draw and the market path, whereas a finite path has a small
nonzero sample covariance. Across non-tracker hybrid paths, the median absolute departure of the
variance ratio from one was 1.12%, and its 95th percentile was 3.41% (Fig. 4b). The correlation
between generator draws and the observed market path averaged near zero for every asset (Online
Appendix S1), which supported the zero-covariance assumption on average but not on each path.


                                                 12
The methods differed more in distributional fit than in factor recovery (Table 3; Fig. S1). All six
methods had median absolute β errors between 0.017 and 0.022. Naive and hybrid composition had
a median absolute intercept error of 0.002 yr−1 , an order of magnitude below the other methods,
because their centered residual paths reproduced the observed training asset means exactly when
combined with observed SPY without clipping. Refitting OLS left an intercept error equal to the
loading error times the market mean. The small intercept errors thus followed from centering, not
from predicting asset means. Gaussian SIM had the lowest median R2 error but almost never passed
the distribution tests, and its median excess kurtosis was an order of magnitude below the observed
median of about 13. Hybrid composition passed 73.6% of KS comparisons, against 7.1% for naive
composition, and its advantage held across the calibrated β range. Its own pass rate nonetheless
fell from 87.6% in the lowest β quartile to 65.9% in the highest (Fig. 4a; Table S3). A larger
market variance share may help explain this trend by diluting the asset generator’s shape (Online
Appendix S8.1). Hybrid composition retained heavy tails, with median excess kurtosis of 7.2 and a
median Hill estimate of 0.37, reported as the reciprocal tail index so that larger values indicate heavier
tails (Fig. S1). The excess-kurtosis shortfall was also present under naive composition, whose median
was similar. Hybrid composition’s median R2 error fell between those of the naive and Gaussian
methods, because its variance-preserving branch targeted generator variance rather than observed R2 .
Direct residual fitting was competitive with reuse, and controlling for residual centering favored the
residual-fit HMM and block bootstrap (Tables 3 and S5). Under each method’s native mean treatment,
JumpHMM-on-residuals passed 75.8% of KS comparisons, slightly above hybrid composition;
block bootstrap passed 73.3% and GARCH(1,1)-t passed 67.4%. The residual-fit HMM and block
bootstrap had slightly lower Wasserstein distances than hybrid composition. The residual-fit HMM
also closely matched hybrid composition in excess kurtosis and Hill estimate, whereas block bootstrap
and GARCH produced lighter tails. Only naive and hybrid composition centered their residual
draws, so we repeated the comparison after centering all six methods on the common 393-asset set
(Table S5). The hybrid KS pass rate stayed at 74.4% because its paths were already centered; the
JumpHMM-on-residuals rate rose from 75.8% to 78.4%, and block bootstrap rose from 73.5% to
76.7%. Both residual methods also had higher AD pass rates and lower Wasserstein distances than
hybrid composition after centering. The control brought every method’s median intercept error to
0.002 yr−1 while leaving variance, recovered loading, R2 , and excess kurtosis unchanged. A separate
training diagnostic found that emission fallback rules also disadvantaged the full-return methods
on 39 assets but affected none of the residual-fit models (Table S6; Online Appendix S12). Using
empirical scales in their populated zero-return states implied an aggregate hybrid KS gain of about
one percentage point, less than the gap to JumpHMM-on-residuals. The main comparisons retained
the original rule because this alternative had no holdout validation (Online Appendix S12). Together,
these checks supported variance correction over naive reuse without a second fit, but did not establish
an advantage over direct residual fitting.

4.3   Holdout performance and VaR coverage

To test out-of-sample generalization without refitting or look-ahead bias, we froze the fitted models
and SIM parameters before evaluating them against 416 complete asset histories from 2025 (Table 4).
Hybrid composition reached an 87.0% mean cross-ticker KS pass rate, against 83.5% for naive
composition. Its median ratio of synthetic to observed 2025 variance was 0.97, whereas naive
composition inflated that ratio to 1.29. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method reached an 86.2% KS
pass rate and a slightly lower median W1 than hybrid composition. As in training, these holdout
comparisons used each method’s native mean treatment, and no common-centering control was run
on the holdout. Higher holdout pass rates did not by themselves establish better generation: all
methods passed more often than in training, and the gap between hybrid and naive composition
narrowed (Tables 3 and 4). For every method, pass rates against matched-length 249-day training
blocks lay between the full-length training and 2025 rates, consistent with lower test power in shorter
samples contributing to the rise. Those blocks also used simulated rather than observed SPY, so the
comparison did not isolate sample length or fully explain the 2025 increase. Nor did improvement
hold for every asset: about three quarters of hybrid tickers passed more often in 2025 than against
their matched training blocks and one quarter passed less often, and every method retained a lower
tail of weakly fitted tickers (Fig. 5).
To test whether the holdout improvement over naive composition extended to reliable tail thresholds,
we estimated per-ticker one-day left-tail Value-at-Risk (VaR) thresholds from 5,000 separate 249-day


                                                   13
paths and counted exceedances on the observed 2025 histories (Table 5). The coverage level α = 0.95
has a nominal exceedance rate of 1 − α = 5% and gave each asset about 12 expected exceedances. At
that level, hybrid composition had a mean exceedance rate of 5.48%, closer to nominal than the 4.04%
of naive composition. At α = 0.99, the hybrid rate of 1.12% and the naive rate of 0.88% lay equally
close to the 1% nominal rate on opposite sides. The JumpHMM-on-residuals method had rates of
5.62% and 1.16%, close to hybrid composition, and Gaussian SIM had the largest 99% rate at 1.72%.
Doubling the threshold pool from 2,500 to 5,000 paths changed every method’s mean exceedance rate
by less than 0.006 percentage points at either coverage level (Online Appendix S11). The advantage
of hybrid over naive composition thus appeared at the 95% level but not at the 99% level. Neither
comparison established a ranking, because each asset contributed few exceedances, only about 2–3
expected at the 99% level, and all assets shared one market period, so their exceedances were not
independent. The hybrid mean exceedance rates were above nominal at both coverage levels, and this
descriptive check could not confirm threshold calibration. The cross-asset standard deviations of the
exceedance rates described how realized rates varied among assets; they did not test miscalibration or
validate coverage in other periods.

4.4   Jump-enabled multi-asset composition

Transferring SPY’s jump settings improved temporal fit in training but worsened it in the 2025
holdout (Table 6, where hybrid composition is labeled Corrected). We compared jumps disabled,
market-only jumps, and market-plus-asset jumps under naive and hybrid composition, without per-
asset tuning. This experiment simulated the market in both windows and used each market path’s
variance in the correction, so its baseline differed from the six-method comparisons. Its ACF-MAE
was calculated for each path and then averaged, unlike the single-asset error calculated from the
ensemble-average autocorrelation. Under hybrid composition, market-only jumps reduced the training
25-lag ACF-MAE by 5.3%; the 95% Monte Carlo interval excluded zero. The KS pass rate fell by
0.3 percentage points, with an interval that included zero (Table S4). Adding asset jumps reduced
ACF-MAE by 14.6% relative to no jumps but lowered the KS pass rate from 73.9% to 61.7%, a much
larger marginal-fit cost than for SPY, whose settings were tuned. In the holdout, market-only and
market-plus-asset jumps instead raised the 25-lag ACF-MAE by 2.7% and 6.9%. Both 95% Monte
Carlo intervals excluded zero, and the 60-lag errors also rose (Tables 6 and S4). Holdout KS pass
rates changed little with market-only jumps; with both market and asset jumps, they fell by about
1.5 percentage points relative to no jumps. Across all settings and both windows, the mean ratio
of composed to generator variance under hybrid composition remained within 0.05% of one. This
aggregate included the trackers, whose branch targeted R2 rather than generator variance, and did not
establish preservation for every asset. Only 2.2% of simulated one-year market paths contained an
episode (Online Appendix S9), limiting evidence about market jumps; the single observed holdout
year also could not establish performance in other market periods.

4.5   Sensitivity analyses
                                                     2
We examined how the branch-selection threshold Rpreserve     assigned assets to branches in the observed
universe and whether any value of the variance floor f triggered clipping (Online Appendix S8). The
R2 -preserving branch applied only to QQQ and SPYG, whose hybrid compositions passed every KS
test in training. The highest-R2 asset outside these trackers, the individual stock BLK, fell about 0.16
below the 0.80 threshold, so the assignment did not depend on a narrow margin. Across the explored
thresholds from 0.70 to 0.90 at the default floor, only the QQQ assignment changed, moving to the
variance-preserving branch at 0.90. At the default threshold, no value of f from 0.05 to 0.30 caused
any asset to be clipped. The empirical evaluation of the R2 -preserving branch was therefore limited
to two assets, and the observed market variance never activated clipping.
A synthetic Gaussian check supplemented the two real assets using the R2 -preserving branch: across
the tested grid, median recovered loadings and R2 differed from their supplied targets by at most
0.0011 and 0.0017, respectively (Online Appendix S8). Each reference series combined the observed
training SPY path with an independent Gaussian residual whose variance gave the target R2 under
Eq. (11). The composer received the true loading and R2 , a second independent Gaussian draw,
and the target residual variance, which made the residual scale in Eq. (12) exactly one. Every
composed series passed the KS comparison with its reference, but their shared SPY path made the
samples dependent, so the pass rates were descriptive rather than calibrated tests. The grid checked


                                                  14
implementation and finite-sample accuracy under this restricted construction; it did not exercise
full-return HMM fitting, non-unit residual scaling, or simulated market paths.
Increasing market volatility activated clipping for most assets, although no path clipped under the
observed market variance (Online Appendix S8). A larger market variance raises the market loading
ratio ρi , the market’s share of the target variance; on the variance-preserving branch, clipping begins
when ρi > 1 − f . To measure when clipping activates, we multiplied deviations of the observed
training SPY path from its mean by γ ∈ {1, 2, 3}, which preserved the market mean and multiplied
its standard deviation by γ. We then recomposed all 423 assets with the scaled path’s variance
in the correction, holding the fitted marginals, intercepts, and calibrated loadings fixed (Online
Appendix S8). Clipping applied to no composed path at γ = 1 and to 76.4% and 95.2% of composed
paths at γ = 2 and 3. Because clipped paths retained their fitted intercepts while the market mean
stayed unchanged and nonzero, their means shifted by (βieff − βi )g m by construction. The stress test
therefore characterized when clipping activated and how it moved asset means; it did not evaluate fit
under the scaled markets.

5   Discussion
Synthetic generators of equity returns should reproduce three stylized facts: heavy tails, weak
autocorrelation in raw returns, and volatility clustering (Fig. 1). We used heavy-tailed hidden Markov
generators with optional jump episodes to represent these features, then examined their reuse in a
multi-asset model with a shared market path. We developed a correction that centers and rescales
each generator’s draws to avoid counting market variance twice, without refitting to regression
residuals (Fig. 4; Table 3). Training and 2025 holdout comparisons tested distributional fit and
whether single-asset improvements in volatility clustering survived composition.
The correction’s practical value was avoiding a separate residual fit while improving variance and
distributional agreement over naive composition (Fig. 4; Table 3). The corrected method passed
73.6% of full-length training KS comparisons and retained heavy tails. On the 2025 holdout, it
raised the mean cross-ticker KS pass rate from 83.5% under centered naive composition to 87.0%
and brought the median ratio of synthetic to observed variance to 0.97 (Table 4). The improvement
therefore persisted beyond the fitting period. Direct residual fitting remained competitive: JumpHMM-
on-residuals had a slightly higher pass rate in training and a slightly lower rate in the holdout. When
all methods’ residual draws were centered over the same training horizon on the common asset set,
JumpHMM-on-residuals and block bootstrap both outperformed the corrected method on distribution-
test pass rates and Wasserstein distance (Table S5). Before centering was matched, the comparison
with residual-fit methods therefore partly reflected different constraints on simulated residual means.
For corrected composition, some assets had lower pass rates in the holdout than against matched-
length training blocks. Reuse thus offered a way to improve on naive composition without refitting,
but did not establish a distributional advantage over fitting the residuals themselves.
The jump comparison showed that improving the single-asset generator did not automatically improve
the composed paths (Tables 2 and 6). On SPY, the generator with jumps reduced absolute-return
autocorrelation error relative to its no-jump counterpart while retaining high distributional pass rates.
Other generators achieved lower autocorrelation error or higher pass rates, so temporal and marginal
fit still required separate assessment. With multi-asset composition, market-only jumps improved
training temporal fit with little change in the KS pass rate, whereas adding asset-level episodes
produced a larger temporal improvement at a substantial marginal-fit cost. Both settings increased
autocorrelation error in 2025, despite the mean composed-to-generator variance ratio remaining
near one (Table 6). Variance control therefore did not ensure that the transferred temporal behavior
matched the data. These tests transferred one SPY calibration from volume-weighted average prices
to closing-price models. The asset episodes also came from full-return models and were not identified
separately as idiosyncratic events. Separate calibrations for NVDA, JNJ, and JPM had strong in-
sample fit, but three assets did not establish a general calibration rule (Online Appendix S7). Per-asset
tuning or distinct shared and asset-specific episode models could change the tradeoff, but neither was
tested in the composition comparison.
Preserving total variance did not preserve the complete return distribution, and corrected fit varied
with market exposure (Fig. 4; Table S3). Corrected KS pass rates fell from 87.6% in the lowest
β quartile to 65.9% in the highest, consistent with a larger market contribution diluting the asset


                                                  15
generator’s shape. The correction accounted for variance supplied by the market, but combining
two independent return distributions still changed the shape of the composed returns. Clipping,
the reduction of the market loading to retain some residual variance, was unnecessary at observed
market variance but activated for most assets when market volatility was doubled or tripled (Online
Appendix S8). That stress transformation preserved the market mean. Reducing the loading while
retaining the fitted intercept changed the asset mean by (βieff − βi )g m , where g m is the market mean.
The clipped branch therefore targeted total variance while retaining a minimum residual contribution,
at the cost of changing the calibrated loading and mean. The stress test did not establish realistic
return distributions under the scaled markets. Variance correction therefore required separate checks
of distributional shape and calibrated market exposure.
The correction supported daily-return comparisons, but broader simulation uses require additional
modeling and validation. The alternative correction for market trackers targeted their calibrated R2 ,
the fraction of return variance explained by the market. Only two observed assets tested it; a Gaussian
simulation checked its formula without fitting full-return generators (Online Appendix S8). Allowing
states whose training observations were identical to generate nearly constant returns improved training
fit (Table S6). For those states, the main comparisons retained emissions scaled by the asset’s full-
series spread, and the alternative still needs holdout validation. Improved marginal fit did not establish
reliable tail coverage: the corrected method and JumpHMM-on-residuals had pooled-threshold one-
day left-tail 99% VaR exceedance rates of 1.12% and 1.16%, against a nominal 1% (Table 5). More
simulated paths improved threshold precision without supplying more observed extreme events; the
249-day holdout was too short for a precise ranking and covered only one shared market period. Serial
dependence also limited the interpretation of KS and AD pass rates, whose tests assume independent
observations. Variance accounting also assumed independent asset and market draws; nonzero
covariance would require an additional cross-term. For portfolio applications, a shared market
path did not supply the sector and style dependence omitted by the evaluated independent residual
draws (Algorithm 2; Online Appendix S2). Omitting positive residual covariance can overstate the
diversification return from periodic rebalancing [42, 13, 43], including for residual-fit baselines that
draw residuals independently. A synchronized multivariate block bootstrap, residual factor model, or
copula could address that omission, but the resulting dependence needs direct evaluation. Per-path
centering imposed a separate limitation: each residual sum was zero at the chosen horizon, leaving no
residual uncertainty in terminal cumulative log returns conditional on the market path and effective
loading (Online Appendix S8). Portfolio and cumulative-risk applications therefore require both a
joint residual model and a construction that retains cumulative residual variation.

6   Conclusion
We developed a variance-corrected single-index method that centers and rescales full-return draws for
reuse without counting market variance twice or fitting separate residual generators (Fig. 4; Table 3).
The method retained heavy tails and improved distributional fit over centered naive composition
in training and the 2025 holdout (Table 4), but did not establish an advantage over direct residual
fitting. Transferring SPY’s jump settings improved temporal fit in training but worsened it in the
holdout (Table 6), showing that single-asset temporal gains did not transfer reliably. These findings
support reuse for daily-return comparisons, without establishing calibrated tail coverage (Table 5) or
validating broader risk applications. Independent residual draws omitted cross-asset dependence, and
per-path centering removed residual uncertainty from terminal cumulative log returns conditional
on the market path and the loading used in composition. Portfolio and cumulative-risk applications
therefore require a joint residual model and a construction that retains cumulative residual variation.

S3    Validation metric definitions
To distinguish marginal agreement from temporal agreement, we used separate distributional, tail,
and autocorrelation summaries (Tables 2 and 6). For one observed series x = (x1 , . . . , xn ) and R
synthetic paths y (r) of the same length, each distributional comparison used one complete synthetic
path and the fixed observed history. The two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic is the largest
absolute difference between their empirical cumulative distribution functions, DKS = supz |Fx (z) −
Fy(r) (z)| [44, 45]. The Anderson–Darling (AD) comparison gives greater weight to differences near
the tails [46]. We used the approximate two-sample KS test and the modified, tie-aware two-sample
AD calculation in HypothesisTests.jl, with asymptotic p-values. A path passed when p > 0.05,
and the pass percentage was 100 times the fraction of passing paths. Pass rates are descriptive because
returns are dependent and training comparisons reuse the fitting history. Independent simulations
supply independent indicators conditional on that history and fit, not new historical evidence or proof
of a shared distribution.

S3.1   Six-method composition evaluation

The four residual-based comparators used the observed asset’s ordinary least-squares (OLS) residuals
to set their distributions (Table 3). The Gaussian single-index model (SIM) used independent Gaussian
draws with the OLS residual variance. The residual-fit hidden Markov model, labeled JumpHMM-on-
residuals after the software package, fitted a no-jump generator to a synthetic price path obtained by
exponentiating cumulative residuals multiplied by the daily time step. The Politis–Romano stationary
bootstrap [47] used a mean block length of 50 days in both training and holdout. The generalized
autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity model, GARCH(1,1)-t, fitted conditional variance to the
residual series [15]. We accepted a fit only if optimization converged, the likelihood and coefficients
were finite, the variance intercept was positive, and the nonnegative ARCH and GARCH coefficients
summed to less than one. The Student-t degrees of freedom had to exceed two, the unconditional
variance had to be finite and positive, and a trial simulated path had to be finite and nonconstant. All
423 optimizations converged, but 30 failed the coefficient-sum condition, leaving 393 eligible assets.
All four residual comparators entered composition without further centering or rescaling. The scores
reflect branch-specific targets. Lower absolute OLS errors indicated closer recovery, but on the hybrid
variance-preserving branch the composed R2 approached the draw’s ρi , capped at 1 − f , rather than
  2
Ri,real . Its R2 error therefore mainly measured this designed shift; loading error also included any
change made by clipping. A composed-to-generator variance ratio of one indicated equal variances,
which the R2 -preserving branch did not target. We omitted cross-sectional rank reordering to test
each ticker’s marginal construction and scored volatility clustering separately in the jump-enabled
comparison. The paired centering control isolated differences in residual-mean constraints (Online
Appendix S10).

amplitude and variance normalization depend on λ; the kurtosis shift uses the same forced fraction.

weakly identifies ϵ: the tuned ϵ = 10−4 lies at the lower edge of the explored range for SPY and
for all three cross-asset tickers, and the tuned optima vary with the Monte Carlo sample (Online
Appendix S7).

S6    State-resolution sensitivity
To test sensitivity to state resolution, we refitted the hidden Markov models with and with-
out jumps (HMM-WJ and HMM-NJ) to the 2,766-day SPY training history using N ∈
{30, 60, 90, 100, 150, 200} states using 1,000 paths per fit, α = 0.05 and the ensemble-ACF con-
vention. Each refit kept five states in each tail set and repeated the (ϵ, λ) search. The no-jump
model’s Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) and Anderson–Darling (AD) pass rates changed by less than
one percentage point, and its absolute-return autocorrelation error (ACF-MAE) was unchanged at the
reported precision. For HMM-WJ, ACF-MAE fell from 0.057 at N = 30 to 0.045 at N = 200, while
the AD pass rate fell from 97.1% to 82.8%; intermediate values were not monotone. Increasing N
narrowed the fixed-size tail sets to more extreme quantile bands and left fewer observations for each
state’s emission estimates, while retuning also changed the jump parameters. The sweep therefore
measured their combined effect, without isolating the contribution of each change. Fallback rules
kept sparsely populated states defined (Online Appendix S12). The primary N = 100 was a practical
compromise; this SPY-only sweep did not establish an optimum for other assets or shorter histories.

S11     Pooled one-day tail thresholds
Pooled one-day tail thresholds used B = 5,000 separately constructed 249-day paths per asset and
method (Table 5). Linear empirical quantiles pooled all 249B daily log returns. Each replication
shared a market path across assets and methods; each asset path retained its centering and scaling
rules, with hybrid scaling using training market variance. Hidden Markov models started from their
fitted stationary laws with jumps disabled; GARCH retained its 100-step warmup. Thresholds were
calibrated for 423 training assets (393 GARCH fits) before reading holdout data. The estimand is
a uniformly selected day in this finite-horizon construction, not cumulative risk. Pools of 100, 500,
1,000, 2,500 and 5,000 paths tested numerical stability; 1,000 independent paths checked simulated
exceedances. Relative threshold changes divided the difference by the absolute 5,000-path threshold.
Doubling 2,500 to 5,000 changed mean observed exceedance rates by less than 0.006 percentage
points. Median absolute relative changes in 99% thresholds were 0.12–0.30% across methods,
with 95th percentiles 0.37–1.03% and a worst case of 3.95%. Independent simulated exceedances
were 5.03–5.04% and 1.01–1.02% against 5% and 1% targets. These checks establish simulation
stability, not real-market coverage. On the common 386 holdout assets, corrected, residual-fit HMM,
naive and Gaussian composition had mean 99% exceedance rates of 1.08%, 1.14%, 0.85% and
1.66%, respectively. Cross-asset standard deviations describe dispersion, not standard errors. Neither
eligibility control nor one shared market period establishes equivalence or precise method rankings.

Emission fallbacks replace within-state moments when a state is sparse or nearly constant. For
one asset, let nk be the number of observations in state k, let Ḡk and sk be its sample mean and
standard deviation when defined, and let Ḡ and sG be the corresponding full-series quantities. The
implemented emission rule was:

The scale σk multiplies a standard Student-t draw; it is not the emission standard deviation. State
intervals included their lower boundary and excluded their upper boundary. A transition row with
no observed outgoing transition was set to 1/N in every column. Such rows are unidentified by
observed transitions. Among the 424 full-return models, 39 had at least one fallback emission,
affecting 50 states. Of these, 39 states contained 11–119 identical observations each, seven were
empty, and four contained one observation. The seven empty states also supplied the seven uniform
transition rows. Fallback states carried 0.399–4.300% of stationary probability per affected asset.
Sparse-state probability was at most 0.0363% per asset; most fallback mass belonged to populated
states with repeated returns. P SPY required no fallback, and neither did any of the 423 residual-fit
models. Fallback mass is 100 k∈F π̄k , where F is the set of fallback states. The per-asset inventory
retained in the replication files uses nc for observations in a constant state and n0 , n1 for empty and
singleton state counts. Its diagnostic ∆V is the percentage change in stationary mixture variance
after replacing only populated constant-state scales by their empirical values; all fits used N = 100
and ν = 5.
To isolate the consequence of broadening populated constant states, we compared the fitted generators
with an empirical-scale alternative on all 39 affected non-market assets (Table S6). The alternative
replaced sG by sk only where nk ≥ 2 and sk < τ ; locations, partitions, transitions, initial probabili-
ties, and sparse-state fallbacks were unchanged. A zero empirical scale gives a point mass at the state
mean. We replayed the original 100 paths of 2,766 observations per asset, paired by state sequence
and random draws, with jumps disabled and the observed training SPY path shared by both treatments.
We scored the uncomposed generator and the naive and hybrid compositions; each composition used
its treatment’s generator variance. Pass fractions remain descriptive for these dependent series with
ties. The empirical-scale alternative improved marginal-fit scores on this training subset (Table S6).
Hybrid KS and AD pass rates rose by 10.28 and 14.08 percentage points, with Monte Carlo standard
errors of 0.49 and 0.60. For the uncomposed generators, the change in stationary mixture variance
ranged from −0.651% to −6.520% (Online Appendix S12). The fallback scale therefore affected
