
We simulated 1,000 paths of 2766 trading days using VWAP price data for both the standard model with no-jumps and the hybrid model with jumps. Applying the multi-objective grid search detailed in the Methods, the optimal jump hyperparameters were identified as ($\epsilon \approx 5 \times 10^{-5}$ and $\lambda \approx 67$). This configuration successfully reproduced the heavy-tailed kurtosis and ARCH effect observed in SPY (Figure \ref{fig:wj_90_combo}). Based on the statistical consistency results, the configuration with a hueristic of $90\leq{N}\leq{100}$ states was used in this study. Increasing the number of states $N$ increased the granularity of the market regimes, leading to a closer fit for in-sample results. However, there is a limit to this refinement; if $N$ was too large, such as 350 states, the empirical estimation protocol failed because the historical dataset became too sparse relative to the state space. In such cases, certain states could remain unvisited, resulting in zero transition probabilities that broke the mathematical integrity of the Markov chain. Conversely, a very small number of states, such as $N=10$, resulted in a coarse model that missed the heavy tails observed in the empirical data. Therefore, the selection of $N=90$ provided a balance between distributional fidelity and statistical robustness.

We performed Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) and Anderson-Darling (AD) tests to characterize the statistical consistency of the simulated excess growth rate distributions against the historical data. Both the KS and AD tests are non-parametric goodness-of-fit tests that compare the empirical cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of two datasets \cite{kolmogorov_1933, smirnov_1948, anderson_darling_1952}. The KS test focused on the maximum distance between the two CDFs, while the AD test placed more weight on the tails of the distributions.

In-sample KS and AD tests showed pass rates above 98\% for both model variants (Table \ref{tab:statistical_results}), indicating distributional alignment across the 10-years of training data.  The standard no-jumps model matched the marginal distribution but failed to capture volatility clustering (Figure \ref{fig:nj_90_combo}). The autocorrelation of absolute growth in subplot (c) remained flat, showing that state resolution alone could not overcome the memoryless limitations of standard transition logic. The hybrid model reproduced volatility clustering, with the absolute-growth autocorrelation mirroring the ARCH effect (Figure \ref{fig:wj_90_combo}). With $N=90$ states, this configuration provided a balance and aligned with volatility estimation techniques using HMM architectures \cite{rossi_volatility_2006}.

Out-of-sample volatility differed by approximately 32 percent, yet volatility clustering persisted in subplot (c) (Figure \ref{fig:wj_90_oos_combo}). Unlike the standard Markov transition matrix, which decayed rapidly, the Poisson jump mechanism mimicked the persistence of market volatility in unseen conditions. These results validated the hybrid framework as a tool for producing synthetic time series that remained statistically consistent with the underlying stochastic process \cite{cont_empirical_2001, ryden_stylized_1998, bulla_stylized_2006}.

The correlation-distance distribution was centered at a mean distance of 1.0, indicating temporal independence between simulated paths and the historical trajectory (Figure \ref{fig:oos_correlation_distance}). The correlation distance, defined as $(1-\rho)$, measured the temporal alignment between simulated paths and the observed historical trajectory. This result confirmed that the generative framework produced independent stochastic realizations that captured the stylized facts of the market without violating the efficient markets hypothesis by attempting to predict specific events \cite{fama_efficient_1970}. This underscored the distinction between a generative framework intended for stress testing and a forecasting tool intended for point prediction \cite{assefa_generating_2021, kwon_can_2024}.
