
We computed descriptive statistics and stylized-facts tests for the SPY daily excess growth rate series over the in-sample (2014--2024, $T=2{,}766$) and out-of-sample (2025, $T=249$) windows. We observed excess kurtosis of 7.7 (IS) and 6.9 (OoS), rejection of the Jarque-Bera normality test ($p<0.001$) in both windows, and rejection of the Ljung-Box test on $|G_t|$ ($p<0.001$) in both windows, which confirms that the three canonical stylized facts (leptokurtosis, negligible raw-return autocorrelation, and persistent volatility clustering) are present in the target data and must be reproduced by any faithful generative model (Table~\ref{tab:descriptive_stats}).

We applied the multi-objective grid search to identify the optimal jump hyperparameters over the search grid $\epsilon\in[10^{-4},\,2.5\times10^{-2}]$ and $\lambda\in[10,\,160]$, evaluating 200 simulated paths per grid point. We found a well-defined interior minimum at $\epsilon^{*}=10^{-4}$ and $\lambda^{*}=100$, which indicates that the jump hyperparameters are identifiable from data and that the objective landscape is not degenerate (Figure~\ref{fig:parameter_sweep}, panel~a). We compared the ACF of $|G_t|$ at the optimal parameters against the observed SPY ACF and observed close agreement across lags 1--252, which confirms that the optimized jump mechanism reproduces the empirical ARCH effect (Figure~\ref{fig:parameter_sweep}, panel~b).

We selected a state resolution of $N=100$ for the primary analysis and examined the sensitivity of all quality metrics to $N\in\{30,60,90,100,150,200\}$. We observed that KS and AD pass rates remain stable across this range for both HMM-NJ ($\geq$98.9\% KS, $\geq$97.9\% AD) and HMM-WJ ($\geq$96.0\% KS, $\geq$88.6\% AD), and that ACF-MAE for HMM-WJ improves slightly from 0.057 at $N=30$ to 0.049 at $N=200$, which suggests that the framework is robust to the choice of state resolution over a wide range (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity_results}). We verified via state-occupancy diagnostics that all states at $N\leq200$ contain at least 7 observations, which confirms that the Laplace equal-probability partition maintains adequate statistical support per state. At $N=350$ certain states are never visited in the historical record, leaving zero transition probabilities that break the ergodic structure of the chain, which establishes the practical upper bound on state resolution.

We simulated 1,000 paths of 2,766 trading days from each of three models (GARCH(1,1), HMM-NJ, and HMM-WJ) and applied two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Anderson-Darling tests at $\alpha=0.05$ to assess distributional consistency with the historical data. We found that GARCH(1,1) achieved IS KS and AD pass rates of only 4.6\% (SE~0.7) and 1.1\% (SE~0.3), which indicates that the GARCH marginal distribution is systematically inconsistent with the observed data despite its well-known volatility clustering properties (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). We found that HMM-NJ achieved IS pass rates of 99.4\% (SE~0.2) and 98.5\% (SE~0.4), and HMM-WJ achieved 97.1\% (SE~0.5) and 90.4\% (SE~0.9), which confirms that the Laplace-mixture emission structure adequately captures the observed heavy-tailed distribution (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}).

We computed the ACF of $|G_t|$ over lags 1--252 for each model and compared it against the empirical ACF. We observed IS ACF-MAE values of 0.031 for GARCH, 0.059 for HMM-NJ, and 0.052 for HMM-WJ. We found that GARCH achieves the lowest ACF-MAE because its variance equation is explicitly designed to match autocorrelation structure, yet this comes at the cost of distributional fidelity, a tradeoff that the quality metrics expose directly (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). We observed that HMM-NJ produces an ACF of $|G_t|$ that decays rapidly toward zero rather than exhibiting the empirically observed slow decay, which confirms that the standard transition matrix alone is structurally incapable of generating persistent volatility clustering (Figure~\ref{fig:model_comparison}, panel~b). We observed that HMM-WJ generates a mixture of two path families: approximately 76\% of IS paths contain no Poisson jump and behave identically to HMM-NJ, while the remaining $\sim$24\% contain at least one jump event and sustain substantial $\text{ACF}(|G_t|)$ decay across all lags, which demonstrates that the jump-duration mechanism is the structural feature responsible for reproducing the ARCH effect (Figure~\ref{fig:model_comparison}, panel~b).

We evaluated out-of-sample performance over 249 trading days (January~2--December~31, 2025) using the same 1,000-path ensemble. We found that GARCH achieved OoS KS and AD pass rates of 79.0\% (SE~1.3) and 71.2\% (SE~1.4), while HMM-NJ achieved 96.7\% (SE~0.6) and 96.3\% (SE~0.6), and HMM-WJ achieved 95.0\% (SE~0.7) and 95.4\% (SE~0.7), which shows that the HMM variants generalise robustly while GARCH degrades substantially (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). We observed that the simulated kurtosis for GARCH collapsed to 1.7 out-of-sample against an observed value of 6.9, while the HMM variants maintained simulated kurtosis near 5.0--5.1, which suggests that GARCH does not preserve tail behaviour outside the training window (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). We observed that the distribution of KS $p$-values shifted leftward from in-sample to out-of-sample, which reflects the expected distributional degradation when stationary parameters are applied to a period with elevated macro uncertainty (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panels~a--b). We found that the observed ACF of $|G_t|$ in the 2025 test window exceeded the HMM-WJ simulation band at medium-to-long lags, which indicates that the IS-calibrated jump hyperparameters $(\epsilon^{*},\lambda^{*})$ underestimate the frequency and persistence of volatility episodes in this period and that the jump parameters are regime-dependent (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panel~d). Despite this ACF gap, the observed marginal density remained within the simulation envelope, which confirms adequate distributional coverage even under regime shift (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panel~c).

We propagated HMM-WJ SPY paths through a Single-Index Model for 424 S\&P~500 constituents, decomposing each asset's excess growth rate as $\hat{G}_{i,t}=\hat{\alpha}_i+\hat{\beta}_i G_{{\rm SPY},t}+\hat{\eta}_{i,t}$, where $(\hat{\alpha}_i,\hat{\beta}_i)$ were estimated by ordinary least squares on the 2014--2024 training window and the idiosyncratic shocks $\hat{\eta}_{i,t}$ were drawn by resampling the empirical residuals. We observed that the SIM $R^2_i$ varied widely across GICS sectors, from near-zero for defensive and low-correlation names to above 0.8 for broad-market ETFs, which confirms that the single-factor structure captures only the systematic component of each asset's dynamics (Figure~\ref{fig:multi_asset}, panel~a; Table~\ref{tab:multi_asset}). We found IS KS pass rates with a median of 66.7\% and a mean of 58.4\% across the 424-asset universe, which are lower than the 97.1\% achieved for SPY alone and which reflect the well-known limitation that the SIM's single-factor linearity and symmetric residual distribution cannot fully capture asset-specific tail behaviour, skewness, or sector-level dynamics (Figure~\ref{fig:multi_asset}, panel~b; Table~\ref{tab:multi_asset}). We observed that the degradation in KS pass rate was concentrated among assets with low $R^2_i$ (those dominated by idiosyncratic risk that the single factor cannot reach), while high-$\beta$ assets closely tracking SPY retained pass rates above 80\%, which suggests that the HMM-WJ generative engine is not the bottleneck and that the limiting factor is the expressiveness of the factor model (Figure~\ref{fig:multi_asset}, panel~c). We found OoS KS pass rates with a mean of 82.1\% and a median of 91.8\% across 417 surviving assets, which shows that the SIM extension generalises to the out-of-sample window (Table~\ref{tab:multi_asset}).

We computed the correlation-distance distribution between the out-of-sample simulated paths and the historical trajectory. We observed a mean distance of $1-\rho\approx1.0$, which confirms that the generative framework produces temporally independent realisations that do not attempt to predict specific historical events, distinguishing it from a forecasting tool and positioning it as an instrument for stress testing and scenario generation (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}).
