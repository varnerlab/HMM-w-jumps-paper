
\subsection{Descriptive statistics and stylized facts}
Descriptive statistics for the SPY daily excess growth rate series confirm that the three canonical stylized facts are present in both the in-sample (2014--2024, $T=2{,}766$) and out-of-sample (2025, $T=249$) windows (Table~\ref{tab:descriptive_stats}). Excess kurtosis reaches 7.7 (IS) and 6.9 (OoS), the Jarque-Bera normality test is rejected ($p<0.001$) in both windows, and the Ljung-Box test on $|G_t|$ is likewise rejected ($p<0.001$), confirming leptokurtosis, negligible raw-return autocorrelation, and persistent volatility clustering. Any faithful generative model must reproduce all three properties simultaneously.

\subsection{Jump hyperparameter optimization and state resolution}
A multi-objective grid search over $\epsilon\in[10^{-4},\,2.5\times10^{-2}]$ and $\lambda\in[10,\,160]$, with 200 simulated paths per grid point, identified a well-defined interior minimum at $\epsilon^{*}=10^{-4}$ and $\lambda^{*}=100$, indicating that the jump hyperparameters are identifiable and that the objective landscape is not degenerate (Figure~\ref{fig:parameter_sweep}, Online Appendix~S2). At these optimal values, the ACF of $|G_t|$ closely tracks the observed SPY ACF across lags 1--252, confirming that the jump mechanism reproduces the empirical ARCH effect.

The primary analysis uses $N=100$ states, but a sensitivity study over $N\in\{30,60,90,100,150,200\}$ shows that the framework is robust to the choice of state resolution across a wide range (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity_results}, Online Appendix~S3). KS and AD pass rates remain stable for both HMM-NJ ($\geq$98.9\% KS, $\geq$97.9\% AD) and HMM-WJ ($\geq$96.0\% KS, $\geq$88.6\% AD), while ACF-MAE for HMM-WJ improves slightly from 0.057 at $N=30$ to 0.049 at $N=200$. State-occupancy diagnostics confirm that every state at $N\leq200$ contains at least 7 observations, ensuring adequate statistical support per state under the Laplace equal-probability partition. At $N=350$, however, certain states are never visited in the historical record, leaving zero transition probabilities that break the ergodic structure and establishing a practical upper bound on state resolution.


\subsection{In-sample distributional and temporal quality}
Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} compares 1,000 simulated paths of 2,766 trading days from GARCH(1,1), HMM-NJ, and HMM-WJ using two-sample KS and AD tests at $\alpha=0.05$. GARCH(1,1) achieves IS KS and AD pass rates of only 4.6\% (SE~0.7) and 1.1\% (SE~0.3), indicating that the GARCH marginal distribution is systematically inconsistent with the observed data despite its well-known volatility clustering properties. HMM-NJ achieves 99.4\% (SE~0.2) and 98.5\% (SE~0.4), and HMM-WJ achieves 97.1\% (SE~0.5) and 90.4\% (SE~0.9), confirming that the Laplace-mixture emission structure adequately captures the observed heavy-tailed distribution.

The temporal quality of the three models diverges sharply. GARCH achieves the lowest IS ACF-MAE (0.031) because its variance equation is explicitly designed to match autocorrelation structure, yet this advantage comes at the cost of distributional fidelity, a tradeoff that the quality metrics expose directly. HMM-NJ produces an ACF of $|G_t|$ that decays rapidly toward zero rather than exhibiting the slow empirical decay, confirming that the standard transition matrix alone is structurally incapable of generating persistent volatility clustering (Figure~\ref{fig:model_comparison}, panel~b). HMM-WJ, with an IS ACF-MAE of 0.052, generates a mixture of two path families: approximately 76\% contain no Poisson jump and behave identically to HMM-NJ, while the remaining 24\% contain at least one jump event and sustain substantial $\text{ACF}(|G_t|)$ decay across all lags. The jump-duration mechanism is therefore the structural feature responsible for reproducing the ARCH effect.

\subsection{Out-of-sample evaluation}
Out-of-sample testing over 249 trading days (January~2 through December~31, 2025) reveals that the HMM variants generalise robustly while GARCH degrades substantially (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). GARCH achieves OoS KS and AD pass rates of 79.0\% (SE~1.3) and 71.2\% (SE~1.4); HMM-NJ achieves 96.7\% (SE~0.6) and 96.3\% (SE~0.6); HMM-WJ achieves 95.0\% (SE~0.7) and 95.4\% (SE~0.7). The simulated kurtosis for GARCH collapses to 1.7 against an observed value of 6.9, while the HMM variants maintain simulated kurtosis near 5.0--5.1, indicating that GARCH fails to preserve tail behaviour outside the training window.

Nonetheless, the out-of-sample period exposes the stationarity assumption embedded in the IS-calibrated jump parameters. The distribution of KS $p$-values shifts leftward from IS to OoS, reflecting the expected distributional degradation when stationary parameters are applied to a period with elevated macro uncertainty (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panels~a--b). The observed ACF of $|G_t|$ in the 2025 window exceeds the HMM-WJ simulation band at medium-to-long lags, indicating that $(\epsilon^{*},\lambda^{*})$ underestimate the frequency and persistence of volatility episodes in this period and that the jump parameters are regime-dependent (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panel~d). Despite this ACF gap, the observed marginal density remains within the simulation envelope, confirming adequate distributional coverage even under regime shift (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panel~c).

\subsection{Multi-asset extension}
Propagating HMM-WJ SPY paths through a Single-Index Model for 424 S\&P~500 constituents, with $\hat{G}_{i,t}=\hat{\alpha}_i+\hat{\beta}_i G_{{\rm SPY},t}+\hat{\eta}_{i,t}$ estimated by OLS on the training window and idiosyncratic shocks drawn by resampling empirical residuals, yields a median IS KS pass rate of 66.7\% and a mean of 58.4\% across the universe (Figure~\ref{fig:multi_asset}, panel~b; Table~\ref{tab:multi_asset}). These rates are lower than the 97.1\% achieved for SPY alone and reflect the well-known limitation that a single-factor linear decomposition with symmetric residuals cannot fully capture asset-specific tail behaviour, skewness, or sector-level dynamics. The SIM $R^2_i$ varies widely across GICS sectors, from near-zero for defensive and low-correlation names to above 0.8 for broad-market ETFs (Figure~\ref{fig:multi_asset}, panel~a), and the degradation in KS pass rate concentrates among assets with low $R^2_i$. High-$\beta$ assets closely tracking SPY retain pass rates above 80\%, suggesting that the HMM-WJ generative engine is not the bottleneck; the limiting factor is the expressiveness of the factor model (Figure~\ref{fig:multi_asset}, panel~c).

Out-of-sample KS pass rates across 417 surviving assets reach a mean of 82.1\% and a median of 91.8\%, confirming that the SIM extension generalises to the test window (Table~\ref{tab:multi_asset}). The correlation-distance distribution between simulated and historical paths yields a mean distance of $1-\rho\approx1.0$, confirming that the framework produces temporally independent realisations rather than tracking specific historical events, consistent with its role as a scenario generation tool rather than a forecasting instrument (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}).
