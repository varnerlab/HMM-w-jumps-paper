
We simulated 1,000 paths of 2,766 trading days using VWAP price data for both the standard no-jumps model and the hybrid model with jumps. Applying the multi-objective grid search detailed in the Methods, the optimal jump hyperparameters were identified as $\epsilon^* = 10^{-4}$ and $\lambda^* = 100$. This configuration successfully reproduced the heavy-tailed kurtosis and ARCH effect observed in SPY (Figure~\ref{fig:model_comparison}). A state resolution of $N=100$ was selected to balance distributional fidelity and statistical robustness. Increasing $N$ improves regime granularity but is bounded above by data sparsity: at $N=350$, certain states are never visited in the historical record, leaving zero transition probabilities that break the ergodic structure of the chain. Conversely, at $N=10$ the model is too coarse to capture the heavy tails of the empirical distribution.

We performed Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) and Anderson-Darling (AD) tests to characterize the statistical consistency of simulated excess growth rate distributions against the historical data \cite{kolmogorov_1933, smirnov_1948, anderson_darling_1952}. The KS test measures the maximum deviation between empirical CDFs, while the AD test places greater weight on the distributional tails and is therefore a more sensitive diagnostic for the fat-tail properties of interest here.

In-sample results (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}) reveal a sharp contrast between model families. GARCH(1,1) achieved KS and AD pass rates of only 6.0\% and 1.4\%, respectively, indicating that its simulated marginal distribution is inconsistent with the observed data despite its well-known volatility clustering properties. HMM-NJ achieved KS and AD pass rates of 100.0\% and 97.0\%, and HMM-WJ achieved 96.4\% and 89.8\%. The ACF-MAE over lags 1--252 was 0.030 for GARCH, 0.059 for HMM-NJ, and 0.052 for HMM-WJ. The lower ACF-MAE for GARCH reflects that the GARCH variance equation is explicitly designed to match autocorrelation structure, yet this comes at the cost of distributional fidelity. The standard no-jumps model passed distributional tests but failed to reproduce volatility clustering, with the ACF of absolute growth rates decaying rapidly toward zero rather than exhibiting the empirically observed slow decay (Figure~\ref{fig:model_comparison}, panel~b). The hybrid model reproduced both the marginal distribution and the ARCH effect simultaneously.

Out-of-sample results over 249 trading days (January~2--December~31, 2025) show that all three models generalise, though with different patterns. GARCH achieved KS and AD pass rates of 78.1\% and 73.1\%. HMM-NJ achieved 96.6\% and 96.7\%, and HMM-WJ achieved 94.6\% and 95.2\% (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). The simulated kurtosis for GARCH collapsed to 1.6 out-of-sample against an observed value of 6.9, confirming that GARCH does not preserve tail behaviour outside the training window. The HMM variants maintained simulated kurtosis near 5.0--5.1, a closer match to the observed 6.9. Volatility clustering persisted in the HMM-WJ out-of-sample paths, with the Poisson jump mechanism sustaining tail-state dwell times that the standard transition matrix cannot reproduce. However, the observed ACF of $|G_t|$ in the 2025 test window exceeded the model simulation band at medium-to-long lags (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panel~d), indicating that the IS-calibrated jump hyperparameters $(\epsilon^*,\lambda^*)$ underestimate the frequency and persistence of volatility episodes in this period. This ACF gap, together with the leftward shift of the KS $p$-value distribution from in-sample to out-of-sample (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panels~a--b), provides evidence that the jump parameters are regime-dependent: the stationary $(\epsilon^*,\lambda^*)$ calibrated on 2014--2024 do not fully capture the elevated macro uncertainty of 2025. Despite this limitation, the model maintained KS and AD pass rates above 94\%, and the observed marginal density remained within the simulation envelope (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}, panel~c), confirming adequate distributional coverage even under regime shift. These results validate the hybrid framework as a generative tool for producing synthetic time series that remain statistically consistent with the underlying market dynamics, while highlighting the need for adaptive recalibration of jump parameters in non-stationary environments \cite{cont_empirical_2001, ryden_stylized_1998, bulla_stylized_2006}.

Propagating HMM-WJ SPY paths through a Single-Index Model (SIM) for 424 S\&P~500 constituents produced asset-level distributional matches that varied systematically with factor exposure (Figure~\ref{fig:multi_asset}) \cite{sharpe_simplified_1963}. For each asset~$i$, we decomposed the excess growth rate as $\hat{G}_{i,t} = \hat{\alpha}_i + \hat{\beta}_i G_{{\rm SPY},t} + \hat{\eta}_{i,t}$, where $(\hat{\alpha}_i, \hat{\beta}_i)$ were estimated by ordinary least squares on the 2014--2024 training window and the idiosyncratic shocks $\hat{\eta}_{i,t}$ were drawn by resampling the empirical residuals. Panel~(a) showed that the SIM $R^2_i$ varied widely across GICS sectors, from near-zero for defensive and low-correlation names to above 0.8 for broad-market ETFs, confirming that the single-factor structure captured only the systematic component of each asset's dynamics. Panel~(b) reported the distribution of in-sample KS pass rates across the full asset universe: the median pass rate was 66.7\% and the mean was 58.4\%. These rates were lower than the 96.4\% achieved for SPY alone, reflecting the well-known limitation that the SIM's single-factor linearity and symmetric residual distribution could not fully capture asset-specific tail behaviour, skewness, or sector-level dynamics \cite{fama_french_1993}. The degradation in KS pass rate was concentrated among assets with low $R^2_i$---those dominated by idiosyncratic risk that the single factor could not reach---while high-$\beta$ assets closely tracking SPY retained pass rates above 80\%. Panel~(c) illustrated this pattern for three representative assets spanning a wide range of systematic risk exposure: NVDA ($\hat{\beta}=1.75$), JNJ ($\hat{\beta}=0.54$), and QQQ ($\hat{\beta}=1.14$). These results demonstrated that the HMM-WJ generative engine was not the bottleneck; the limiting factor was the expressiveness of the factor model used to translate the market-level simulation into individual asset paths.

The correlation-distance distribution for the out-of-sample simulated paths was centred at a mean distance of $1-\rho \approx 1.0$, confirming that the generative framework produces temporally independent realisations that do not attempt to predict specific historical events \cite{fama_efficient_1970, assefa_generating_2021, kwon_can_2024}. This distinguishes the framework from a forecasting tool and positions it appropriately as an instrument for stress testing and scenario generation (Figure~\ref{fig:statistical_validation}).
