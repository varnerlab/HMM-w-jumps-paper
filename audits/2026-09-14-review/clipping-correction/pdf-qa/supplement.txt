S2. Rebalancing under independent residuals

    To determine what a portfolio-level use of these paths inherits from the
independent-residual construction, this appendix derives the diversification return
of a rebalanced portfolio under Eq. (4). The variance correction in Eq. (8)
sets each synthetic asset’s second moment to its generator target. When that
target matches the empirical second moment, the second-order formula for the
diversification return is matched at the asset level as well. What the construction
does not match is the cross-asset residual covariance, and the derivation below
isolates where that omission enters.

S3. Validation metric definitions

    To distinguish marginal agreement from temporal agreement, we used sepa-
rate distributional, tail, and autocorrelation summaries (Tables 2 and 6). For
one observed series x = (x1 , . . . , xn ) and R synthetic paths y (r) of the same
length, each distributional comparison used one complete synthetic path and
the fixed observed history. The two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic
is the largest absolute difference between their empirical cumulative distribution
functions, D = supz |Fx (z) − Fy(r) (z)| (Kolmogorov, 1933; Smirnov, 1948). The
Anderson–Darling (AD) comparison gives greater weight to differences near the
tails (Anderson and Darling, 1952). We used the approximate two-sample KS test
and the modified, tie-aware two-sample AD calculation in HypothesisTests.jl,
with asymptotic p-values. A path passed when p > 0.05, and the pass percentage
was 100 times the fraction of passing paths. Dependence within returns, shared
observed data, and parameter fitting prevent these percentages from being inter-
preted as calibrated significance levels or evidence of model equivalence. They
describe agreement with the supplied history.

Wasserstein-1 and histogram Hellinger scores for each path (Table 2). Let x(j)

    The kurtosis contribution pulls in the opposite direction. Treating a jump

tail exponent is set by ν, not by the jump content. The objective of Eq. (3)
balances these two effects.

where they are not. Within a single-episode jump path, the ACF component of

a path is controlled by λ. The parameter ϵ moves the objective only through
the incidence of jump paths, which the jump-active conditioning removes, and
through the small fraction of multi-episode paths. This weak dependence is
consistent with the tuned ϵ = 10−4 lying at the lower edge of the explored range

S9.1. Role of the centered full-return draw as the residual
    The hybrid composition places εi = si g̃ic , a centered and rescaled draw of
asset i’s full return, in the residual slot of Eq. (4). On its face this is a category
mismatch: the residual of a single-index decomposition is the idiosyncratic
component of the return, orthogonal to the market, while g̃ic is distributed like
the centered total return, market-driven component included. This appendix
states what the substitution preserves exactly, what it preserves approximately,
and how the approximation degrades as the market loading grows.
    The residual slot must supply three things: a zero mean, so that the intercept
                                                                     2
and market term alone set the conditional mean; the variance σgen,i       − βi2 σm
                                                                                 2
                                                                                   not
already contributed by the market term; and the asset-specific distributional
shape, the heavy tails and volatility clustering that motivated the single-asset
generator. Centering gives the residual path an exactly zero sample mean
(Eq. (6)). On the unclipped non-tracker branch, using the market path variance
in Eq. (8) gives the residual exactly the remaining variance. The composed
variance also contains the sample cross-covariance term, which need not vanish
on a finite path. Under the independence and large-T assumptions of Section S9,
the recovered loadings and intercept converge to their targets. Using the frozen
training market variance, as in the six-method holdout evaluation, adds a further

models used separate random-number streams, so matching replication identifiers

(Table S12). The Monte Carlo standard

S12. Pooled one-day tail thresholds

    To estimate each generator’s one-day tail without relying on the few extreme
observations in a single 249-day path, we pooled returns only after constructing
separate paths (Supplementary Table S15). For every frozen training asset and
composition method, we generated B = 5,000 paths of length 249 and calculated
linearly interpolated empirical quantiles from all 249B daily log returns. A
market path was shared across assets and methods within each replication. Each
asset draw retained its own centering and variance correction, with the observed
training market variance used in the hybrid rule. The hidden Markov models
started from their fitted stationary state distributions, jumps remained disabled,
and the generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH)
simulator retained its 100-step warmup. We calibrated thresholds for all 423
training assets, including 393 with accepted GARCH fits, before reading the
holdout histories. The resulting quantile estimated the return distribution of a
uniformly selected day within this finite-horizon construction. It did not estimate
a horizon-independent path law or restore cumulative residual uncertainty.
    To distinguish numerical stability from holdout performance, we fixed pool
sizes of 100, 500, 1,000, 2,500, and 5,000 paths before scoring the observed
returns (Supplementary Table S15). An independent ensemble of 1,000 paths
per asset and method checked the simulated probability of falling below each
threshold. Disjoint random streams separated calibration and validation, while
preserving the shared market within each ensemble. Doubling the pool from 2,500
to 5,000 changed the median asset’s 99% threshold by 0.12–0.30%, depending
on method. The corresponding 95th percentiles of absolute relative change were
0.37–1.03%. Individual thresholds were less stable than aggregate rates: the
largest 99% change was 3.95%, for a GARCH asset. Every method’s mean
observed exceedance rate changed by less than 0.006 percentage points at both
coverage levels. Mean exceedance rates on independent simulated paths were
5.03–5.04% and 1.01–1.02%, close to the respective 5% and 1% targets. These
checks assessed Monte Carlo stability under the fitted generators; they did not
establish coverage on real returns. The reported cross-asset standard deviations
were not standard errors, and no coverage significance test treated shared market
histories or dependent days as independent observations.
